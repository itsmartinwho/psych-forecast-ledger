Part One: What the Rules About Insurance Authorizations Actually Say

Muir : Why does an insurance company have to actually cover anything at all? What are the rules they have to follow?

Feldman : Let’s start with the language of the insurance policy, which after all is a legal contract between the insurer and the individual. At the core of the contract, it says that we will pay for care that is medically necessary. They’ll put some limitations around that, some of which are completely reasonable. It has to be related to healthcare, and it has to be based on some diagnosis that a professional has made that says this is appropriate care. And that’s in the contract.

Muir : Well, I suppose they’re gonna need some sort of standards, Joe.

Feldman : Those standards are developed by practitioners and they’re reviewed by their peer practitioners. And those standards are published by organizations like the American Psychiatric Association, that describe the body of work that guides clinicians as to what care should be provided.

Muir : And what happens when the insurance company uses its own standards instead?

Feldman : The AMA made a statement in regard to some funny word play where one of these proprietary plans issued a statement that said, “No, no, no, our standards are based on the peer-developed standards.” And the AMA said: don’t be fooled. Based on is not the same as the actual article . The other related issue is that insurers may not disclose what those standards are and how they were applied. It’s holding their cards very closely and saying, “We’ve looked at our standards and this care is not medically necessary, but we’re not gonna tell you why.”

Part Two: The Call

I placed a peer-to-peer review call to [redacted subsidiary], a wholly owned subsidiary of [the same redacted company], seeking prior authorization for transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) for a patient with severe, primary obsessive-compulsive disorder. The following is drawn from that call experience. The call was an experience in much the same way that getting a nail in your tire is an experience. It forces you to slow down, reevaluate, and consider whether you need to learn how to change tires. The phone call and question happened before my conversation with Joe from Cover My Mental Mealth, so please understand it in that context…and now…the call:

Reviewer : Hi, I was calling for a peer-to-peer from [redacted subsidiary].

Muir : Yes. For a TMS request.

Reviewer : Yes. Hold on. Morning. Well, thank you for taking my call.

Muir : Of course. If you can just start by telling me your name, spelling it, and your training.

Reviewer : So I am… the company’s using initials at this point for security reasons.

She did not provide additional information that could be used to verify her identity or medical license.

Muir : The patient has severe and primary obsessive-compulsive disorder. This is an FDA labeled treatment for obsessive-compulsive disorder, and the treatment is being done with the BrainsWay H7 coil, which has multiple clinical trials that demonstrate its efficacy in this population, including large-scale trials that demonstrate its durability, which I was a co-author on. I’m happy to get into that data, but I understand it is not currently part of the coverage criteria for [redacted company], which is perplexing given the strength of the data supporting it.

Reviewer : Yeah. And that was the reason for the peer-to-peer. It’s not covered at this point.

Muir : As the peer-to-peer reviewer, have you ever treated a patient with transcranial magnetic stimulation?

Reviewer : So the “I” in the peer-to-peers… I’m reviewing based on the guidelines that the company uses.

Muir : I am aware of that.

We talk over each other a little bit here, and then I just find out if I can ask a question.

Reviewer : Ask me anything you like.

Muir : Have you ever treated a patient with transcranial magnetic stimulation?

Reviewer : I am familiar with TMS. I’m not treating with TMS at this point.

Muir : I’m asking you about your clinical experience. Have you ever treated a patient with transcranial magnetic stimulation?

Reviewer : So we’re talking about the policy guidelines.

Muir : No, we’re not. We’re talking about a patient . This is a peer-to-peer. You are a physician. I am a physician. Have you ever treated a patient with transcranial magnetic stimulation? It’s a yes or no question.

Reviewer : I answered the question. I am not treating patients with TMS at this point.

Reviewer : We use the guidelines that are based on evidence that we have available.

Reviewer : I’m using the [redacted subsidiary] clinical guidelines, which have a list of all of the references that have been reviewed.

The call continued, tragically… nothing was resolved, and the patient got DBS brain surgery approved instead of this outpatient procedure. I'm not even kidding. I participated in that peer review also.

Part Three: What Should Have Happened

Muir : Joe, what is supposed to happen on one of these calls?

Feldman : The first order of business in a peer-to-peer review is a discussion about the patient’s care. It’s two peers talking to one another about this particular patient’s presentation, about treatment that has been tried before, and what leads the treating clinician to determine that X, Y, Z course of action is appropriate. Once that’s discussed you have at least a foundation to have a different conversation, which is: is this care that should be covered under this member’s insurance policy? So if you jump right to, “Wait a second, this care isn’t even covered,” you’re out of order.

Muir : And what about the qualifications of the reviewer?

Feldman : Both NCQA and URAC specify that in a peer-to-peer review, the competence of the clinician must be the same as the clinician who they’re speaking with. The essence of it is they have to have been in the same shoes as the treating clinician. It is reasonable to expect unambiguous confirmation of the training and the specific experience in a clinical scenario like the one that is being described. That is not negotiable.

Muir : And if they’re not qualified?

Feldman : There’s no shame in them saying, “You know what, I’m not the right person to have this conversation.” That’s perfectly legitimate. And their job should then morph to: I want to find someone who can help represent the interest of the insurance company, with someone who can say, “You know what, let’s have a conversation now about this treatment-resistant OCD and whether the care plan that you’ve identified is appropriate or not.” That’s a reasonable path forward.

Part Four: What You Can Do To Advocate For Your Patients

Muir : So let’s say this has happened to someone reading. What do they do?

Feldman : The first order of business, of course, is getting care to the patient on a timely basis. One of the observations that we make on Cover My Mental Health is to consider filing what’s called a formal insurer complaint. A complaint that goes directly to the insurance company that there is some issue with regard to the care delivery, where there’s a breakdown. Our investigation of this particular course of action suggests that you will get faster attention within the insurance company, because these formal complaints directly to an insurance company require reporting to their regulator and to their accreditation body. And that’s something that is a point of leverage.

Muir : does it go beyond the insurance company itself?

Feldman : There are three different organizations in government that could be appropriate for filing a formal complaint. A state attorney general is certainly one. Another would be the state insurance regulator. You can find a link to your state’s insurance regulator at the NAIC website. You can also file a complaint with the federal regulator responsible for this: the Employee Benefit Security Administration, EBSA . A complaint can be filed either online or by phone.

Muir : So if you really wanted to make sure the insurance company was on notice…

Feldman : You would file a complaint with the insurance company itself, so they’re aware. And with EBSA at the federal level. And with the state attorney general, in case it’s something they want to look into for their own reasons. And with the state-level insurance regulator, because insurance is regulated both on the federal and on the state level.

Muir : What’s the most important of those?

Feldman : I think the most important, and the most likely to have effect fastest, is a formal complaint to the insurance company. That would follow, for example, a medical necessity letter, or some documentation that a peer-to-peer review call was not being provided. Some evidence of that breakdown. Then having filed a formal complaint to the insurance company, that particular step can be included in a complaint that’s filed to a state regulator or a state attorney general’s office.

Muir : And a medical necessity letter, say more about that.

Feldman : A medical necessity letter from the clinician is, we think, one of the most important tools that a clinician can use to advocate for their patients. That can get the job done. It needn’t be a big battle and a campaign of many, many steps all taken in rapid-fire sequence. A medical necessity letter put on file may be sufficient. If that doesn’t get the job done, it could lead to a prompt to file a formal complaint. It could lead to a request for a peer-to-peer review. It could also lead to a formal appeal, which is in a typical insurance policy the formal dispute resolution approach that is provided.

Muir : And regulators, do our complaints actually reach them in a way that matters?

Feldman : I had a conversation with a state insurance regulator at a conference. He was from Mississippi. And I was talking to him about complaints coming in from clinicians, and I said, so how many of these complaints would you need to hear before you decided [that] we should probably investigate this? Would you have to have, what, twenty-five or fifty or something like that? And he said: if we had even a handful, we assume that there’s more out there that we will never hear about, and we’ll get after it. So assume that your one act of filing a complaint is going to make a difference.

Muir : The argument that I often hear is that this is all just too complicated, that they’re going to wait us out.

Feldman : The reason we buy health insurance is because we want to take care of ourselves and our families. And it’s just a reasonable expectation that that means the insurance company is gonna pay for the care that we need, based on the decisions of the clinicians who they have selected to be in their network to take care of us. That’s all we’re talking about. Clinicians and patients and families do not have to take no for an answer.

Today, over at Healio, we wrote about new data on ketamine! A quick taste!

A recent study published in Molecular Psychiatry provides important insight into this question. Using advanced positron emission tomography imaging, researchers from Yokohama City University were able to directly visualize activity at AMPA receptors (AMPARs) in the living human brain, a key component of glutamate signaling.

Glutamate is the brain’s primary excitatory neurotransmitter and plays a central role in synaptic plasticity, which is the ability of neural connections to strengthen, weaken and reorganize. Increasingly, researchers believe depression may involve disruptions in these plasticity mechanisms rather than simply “imbalances in chemicals.”

…

In the study, investigators used a specialized PET tracer called [¹¹C]K-2, which binds to AMPARs, allowing scientists to observe receptor dynamics in the living brain. Across three separate clinical trials, researchers examined 34 patients with TRD and 49 healthy controls.

Their findings were striking. Patients with TRD demonstrated altered AMPAR patterns in brain regions associated with mood regulation and reward processing. After ketamine treatment, these receptor patterns shifted in ways that appeared to normalize neural signaling. Most notably, the magnitude of these changes correlated with improvements in depressive symptoms.

My youngest interviewee is my own daughter. I have twin children. I have a boy, Trent, and a girl named Quinn. Trent is pretty popular, and spends a lot of time crushing it with his friends on Roblox and Minecraft. Quinn is more entrepreneurial, more athletic, and also has dyslexia. I'm trying to convince Trent to join us on the newsletter also, but he's got a lot going on. Quinn, on the other hand, has already tested the major AI models, identified her target market, and “mapped out” two startups. She also has a few notes on my parenting. Quinn has opinions. She shares them regularly.

This interview was conducted two days ago, however, today is the twins birthdays. She and Trent turn 10 today.

Subscribe now

This article is in honor of that exciting celebration. Dear readers, I am trying to get Trent to chime in also. It is likely to take your urging as well. Feel free to add comments to this article requesting to hear from Trent.

Quinn is the kind of child who challenges my assumptions. She says the quiet part out loud. I have no idea where she gets that from. It must be her mother.

We’d talked before about entrepreneurship. This time, I wanted to go deeper: What does she actually want to build? Who’s the customer? How does she think about money? The answers were more sophisticated than I expected, and occasionally humbling.

What follows is a lightly edited transcript of our conversation.

Let’s start where we left off. What is it about entrepreneurship that appeals to you?

I like doing business-related things. I want to design my own company. I’ve wanted to do it because I like technology a lot.

How did you get into technology?

I learn a lot from Hard Fork, the New York Times podcast. I’ve tested out all the AI models they talk about. ChatGPT, I don’t think it was very good, based on what I tried. Gemini was mid. It wasn’t bad, but it wasn’t good either. And then Claude? That’s the one I liked the most, because it actually listens. When I asked it things, it was always right about what I asked.

And those experiences inspired you to want to build something yourself?

Yeah. I want to help people who are falling for scams. That’s one thing. But I also want to start a company about ADHD and dyslexia, because I have both of those. People feel more comforted when someone else has what they have. And a lot of famous singers have ADHD and dyslexia, so people look up to them. It helps you not judge yourself for it.

Tell me more about that company. What does it actually do?

I might write articles — like, how having dyslexia and ADHD means you have differences, but you’re always great. Motivational essays. And content for parents too, so they understand. And I also want to build an AI chatbot, like what ChatGPT did, but designed specifically for kids who have ADHD and dyslexia.

Who is the customer?

People who want to learn about it. Parents. And kids, there would be a kid version.

What’s the main problem you’re solving?

The internet has a safer option for kids already, but it blocks things that kids actually need. I tried looking up a place, just a location, and it said it couldn’t be reached. I want to make something that’s actually safe, but doesn’t treat kids like they can’t handle information. It should teach boundaries, not just block everything.

That’s a real product gap. But here’s a hard question: your own parents let you on the regular internet. Do you think that was a mistake?

No. Because I wouldn’t have ever learned about AI. I would have been behind. Navigating the internet is a skill everyone should learn. It shouldn’t be taught that you shouldn’t use it. It should be taught that you have boundaries, but you stay in the safe parts, and you don’t go into the deep dark places.

So should that decision be left to parents?

Yes, the parents’ choice. But maybe wait until a little older than the kid’s actual age, especially for AI. Because they might not be ready. I would highly recommend it, but maybe a little older.

Fair. Now, if a parent is considering your product, why should they trust your company?

It would be free for everyone to try. No matter what.

How do you make money if it’s free?

…Sponsors?

Advertising could actually make the product more dangerous — advertisers become your real customer, and then you’re making decisions for them, not for the kids.

Oh. Seriously?

Seriously.

Okay. I haven’t thought that far. …Maybe a free trial, and then it’s not extremely expensive after that. You want to keep it accessible.

You’d make it up on volume. Which company does that remind you of?

Walmart. They sell a lot of things at a lower price, so they make more in total. Also, something I heard a lot of chefs who work at Michelin-star restaurants, their first job was at McDonald’s. Because you learn teamwork. That’s really important for companies.

Last question. What’s the most important thing a parent needs to know about why your company is the one they should trust?

That it would ensure safety. And that I’m building it because I have ADHD and dyslexia. People feel more comfortable when someone who has what they have is the one helping them. I know what it feels like. That’s the story.

Quinn is nine years old and has a lot of ideas. She is currently accepting no funding, but is an advisor (via her mom’s shares) in Ginko , a company that helps kids and families regulate their technology use.

A note from the newsletter author: What strikes me most about this conversation isn’t Quinn’s business instincts. Those are real. It’s her instinct that lived experience is a form of credibility. “I have this too” is not a liability to disclose— it is a competitive advantage to lead with. In psychiatry, we spend years trying to teach that to clinicians. She arrived there on her own. She also arrived there having learned about AI from Hard Fork, which is either an endorsement of Kevin Roose and Casey Newton’s work or a sign that we should all be rethinking who we assume is in the audience. Or, more likely, both.

If this writing resonated with you, forward it to someone who works with kids, patients, or anyone thinking about how kids of her generation relate to technology.

This Sunday, I spent time with my kids. I also had my mother, Vita, my mother-in-law, my father-in-law, and the rest of the universe swirling around. My children just turned 10. They are twins. This year, instead of a birthday party with their other friends, they decided to spend time with their parents. I am very aware this is not going to be the kind of birthday they want much longer. It was a huge win, at least for me.

I’m a big fan of my children. My readers can already tell. I want to write a little bit about Trent today, because you hear about Quinn often.

Advertising Moment: Inessential Pharmacology

I have a new IN-PRINT book available for purchase. Inessential Pharmacology is available from Amazon (affiliate link), in both hardback and paperback .

A Kindle ebook and an unsettling AI audio version are also available.

Trent is as humble as Quinn is… not. Trent knows he’s capable. He doesn’t have to worry about it. He doesn’t have to overcompensate for the kind of dyslexia my daughter has. Or the kind of dyslexia I have. Or my myriad other impairments. There’s an important role for overcompensation in the tapestry of humanity. A lot of us build lives around proving early characters, with ill-conceived ideas of their own limitations, dead wrong. That’s one way to succeed in life. But it’s not the only one.

Trent and Dad in a cockpit at the Intrepid Museum.

Trent, who reminds me very much of my father, has a quiet, maybe even cool, confidence. He’s had the experience, over and over again, of being able to handle stuff. Don’t get me wrong, he’s 10 years old. He can’t use a knife safely on his own. I’ll encourage him to make things for dinner, and he’ll look at me, a little side eye, and say, “Come on, Dad, I’m not even 10! ” This excuse is going to run out quickly. However, when we were traveling in Japan last year, right before their ninth birthday, he was the one who helped us get around on the train.

We had a challenging situation to deal with this weekend, particularly for Carlene. She got an email from someone that was pretty upsetting. She wanted an alternate perspective and handed the phone to Trent. He read it, and the look on his face said everything.

“This person seems really unhappy.” He then handed the phone back to his mother, knowingly .

He said it with the kind of reserved confidence that will do well in senior management. Being a leader in large organizations is about sometimes using words like “ disappointed ” with knife-like precision. More often, it’s about being enthusiastic when people do the right thing. Withholding praise when they don’t is more powerful than harsh words. Trent, at 10, seems to have gotten the memo.

He’s a kid who generally knows what to do. He’s also popular among other kids, which is very strange for me to think about, because I wasn’t. Don’t get me wrong, I had friends, and I still think I have wonderful friends. But Trent doesn’t really struggle with friendship. He appreciates who people are, and they like him for who he is. He doesn’t have to make up for anything. I think this is a pretty good starting place if you’re considering humility as a “good-fit” personality feature.

A lot of people think well of me; others, less so. But I doubt either my fans or my critics would disagree that humility is not my strong suit . I’ve had too much to make up for in my life to routinely downplay what I’m capable of. I have had to put on a good show.

But if you didn’t have to put on a good show? That’s the birthplace of humility, if you’re lucky enough to be both capable and know it from an early age.

I’ve begun to understand ambition, which I have in abundance, and to some degree grandiosity, which is also a fair personality feature to notice about me, as something that starts early. My son has neither. My daughter has both. They are both charming, interpersonally gifted, gracious, and kind. But Quinn has something to make up for. Having ADHD and dyslexia means she’s had to work harder than he has. Maybe ambition, grandiosity, and the desire to prove it are reactions to those early adversities for most people?

I called my mother this weekend to think some of this through out loud. She raised two dyslexic children, one more severely than the other. She’d just spent a week with my kids. I figured she’d have something to say.

The twins are on a trampoline.

Owen : I just spent a wonderful weekend with Trent and Quinn, who have turned 10. You’ve noticed, I would guess, that they are very different children. One of the ways they differ is in a way that mirrors my sister and me. Trent doesn’t seem to have the ADHD and dyslexia that I did, and Quinn very much does. Do you think early adversity and having a different brain lead to overcompensation? Is the need to prove it a predictable outcome of early adversity?

Vita : It isn’t that simple. You’ve arrived at a foregone conclusion. There are just so many other factors.

Owen : That’s why I’m calling you. I thought it was too simple.

Vita : I can speak to your situation and your sister’s more closely than I can to your children, because I haven’t spent as much time with them. Part of what existed with the two of you was that your father was gone all the time. He was on the road six days a week. He’d come home on a Friday night and leave on a Sunday night or Monday morning, and that was it. So the two of you jockeyed for attention from the only person who could provide it, which was me. That set up a contest between you from the start. It was “Mommy, look at me” from both of you, and I couldn’t look at each of you one hundred percent of the time. That set up a rivalry.

Owen : I don’t remember it that way, but then again, I wasn’t looking at it from the vantage point of a parent.

Vita : I’ll never forget the recording you made. We had a little tape machine, and you wanted to do a show. Your sister was two, and you were four. You did not let her get a word in edgewise. You wanted to be a radio producer. Your sister’s language was not as facile as yours, and she was two years younger. She would start to say, “My name is Lindsey,” and you’d cut her off. That’s how she handled it. You never lifted a hand to her. But she had to fight back any way she could. You, despite being on paper the brighter of the two, had a sister with one of the most creative and amazing marketing intelligences I’ve ever seen.

Owen : When I look at my kids, I see something similar. Trent is a lot more like Lindsay and like my Dad. And I think it’s fair to say Lindsey is quite a bit more humble than I am.

Vita : Humility has never been your strong suit.

Owen : I’ve been musing on humility as I think about Trent. Is it a personality trait, or is it the product of having had an easier time with something early on?

Vita : Both, probably. But if you’re comfortable with who you are, humility is a natural state of being. My guess is that Trent is much more at home with who he is than Quinn is, and she’s always testing it, displaying it, proving it to herself and everybody else. Humility isn’t going to help you with that.

Owen : My hunch is that kids who are blessed with being comfortable with who they are don’t need to prove anything to anyone, at least in childhood. That makes it way easier to find your way to humility. Vita : That was in play with you. Having the disabilities you had made you quite insecure about your own intelligence. When you applied to schools, you refused to be prepped for any of the tests. You wanted to see where you were in your native state. You got your SAT scores back. They were high, and they were acceptable for most schools. The assistant principal came in while I was doing it, and when I finished, he said it was worthy of a college freshman. He walked out of the room, and my teacher said to me, “Why don’t you talk like a little girl like Bonnie next to you?” The accusation was the same: somebody else wrote it, it couldn’t possibly be you. It was not easy to be the advanced student, and the resistance came less from classmates than from teachers.

Owen : Is there something about precocious children that makes people uncomfortable?

Vita : When they’re little, people are amused. You were very verbal at a very early age, and everybody thought you were charming. But when you’re thirteen, the teacher looks at you and thinks: this kid is brighter than I am. It depends on the security of the teacher. There was Salvastrini, your math teacher, who said to me, “This kid is smarter than I am, so I really have to work to keep ahead of him.” It didn’t make him question himself. It made him praise you and try to figure out how best to serve you. But an insecure teacher will try to knock you down so they feel better by comparison.

Owen: Quinn said something to me in the car this weekend. She said she thinks mommy is very smart, and daddy is too. I asked why we were measuring . Trent had mentioned that Dad knows something about everything. They’re studying the American Revolution right now, debating whether they’d be loyalists or patriots. Quinn aligned with the Patriots. Trent saw himself as more akin to the loyalists. He said the British Crown paid for these colonies to exist in the first place, so who are they to complain?

Vita : That’s a perfectly reasonable way to look at it. He’s more of an establishment type. And she’s a revolutionary.

Owen : Humility is an establishment trait. If the world has been kind to you, it’s easy to be humble. I have an admiration for humility because I haven’t had the effortless experience where humility would make sense. I’ve had to prove it. Trent has had positive feedback from friends and teachers. He’s had a charmed early experience where he’s been able to be humble comfortably. Quinn has not. She’s audacious, ambitious, and outgoing in a different way. They’re both great, but they have experienced adversity very differently.

Vita : However, you’ve come through a real growth period in the last six or eight years. The more you’ve become comfortable in your own skin, and you largely are now, the more you exhibit humility. You don’t have to keep telling me how wonderful and brilliant you are. You’ve gotten much more comfortable with yourself. The upshot is that you are now wholly able to skip over all that and get to the work of helping people, which is your goal. Everything is in service to that. If you want people to see and know you, it’s in service of that. Sure, you like it when people praise you. But that’s not the end for you now. The prize is to wipe out mental illness. And it’s changing you. I can see it.

Trent likes giving a thumbs up.

Owen : That is kind of you to say. For parents: strive for children who are either ambitious or humble, but not neither .

The Frontier Psychiatrists is a publication that advocates for a better future. In that future, we'll have brains that don't break down on their owners as often, and when they do, we fix them promptly. When you call a plumber, they don't judge you for having a sink. They don't look askance at your toilet. They look up the model and manufacturer and figure out what part you need to fix it. It's not a guessing game; you don't have to talk about it for a year. There's an established supply chain for plumbing, there's a licensed profession, and it works pretty smoothly.

Radial, Chattanooga.

Brain Medicine has not had the same smooth runway. We unfortunately split it up into psychiatry and separate neurology in the beginning. Then, along with our colleagues in anesthesia, we created the ability to perform neurosurgery, and neuroradiology became a separate specialty as well.

This is not just a range of specialties; it’s a diffusion of responsibility. Plumbers own the problem of getting water around your home. They work with distributors of goods that can fix a plumbing problem. Maybe you need a master plumber, but he’s still a plumber. There’s a regulation on what can be done in plumbing, just as there is with medicine. Brain medicine? It needs to be a lot more like plumbing—a straightforward discipline that combines the expertise necessary to move brain signals from one place to another, such that everything flows appropriately.

This is gonna take a change in public consciousness. We’re gonna stop talking about mental health—Mental health is just a subdomain of Brain Medicine.

The problem of mental health is not different than the problem of movement disorders, when it comes to the brain circuits and output of the brain. Depressed people wring their hands. They fidget. They have psychomotor retardation at other times. These are problems with movement. But we’re not calling it a movement disorder, we’re calling it a mental health problem. They can also be problems with gastric motility, indigestion, and constipation, which is a movement disorder of the gastrointestinal tract. OK, so sometimes it’s a movement disorder of smooth muscle control, and at other times it’s a movement disorder of skeletal muscle motor control. Sometimes we say things that don’t make any sense, despondent, disconnected, even psychotic, but at the end of the day, the brain represents feelings to our consciousness, and our motor control centers fire to create patterns of skeletal muscle activation that allow our tongue, jaw, larynx, and the like to make sounds.

Brain Medicine is our future terminal specialty that brings together the existing disciplines of psychology, psychiatry, neurology, neuroradiology, and neurosurgery, with all working together under one overarching umbrella. Why have an overarching umbrella? Because then you have an understanding of what the problem is, which tools can be brought to bear to solve it, and who to call. If your sink doesn’t work, you call a plumber. If your brain doesn’t work, you call a brain medicine doctor.

That’s the vision. This week, we’ve moved a little bit closer to getting the word out.

As a psychiatrist, I'm a physician. The weird thing about being a psychiatrist, although I'm a sub-specialist, I see my patients more than they see most other doctors. That means public health issues are often the responsibility of psychiatrists. We are essentially providing primary care to all of our patients. This is both "essentially" true and literally true for some patients—the definition of primary care in some value-based care payment models is the medical specialty that generates more than half of the evaluation and management service visits in the year. For patients with severe or chronic mental illnesses, sometimes we see patients as often as every single week. As such, what follows is an update for general medical care, but it's coming from a psychiatrist who knows the burden of medical illnesses for my patients.

Just the other day, I was having a conversation about reactive arthritis. Reactive arthritis used to be known as its eponymous name, Reiter’s Syndrome. This is one of the many clinical trials we learned in medical school, characterized by oligoarthritis, usually have a large joint like a knee or a hip, but not impacting a bunch of joints all at once. At the same time, patients will also suffer from conjunctivitis (and sometimes visual impairment), as well as urethritis— that's inflammation of the urethra. This leads to the tremendously helpful mnemonic:

“can't see, can't pee, can't climb a tree.”

Usually, reactive arthritis is fallout from an infection. The most common is chlamydia, followed by gonorrhea, and then gastrointestinal illness from food-borne pathogens.

Which brings us to gonorrhea. There is gonorrhea news. I know, the thing you're looking for in this newsletter is definitely news. It might not be news about gonorrhea, but it is called the newsletter, so here we are.

There is a problem with gonorrhea.

This picture of flowers is here just to reduce the emotional temperature. © Owen Muir, 2026.

There is a problem beyond just the problem of gonorrhea . Infection with this pathogen, a bacterium often transmitted through sexual contact, has historically been treated with antibiotics.

Gonorrhea and its close companion, chlamydia, are two of the most common sexually transmitted infections. This comes up for mental health professionals because our patients worry about STI's. Some of our patients, like those with manic depressive illness and post-traumatic stress disorder, can put themselves in risky situations as a result of their illness. They can be more prone to these infections:

Patients with bipolar disorder have been found to have a 1.67 times greater risk of developing STIs compared to controls, with particularly elevated risk for HIV (over 3.5 times higher) and syphilis (over 2 times higher). 1

And, in PTSD, the data is similarly concerning.

A nationwide Danish cohort study found that people with a PTSD diagnosis had roughly 1.8 times the rate of any type of infection compared to those without, and specifically identified higher rates of STIs among women with PTSD 2

It's more of a problem for “psych patients.” It's also true that many of our patients with obsessive-compulsive disorder can obsess about sexually transmitted infections. Therefore, when there is a problem with the treatment of a sexually transmitted infection, it's crucial that Psychiatrists and those interested in patients suffering from psychiatric disorders know about the update.

Today, I present the ghastly apparition of super-gonorrhea. OK, in science terms, it's actually non-susceptible gonorrhea. Historically, you'd go to the doctor for sexually transmitted infection testing, and they would use either doxycycline or azithromycin as a treatment for chlamydia, and they often empirically treat, if there are symptoms, with cephalosporin, usually the one-shot injectable ceftriaxone.

That is no longer a good option for everybody, particularly in the City of New York. Public health enthusiasts look at trends in localities to know what is the best treatment for people who happen to live there based on the bacteria that commonly infect people in their locality. Many infectious diseases are hyperlocal. This is why it's important to have a functional Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, and I would encourage the federal government to prioritize a well-funded CDC with happy employees who are treated exceptionally well.

As of today, April 16, 2026, there has been a disturbing increase in cases of non-susceptible to traditional cephalosporin treatment gonorrhea. This requires knowing that this is a problem, and knowing what to do about it. Here is the summary from the New York State Department of Health:

• Cases of non-susceptible gonorrhea continue to occur in New York City.

• Two new oral medications, zoliflodacin and gepotidacin, are now available for the treatment of uncomplicated urogenital gonorrhea.

• Zoliflodacin and gepotidacin can be considered in cases of non-susceptible gonorrhea, cases in which standard of care is contraindicated, or when a person has intolerance of or, after appropriate counseling, declines first-line treatment.

• These new medications should be reserved for people with few or no other treatment options given limited safety data.

• The first-line treatment for uncomplicated urogenital and extragenital gonorrhea remains a single 500 milligram (mg) intramuscular dose of ceftriaxone. Treatment for coinfection with chlamydia with oral doxycycline (100 mg twice daily for 7 days) should be administered when chlamydial infection has not been excluded.

• Clinical consultation for gonorrhea infections with suspected cephalosporin treatment failure is available by calling the NYC Health Department Provider Access Line at 866-692-3641.

• There is currently no clinical nucleic acid amplification testing (NAAT)-based antibiotic susceptibility testing (AST) available to determine if suspected treatment failure is related to an antibiotic-resistant gonorrhea infection. In such cases, perform NAAT and culture.

• Send culture specimens to laboratories that perform AST and include cephalosporins (ceftriaxone and cefixime) in the antibiotic susceptibility panel.

• If a laboratory that performs AST is not available, send NAAT and culture specimens to the NYC Health Department’s Public Health Laboratory. Call the NYC Health Department’s Sexually Transmitted Infections (STI) Surveillance Unit at 347-396-7201 (Monday to Friday, 9 a.m. to 5 p.m.) for pre-authorization or to notify of suspected treatment failures.

In summary, there are now cases of gonorrhea that don't respond to first-line treatments. The options we have for non-susceptible gonorrhea are limited and have limited safety data. The standard test for gonorrhea doesn't differentiate between susceptible and non-susceptible gonorrhea, because they use nucleic acid amplification to work quickly. But it doesn't provide the same “sensitivity to antibiotics” data that older bacterial culture methods provide.

How big a problem is this? It's not huge yet:

Neisseria gonorrhoeae isolates with reduced susceptibility to cephalosporins continue to be reported in NYC, with 0.4% of isolates in 2025 demonstrating reduced susceptibility to ceftriaxone or cefixime . Antibiotic-resistant strains of gonorrhea and transmission of these strains have been, and continue to be, a public health concern.

Yes, before we obsess about this tiny risk, it's worth knowing there are new options that could bring hope even to young children who are also at risk in the context of sexual abuse and early sexual debut:

In December 2025, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved two new oral medications, zoliflodacin and gepotidacin 3 , for the treatment of uncomplicated urogenital gonorrhea in people ages 12 years and older. 1 Both are considered first-in-class antibiotics. Zoliflodacin and gepotidacin can be considered in cases of non-susceptible gonorrhea, cases in which the standard of care is contraindicated, or when people are intolerant of or decline first-line treatment. These medications should be reserved for people with few or no other treatment options given limited safety data.

OK, so what are we gonna do? We have recommendations from the New York State Department of Health to follow!

Guidance and recommendations for clinicians related to gonorrhea screening frequency, diagnostic methods, counseling, and management of sex partners (including use of expedited partner therapy ) can be found in our July 10, 2023, Dear Colleague letter . Updated information about treatment options and important guidance about retesting and management of suspected treatment failures are provided below.

Given most people are gonna be treated empirically, it's crucial, for those living in New York, or other areas where non-susceptible gonococcal infection becomes prevalent, to get a test after their antibiotic course to make sure they were cured. Again, I will quote the New York Department of Health as an appeal to authority:

Test of cure and retesting guidelines

All people with pharyngeal gonococcal infection should undergo clinical evaluation and NAAT test of cure 7 to 14 days after treatment to ensure that there are no signs and symptoms of gonorrhea and that treatment is considered adequate and complete.

Within three months of gonorrhea treatment, regardless of site of infection, retest people to ensure reinfection has not occurred. Some people with resolved symptoms and for whom reinfection has been ruled out may have a repeated positive NAAT, which could represent a false positive result. If the repeat NAAT was from the same manufacturer, consider testing with a NAAT that uses a different platform.

And if the treatment didn't work, it's still not likely that it's super gonorrhea causing it:

Most persistent symptoms or recurrent positive tests for gonorrhea are due to reinfection rather than antibiotic-resistant treatment failure . Ask the person about interim exposure and retest for gonorrhea using both NAAT and culture with AST at sites of exposure. Send culture specimens to laboratories that perform AST and include cephalosporins (ceftriaxone and cefixime) in the antibiotic susceptibility panel. AST for gonorrhea isolates is also available through the NYC Health Department’s Public Health Laboratory via eOrder . Call the NYC Health Department’s STI Surveillance Unit at 347-396-7201 (Monday to Friday, 9 a.m. to 5 p.m.) for pre-authorization.

There's even a phone number to call if you need to phone a friend at the health department to talk about your patient, which is helpful for those of us who are psych psychiatrists and pretty far out from our work, infectious disease and population health:

Clinical consultation for clinicians who diagnose gonorrhea infection with suspected cephalosporin treatment failure is available by calling the NYC Health Department Provider Access Line at 866-692-3641, Monday to Friday, 9 a.m. to 5 p.m., or by contacting the STD Clinical Consultation Network .

Cases of suspected treatment failure should be reported to the NYC Health Department’s STI Surveillance Unit by calling 347-396-7201, Monday to Friday, 9 a.m. to 5 p.m. The NYC Health Department will facilitate communications with the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, as needed.

Please take great care of yourself and your patients. It's worth noting that sexually transmitted infections don't mean you're a bad person, and they shouldn't ruin your life. They can lead to adverse consequences like reactive arthritis if they're not treated early. Many sexually transmitted infections are asymptomatic. People don't notice anything. This means if you're sexually active, you should get tested regularly. They are both free places to do this, and you can just pay out-of-pocket if you go to quest.com or other testing websites. It's not challenging to do, it's private, and it can help prevent long-term consequences for both yourself and the people you love. Shame can really get in the way, and it's really easy for people with propensity to psychosis or obsession to become preoccupied with the risks and ignore the actual treatment of the health condition. We don't stigmatize people who get colds, we don't stigmatize people who get cancer, but we do stigmatize people with sexually transmitted infections and with psychiatric conditions. So, what's the most important advice for my colleagues in healthcare?

Don't be a jerk about this.

Please bring your most compassionate self to your visit with your patient, particularly those with psychiatric illnesses, or who are concerned about sexually transmitted infections, or who should get tested. Act like it's “no big deal,” and then “everyone gets tested all the time,” because it shouldn't be, and everyone should, respectively.

Be kind to each other, be kind to yourself, get tested regularly, and make sure you test for the cure. And if you have a suspicion of treatment nonresponse, gynecological infection, there's a phone, a friend at the Department of Health, use it.

Zoliflodacin 3 grams oral suspension in a single dose

Dan Karlin, M.D., Our Interviewee.

OM: Now, we’ve known each other for years. I remember even maybe eight years ago having this conversation about the future of measurement, and so I’ve been an AI maximalist in some ways thanks to you saying, “If only we could measure this change.” And you had this strange thing happen: a measurement guy who happened to be a psychiatrist gets a call from someone who I knew, who you used to be adjacent to at camp, shall we say.

DK: I was never his counselor, but I was his counselor’s supervisor.

OM: And you had a number of other superstars: Adam Farren was at your camp, he’s at Canvas now; Jacob Brody was a camper. Whatever that camp was, a lot of people went into healthcare technology.

DK: It’s funny to encounter people who weren’t there but overlap with everybody else. It was a YMCA camp. The YMCA aspect certainly didn’t mean there was a religious requirement, as you observed. You could have absolutely gone.

OM: Only if I’d been invited. And so you were presented with this opportunity to consider joining a company that was working on taking LSD and turning it into a therapeutic. When you’re evaluating that, there’s a lot of business stuff that goes into it, but you were brought this drug, which you certainly knew about; it’s been part of the history of psychiatry since we’ve had any compounds around. How did you think about it?

DK: Like you, and like so many folks, I had been paying attention to the research in the space, though that research was largely oriented toward psilocybin for sociocultural reasons, really. LSD was tarred and feathered the worst in the prohibition era. In part, I think, because LSD was the drug that spurred the first psychedelic era. The discovery of the remarkably potent effects of LSD, which exerts its effects at microgram levels (we’re giving a hundred micrograms in our trials), was very exciting to psychiatry, to medicine. There was this upswell of interest, papers by all sorts of folks on various potential uses in psychiatry and medicine. But of course there was also non-medical use, and that non-medical use was associated with certain countercultural aspects of society. It was also, of course, associated with the most dominant hegemonic forces in society, like our CIA. So both sides of that spectrum were tremendously interested in the drug and its potential power.

When I looked at the company and the drug under development, it didn’t take much for me to recognize that the bulk of the historical evidence, albeit evidence gathered under not the same conditions or with the same rigor as we conduct research today, and this accumulating evidence from the second wave of psychiatric interest in psychedelics, suggested that this drug worked differently than the psychiatric drugs that had dominated psychiatry for the past sixty years. It seemed to have remarkably transformative effects.

OM: And LSD is definitely a psychedelic. When the definition of “classic psychedelic” includes “LSD-like effects,” one thinks that puts it squarely in the middle of the category. Similar to Haldol: it’s really potent, it’s definitely antipsychotic. The most potent thing defines the class.

DK: We try to define classes as best we can, and then the interesting stuff is always in the details, not what gets papered over with the language and the label.

OM: The only one we don’t have to debate.

DK: Classic psychedelic? It is indeed. But what folks were seeing that I found most interesting is that when treating people, almost regardless of the presenting condition, with classic psychedelics, while there are changes that can absolutely be measured with symptom scales, these drugs were doing something different than basically all psychiatric drugs. When you think about what we use today in psychiatry, they’re suppressive: they suppress some sort of thing. And if that thing is mostly bad, great. If you feel mostly bad and you take a drug that makes you feel less bad as its dominant effect, that’s useful.

OM: Even in our grandiose naming, we put “anti” in the name.

DK: Anti-something. Not “pro-happiness.” An antidepressant. And that’s true of antipsychotics. It’s also interestingly true of drugs that categorically seem to have a different name, like psychostimulant. We don’t use psychostimulants to stimulate; we use them to reduce the effect of extraneous stimuli and allow people to focus on the stimuli they wish to. That’s actually a suppressive effect. So for people with ADHD, stimulants are suppressive.

In a world of mostly suppressive drugs, the way patients treated with classic psychedelics talked about the effects of their treatment was actually expansive. They talked about having more interest: more interest in their own lives, more connection to themselves, more connection to others, more ability to connect to their own story of themselves. Which sounds to me a lot like how people talk after very successful psychotherapy, not how people talk after they’ve been on an SSRI for six to twelve weeks. Of everything about the category, this sense that maybe these drugs could be opening for people rather than closing, enabling for people’s lives rather than just suppressing badness, is what made this an obvious move for me.

OM: And so you made the change. You went from being a measurement guy to being at the helm, as Chief Medical Officer, of a company developing pharmacologic agents for therapeutic use.

DK: Of course I still have to oversee measurement. We measure a lot of things and look very closely at those measurements as we go. Measurement is at the core of all science. If the point of science is to try to learn things that are true about the world, then measurement is arguably the only way to do that. But measurement isn’t the core goal of what we do. What we do is try to demonstrate, through measurement, that the drug itself does the things we’d like it to do without doing too many things we don’t want it to do, so that its efficacy exceeds its harms, and that both are describable: we can provide good evidence for the positive effects and provide real insight into the potential negative effects.
