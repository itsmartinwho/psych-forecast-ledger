This newsletter has, as an editorial bent, been excited about people getting well from psychiatric illness much more quickly (and completely!) than was previously assumed possible. We even started a live-action newsletter event called Rapid Acting Mental Health Treatment (RAMHT). It’s pronounced like “Ramp-t,” not “Ram-it.” Please, have some decorum and taste.

Get RAMHT Tickets!

We—a team led by Owen Muir , M.D., and Grady Hannah , and a growing team since then, including Carlene MacMillan , M.D., and others—have been producing live action events twice a year since 2022.

I had this lovely photo taken at CNS Summit 2025.

Partners have included friends like Magnus Medical , Simple Practice , Lykos , Videra Health , MdHub.ai , Sanmai , Ampa Health , Osmind , Radial , GrayMatters Health , Mind Med , Acacia Clinics , Nightware , Hydra Strategies , Psyrin , Emobot , and BrainFutures . Talk about a prime group of innovators!

We were a little ahead of the curve, but the world is catching up. Let’s do a quick run-down of what has happened, publicly, when it comes to rapid-acting treatments and technologies:

AI-Scribes Have Transformed Mental Health Care

Up to 40% of therapists now use AI scribes! Unlike AI therapy bots, the impact has, empirically, been good for patients and therapists:

Burden was significantly less at 3-months compared to baseline. Productivity increased by an average of 5.8 %. From the interviews, four themes described participants’ experiences: using the scribe had a positive impact on therapeutic alliance and administrative workload, trust facilitated the use of the scribe and AHPs appreciated the scribe for different aspects of documentation. 1

It is making it less onerous to provide care, but helping get the documentation done faster. Reducing burnout among therapists and clinicians is good, for your author specifically, I will note. Thanks, MDHub and others!

fMRI Guided SAINT TMS is Covered by Medicare (in some settings, anyway)!

Nolan Williams, a generational innovator, helped create both SAINT and the company to commercialize this technology, culminating in both an RCT 2 and open-label papers 3 . Medicare covers it in some hospital-based outpatient settings!

And, on top of that, they built an algorithm based on wearable technology to predict when you need a subsequent treatment to prevent relapse. 4

Accelerated dTMS and Adolescent dTMS are now FDA-cleared Treatments !

fMRI is all well and good, but it’s not available everywhere. I’ve had a close relationship with many friends in industry over many years, nudging, publishing, encouraging, and, to be frank, nagging. I wanted to see the dTMS technology created by BrainsWay, a technology that doesn’t require fMRI to target, get an FDA-label for an accelerated protocol, and for adolescent depression. Both are true as of 2025. This makes the treatment scalable and thus accessible to more people who need it. 78% remission of depression in a treatment that can be administered in just six half-days (plus taper sessions for durability) is a big deal.

I will smugly point out that, prior to being an investigator contributing data to both of the above to FDA submissions, I was screaming into the wilderness about both accelerated treatment 5 and adolescent depression 6 treatment.

Well, it’s looking less wilderness-like now. The next step, of course, is broad insurance coverage. Feel free to call your health plan, HR department, and government and request that this be included in your covered benefits!

The Psychedelics Are Here…Almost.

We had multiple studies published results this year, including impressive phase II data from MindMed, on their LSD analog MM-120 . Psilocybin , including COMP360 , has made tremendous progress, and…just as AtaiBeckly announced data on their 5-MEO-DMT asset , I helped co-author a paper with colleagues, including Burton Tabaac and Kenneth Shinozuka. We have a preprint available here .

Multiple Companies Are Now Attempting to Scale Rapid-Acting Treatments

My readers have noticed I have a new-ish job as Chief Medical Officer at Radial , which is hard at work bringing accelerated and rapid-acting treatments to patients. We are, thankfully, not alone! Beckley Clinical, Stella Centers, Salma Health, Everbright Health, Hope Theraputics, First Tracks, Big Leap, and even Osmind are all working on the picks and shovels approach to getting rapid-acting treatments to everyone. There are a lot of people working very hard to work out the best way to support this burgeoning market. There is, in my opinion, more than enough suffering for the number of companies above and more.

In this newsletter, we’ve been banging a drum for years—people deserve to get well sooner rather than later. It’s more true than ever, but insurance coverage still lags—I’m looking at you, 2026.

Thanks for reading, all.

Tragedy shapes us. Shakespeare understood this, as did close to all of the kids I ever saw in the Bellevue Comprehensive Psychiatric Emergency Program.

“What’s done cannot be undone”.

—Macbeth

A new study— Mortality Risk Following a Household Suicide — in JAMA Open highlights this uncomfortable fact, yet again, but with data 1 .

The authors asked a simple question:

Does household exposure to suicide increase mortality among surviving members?

But with a MASSIVE data set. This may be the biggest sample I’ve ever seen or written about:

In this cohort study of over 101 million Brazilians

I’ve written about cohort studies before, and I’m sure I will again. Since it would not be ethical to randomize someone to a known “harmful thing,” we are forced to research only what is related to that harmful exposure by examining large datasets and using the resulting correlations to craft subsequent research to prevent harm.

Modest Mouse, Live, December 2022, 1 week before the death of Drummer Jeremiah Green. © Owen Muir. This photo will make sense by the end.

This data was from a huge epidemiological study called, fittingly, the 100 Million Brazilian Cohort.

It’s not a completely representative sample of everyone one could imagine:

The baseline of The 100 Million Brazilian Cohort comprises 131 697 800 low-income individuals in 35 358 415 families from 2011 to 2018. The Cohort population is mostly composed of children and young adults, with a higher proportion of females than the general Brazilian population, who identify themselves as Brown and live in the urban area of the country. 2

And these restrictions limit generalizability. However, the number of individuals is staggering. Only a subset—101 our or 131m—had data that allowed inclusion in this analysis.

Suicide, as I’ve written before, is risky to those around it. We have media guidelines about even writing about death by suicide—guidelines I’ve been careful to follow when I’ve had to do so. If, however, the suicide you read about or see on the news is, in a way, toxic, the exposure to someone in your own household dying by suicide could be understood to be a higher dose of that memetic poison. The tragic death of someone close to us by suicide—even imagined suicide—is among the most famous of Shakespearean plotlines! Romeo finds the sleeping Juliette, but fearing her death by suicide, in his grief, ends his own life by poison:

For fear of that, I still will stay with thee,

And never from this palace of dim night

Depart again. Here, here will I remain

With worms that are thy chamber maids.

Oh, here will I set up my everlasting rest,

And shake the yoke of inauspicious stars

From this world-wearied flesh.

—Romeo and Juliette, Act 5, Scene 3.

This close proximity—that of star-crossed lovers—wouldn’t even have met the criteria for being in the same household in this Brazilian sample. They had to double check, of course. At this scale, it’s crucial even to ask—how accurate is the definition of household members? They used some fancy machine learning on a sample of pairs of individuals to determine if they had the correct pairs. This generated a ROC—AUC curve, similar to the output of my prior research study on AI identification of Tardive Dyskinesia.

The ROC-AUC curve for the “household” definition from the supplemental materials.

The authors tuned this data to determine that:

From the ROC curve, the optimal cut-off point of 0.92 …was identified. Using this optimal cut-off to declare matches, 97.8% of the linked pairs were estimated to be true matches, and 2.2% of the linked pairs were estimated to be false matches.

Which means when they thought they saw a pair in the same household in their data, they were correct 97.8% of the time—only 2.2% were likely to be star-crossed lovers who actually were in separate Capulet and Montague households but spent so much time together they might be misidentified.

They did more tidying up of the data from there, excluding those below 10 years old and above 110:

Since suicides are rare in age groups below 10 years old, we excluded individuals younger than 10 years old at the time of registration or those who would reach 10 years old during our follow-up period (January 1, 2001, to December 31, 2018). …We also excluded individuals aged above 110 years at registration, as this likely led to missing or unlinked death certificates.

Because if someone seemed to be 110 , they were actually dead. There was a vast volume of other error checks, data clean-up, and good sense that went into the authors’ ability to confidently know they were answering the question they had initially asked:

What risk does the suicide of a household member pose to the survivors?

The risk is high—more people die after a household member completes suicide than would otherwise be the case, and soon after the death by suicide was the risk most pronounced:

The risk of suicide among exposed individuals was more than 4 times higher (aHR (adjusted Hazard Ratio), 4.42) with 101 of these deaths (44%) occurring within 2 years of the index suicide case.

These subsequent deaths are not all subsequent suicides, however:

All the ways that sorrow can lead to death.

Not all loss is equally poisonous to the lives of household members thereafter—the loss of a woman in a household is higher risk, as is the loss of a young person.

Exposure to a female index case (ed., “index case” = person who died by suicide) was associated with higher all-cause mortality excluding suicide among surviving household members [compared to a male death by suicide].

and…

Exposure to a younger index case (aged 10-24 years) was associated with higher all-cause mortality (HR, 1.16; 95% CI, 1.08-1.26) and suicide (HR, 1.68; 95% CI, 1.22-2.31) compared with an older index case (aged 60 years or older).

But, perplexingly, if a young female died by suicide, it was less likely to lead to harm to household members than an older male’s death:

An interaction showed that a young female index case was associated with lower risk of all-cause mortality (HR, 0.72; 95% CI, 0.61-0.84) relative to older male cases

We mourn differently for different family members, at least in Brazil, and metabolize the loss with varying degrees of toxicity, it seems.

Older men in the household—Fathers, we imagine, and perhaps grandfathers—died soon after a suicide loss. Still, their hearts failed more “naturally” along with the triple risk of death by suicide themselves:

Among surviving household members, male individuals had a 75% higher risk of all-cause mortality excluding suicide (HR, 1.75; 95% CI, 1.64-1.86) and over 3 times higher risk of suicide (HR, 3.58; 95% CI, 2.65-4.84) compared with female individuals.

Here is the graph of “time to death” after losing a household member to suicide:

To put that in context, there are very, very few single-incident exposures that can cause this sort of massive increase in the risk of death:

• Large dose radiation exposure (One-time dose ≥1 Gy from an industrial accident, such as fluoroscopy overdose.

• Surviving carbon monoxide poisoning that almost asphyxiated you the first time

• Surviving cyanide poisoning

• Severe smoke inhalation (house fire, etc).

• Being a firefighter who responded to 9/11 and survived that day (roughly a 140% mortality rate).

Losing a loved one to suicide is poison. It’s as deadly or more deadly than the most lethal things we tend to imagine. We have a whole world trade center survivor program to follow closely those who survived 9/11. This data suggests that we should follow the survivors of suicide just as closely, and with no less concern.

Modest Mouse, as usual, were being accurate epidemiologists when they wrote the following:

Broken hearts want broken necks
I’ve done some things that I want to forget, but I can’t

—Modest Mouse,

“Broke,” off the “Building Something Out of Nothing”

We need to be careful with our lives and with the lives of those who are struggling with profound loss.

This is a new series. My writing is usually for a general audience. You, anybody, maybe your patient, perhaps you are a health professional, maybe you run a massive health plan, I don't know. It's addressed to people broadly. It's been pointed out to me, by Derek , the author of 5HT , and a prolific healthcare communicator, that I should write directly to some readers , and, sadly, not to others. So today, I'm starting to do that. This missive is directed to health professionals. The rest of you, dear readers, are encouraged to read along, but it's not for you. It's for them. Health professionals: this advice is for you, designed to help make your life less chaotic and stressful.

All your Ex-lovers visited me on Halloween! I’m so, so sorry. © Owen Muir, 2025.

The rest of my readers are consigned to being voyeurs in this article and other articles like it! Without further ado, this is the first of “Owen's Letters to the Health Professionals,” not a name that I spent a lot of time on. Feedback is welcome, dear readers—I love hearing from you. As an aside, this advice applies to anyone who prescribes treatments, but I’m using “doctor” and “physician” as shorthand to avoid “provider” or “doctors and advanced practice nurses and physician’s assistants and psychologists in some states with additional training.” The language is meant to be linguistically less clunky , not exclusionary.

Good morning, readers.

That simple statement included an assumption—that you will be reading this newsletter at the same time of day that I’m writing it. I’m also thinking of readers, generally—anyone who could be reading this newsletter, on the internet, and free to all.

The audience I have in mind matters for what I write. For health professionals, to whom this column is addressed, the audience for what you write, in the form of prescriptions and medical documentation, isn’t always obvious. You write a prescription to the pharmacist to allow the dispensing of the medicines or other treatments as you, with your medical judgment, deem fit.

Your friends and family will know you have this superpower. Your romantic partners will, if they deign to connect with you, know it too. It’s totally normal to want help from a lover when a moment of distress or illness strikes.

It’s also true that love can sour. Some lovers end up being $&^#ing crazy. I don’t mean that in the specific context of “psychiatric illness,” although those exist in humans at very high rates of population prevalence. Your lover could have a delusional mental illness. Or they could hold grudges. Or they could have a broken heart. I don’t know what will go wrong in any of our relationships. I do know, for a certainty, that some of our relationships will end, and tension will arise. I know humans will seek to exact revenge. That revenge will be calibrated to hurt us. Everything you have ever done will be reinterpreted with malice. Reliably, your professional license will be used against you. The painful truth?

I’ll repeat myself, at the risk of being rude, there are 50 ways to stick it to your lover. This article is about how to protect yourself while doing the right thing. The simplest way to avoid major and avoidable heartache is to…

NEVER EVER to PROVIDE MEDICAL CARE for people in your life…

who are not patients in your practice. This should be a class in medical school, or the final questions on your Step One exam.

Some examples:

Q: “I have a UTI, can you call in nitrofurantoin?”

Best medicolegal A: No. I’m a radiologist ; it’s outside my scope of practice.

Q: Oh, gosh, my doctor is not picking up! Can you refill my Zoloft since we are on vacation?

Best A: “Call your doctor’s coverage?”

Follow-up Q: “I really don’t want to go into withdrawal and ruin our honeymoon!”

Best medicolegal A: “Let’s do a thorough initial evaluation, document it in my medical record system, and never have a sexual relationship ever again, because the second I write that psychiatric med, you become my patient, and there is a prohibition on sexual relationships with our patients.”

I’m not saying your partner is a total psychopath who is planning on extorting you based on the above rationale. I am also not saying they won’t turn out to be exactly that. Worse yet, they don’t care about the extortion—they want to exact revenge for their heart’s ache, and in their grief, decide to scorch the earth with your professional career.

We all have Skeletons in our closets, don’t let your prescribing be one!

Some people are vengeful. Some are malicious. You are unlikely to know who is who in advance. The only safe stance is to assume things could go south.

I also realize we all have moments of lapses in paranoia. I never assumed my ex-fiance would leave my life, and our apartment, with a bottle of Vicodin that had been prescribed for me after a surgery, a bottle of Klonopin prescribed for my sleep difficulties years previously, and my mom’s 3-carat engagement ring. Nor did I expect this to become a matter of public record in the NY Post 1 :

Diamonds are forever — but this engagement sure wasn’t.

A Brooklyn man is suing his ex after she refused to return a $40,000, white-gold diamond ring, according to court papers.

Owen Muir handed over the family ring to [redacted] in August 2012. But following their breakup, he wants a judge to force [redacted] to turn over the ring — or its cash value.

[redacted] could not be reached for comment.

Oh, it ended up as the talk of the town in Australia, too!

Owen Muir handed over the family ring to [redacted] in August 2012, the New York Post reported.

But after the breakup, he decided a judge was needed to force Ms [redacted] to give back the ring - or its cash value.

And no—I never got my mom’s ring back. Vita asked me to try, but Ms. [redacted] had lawyers in the family, and I didn’t have the many 10s of thousands of dollars to see that litigation through to the end, so $10k+ of legal fees later, I was forced to drop it. The comments section of those Rupert Murdoch-owned rags was off the hook—but it seems the Post retires old comments after a decade or so, so I can’t easily reference them.

And, THANK GOD , I did not prescribe those medicines to her. However, as a physician, I had an open question as to my obligation to report the diversion of controlled substances to my prescribing physicians, which, of course, I did.

I'm going to assume that people reading this do not have a time machine . They can't Orson Wells 2 it and undo prior decisions to prescribe treatment to family members and loved ones. They also can't go back in time and not get into relationships with people who, later, turn out to be much more problematic than they ever imagined.

That brings us to tip number one:

Don’t treat your family members—do a family member swap!

The AMA has ethical guidance that agrees with my headline above:

Physicians generally should not treat themselves or members of their immediate families. Professional objectivity may be compromised when an immediate family member or the physician is the patient; the physician’s personal feelings may unduly influence his or her professional medical judgment, thereby interfering with the care being delivered.

That word, “generally,” is doing a lot of heavy lifting in that first sentence. It also, in most cases, doesn’t violate a community standard to do so, making it less likely you’d lose a med mal case if your now vengeful lover decides to file a lawsuit. There must be a dereliction of duty resulting in direct damages to lose one of those. However, the point is not to leave your life open to that kind of chaos, even if you’d win. Lawsuits suck, and lawsuits that force you to spend time with vengeful ex-lovers are all of that suck, squared. Thus, there are a few options. The best is to have a handy referral, like General Medicine , in your back pocket. General Medicine 3 handles most medical problems for most people—” I need a refill,” “I need an antibiotic,” etc., well and quickly. The most important thing about such a company is that they aren’t dating or married to the person who is asking you, and won’t subsequently break up in a way that could be messy. The other slightly less clean version of this is to “swap” with a friend from your medical life. You can send your family member to them, and they can send their family members to you. This way, it’s someone else, and they have the same incentives as all physicians to conduct an appropriate evaluation.

If it’s an Emergency, sometimes it’s OK to write a Prescription.

The AMA ethics guidelines highlight that sometimes it’s fine-ish:

It would not always be inappropriate to undertake self-treatment or treatment of immediate family members. In emergency settings or isolated settings where there is no other qualified physician available, physicians should not hesitate to treat themselves or family members until another physician becomes available. In addition, while physicians should not serve as a primary or regular care provider for immediate family members, there are situations in which routine care is acceptable for short-term, minor problems.

This advice comes with one major caveat: this doesn’t apply to controlled substances:

Except in emergencies, it is not appropriate for physicians to write prescriptions for controlled substances for themselves or immediate family members.

Keep in mind, it doesn't have to be an emergency, according to your soon-to-be-vengeful ex-partner; it would have to be an emergency, according to any outside observer. Don't write controlled substances for people who are not patients in your practice, ever. That's not from the AMA, that's advice from me, because you never know when that person is gonna try to use it against you, and it's never likely to look good. There's always a way to get somebody else to write that prescription, and it always looks better for that to be the case. If there is an emergency, and you do have to write that prescription, you want to subsequently call a colleague, after the emergency has passed, and have them document something or other that can be used, later, to establish how completely reasonable your emergency response was.

Document everything… on paper.

If you're gonna write a prescription for someone in your life, and there is even the tiniest chance, which is always the case, that something will happen, you'll have a falling out, and that person will come for your head, is there a strategy to protect yourself?

Yes. Even if they're not gonna sue you for malpractice, a board complaint can make your life pretty miserable for a very long time. What seemed reasonable at the moment is a lot less likely to seem reasonable when framed in a malicious light later.

Thus, be prepared for what a medical board would say. And when it comes to prescribing, the standard of medical care is to document what you're doing and why. Now, I'm sure all of my colleagues reading this always document the medical care they provide immediately . We probably document concurrently. I'm not going to second-guess when you do your documentation, but every once in a while, in the heat of the moment, it's easy to forget to complete that note.

But you need a note. You have to document your assessment and your plan for all your patients, and that means if you wrote a prescription, that person became your patient, and the big thing that medical boards will call out if there is some nonsense later? Is that you didn't document it? So if you're going to prescribe a loved one, it means you're also going to write a note . Just this fact alone is likely to make you less enthusiastic about filling prescriptions for people in your life outside of your practice.

Q: Can you write me a prescription?

A: Sure!

Q: Can you generate an entire medical chart, and then evaluate my allergies, comorbidities, talk to me about risks and benefits, and then fill a prescription, and all of that without getting paid?

A: Oh, my.

However, there is no current legal mandate that all medical records be completed electronically. And paper doesn't have a rigorous and auditable electronic trail as when the paper chart was created. Therefore, the date you handwrite on a piece of paper? That's the date of that medical encounter. I encourage all of us to, as a standard of practice, every time we're writing a note for a family member, do an assessment, do a plan, write a note, write it by hand, write it on paper, and stick it in a file folder, somewhere locked in your home. This way, if a vengeful lover decides to report you to the medical board for writing the prescriptions you shouldn't have, the major bludgeon they have to come after you with will be averted. Medical boards hate medical care without appropriate documentation, and with good reason. So if you're going to write a prescription for a family member, you're also gonna write a note. But the more you write those notes, routinely, on paper, in a physical file folder, the more plausible it is that that's what you do every time you write a prescription for somebody who's not seen in the normal confines of your medical practice, and the more defensible, each prescription, documented on paper, in a file folder, becomes.

I would recommend being old-fashioned when documenting in such instances, because it becomes impossible (for malicious actors) to go back in time and determine exactly when each note was physically written. I'm not saying you should write these notes after the fact, although that is not verboten, medico-legally. It may be a lot of work, and that's the point; it should discourage you from engaging in this practice at all. But if you're gonna do it, protect yourself, and that means documentation, and the best sort of documentation for these use cases is on paper, written by hand, and stuck in a lock filing cabinet, in the back of a disused lavatory, at the bottom of a staircase, with a sign on the door, saying beware of the leopard.

New York’s Center for Hospital Care, or NYCHC, is fictitious. Medical school in America takes four years. The fourth year is a strange one. In most schools, in the first and second year, in keeping with the Flickinger report from over 100 years ago—which continues to drive medical education—you learn basic science. There is a lot of basic science to learn. You spend some time in outpatient medical offices with primary care doctors. It’s here that you get exposure to real patients and real doctor stuff, but you don’t have any actual responsibility. It’s supposed to feel like a responsibility, but somebody else always has their hand on the wheel or just next to yours, so the car doesn’t crash.

The third year of medical school is also kabuki theater. You act as if you have responsibility. Occasionally, by accident, you may be responsible for something. The first time you get close to responsibility, the kind of responsibility you’ll have in your first year of residency training, referred to as an internship, is in the fourth year. This is when young medical students, or old medical students, as was the case with me, audition.

Sub-internships, or sub-Is for short, are monthlong rotations where you pretend to be an intern. And you might be given some responsibility.

One issue in the US health care system is that the number of residency training spots, without which you can’t practice as a doctor, has been capped by the federal government since 1997. They are funded by Medicare. Residents are physicians. They generate billing for hospitals. They are a butterfly that’s only halfway out of the chrysalis, and you can imagine how awkward that would be for a butterfly. No crawling around like a caterpillar and flying around like a butterfly, just this awkward flopping escape from one thing as the metamorphosis takes its course.

My sub-internship, my audition, was at NYCHC.

Sometimes, in the middle of pretending, something real happens. Abraham Lincoln was shot with a real gun in the middle of a play. Alec Baldwin accidentally shot someone on set. At NYCHC, I had a very real running with a strange neurological condition.

“I’ve been dead for some time,” the gentleman said, reclining casually in his hospital bed.

There wasn’t a lot of affect in his tone. That’s the sort of thing you say if you’re learning how to do a mental status exam. The patient’s mood was whatever they said it was, but you put that in quotes. This person said, “Good.” So in the medical note that I was learning to write, I would write mood: “Good.” The effect is how the person looks like they feel; it’s an observation. It’s supposed to be objective. And, objectively, the person didn’t look very upset.

The gentleman just said he’s been dead for some time. From most people, I would expect that statement to come with some distress or confusion or something. No one would expect that to be said all. Axiomatically, dead people don’t say that. It’s only the not-dead who can say that they are dead, because dead men don’t talk.

Affect: congruent with stated mood. Stable. Euthymic.

“Hi, there. I’m working in consultation, liaison psychiatry, and service. My name is Owen Muir, and I’m not a doctor yet—I’m a medical student.”

I said, like they train us to do, about the fact that I’m not a doctor. With ten years of distance, it wasn’t his confusion about my role on the team that was to be worried about. He thought he was dead. That’s a real problem. You know, in your brain.

“Oh, well, now. A while. It must be a while,” the gentleman responded.

This was a vague response.

Two hours later, in a cramped hallway, there were a couple of computers on wheels, which had been renamed “workstations on wheels” because someone was offended at one point in one hospital when somebody asked where the COW was. Computer on wheels. Cow . And someone got offended. So, workstation on wheels, or WOW—that was a solution to the problem of sensitive people with bad hearing. I was on the WOW. And I was looking up Cotard’s delusion.

This vague response that I had heard earlier and the rest of the conversation that followed fit in quite nicely with what I was reading. This was weird.

In what I consider to be a historical mistake, despite the joint status of the American Board of Psychiatry and Neurology, we decided to make those different medical specialties. Jules Cotard (1840–89) was a neurologist and psychiatrist when they were the same thing.

Dr. Cotard referred to “The Delusion of Negation” as a mental illness of varied severity.

Walking corpse syndrome is a rare mental disorder in which the affected person holds the delusional belief that they are dead, do not exist, are putrefying, or have lost their blood or internal organs. [1] Statistical analysis of a hundred-patient cohort indicated that denial of self-existence is present in 45% of the cases of Cotard’s syndrome; the other 55% of the patients presented with delusions of immortality.

This didn’t make it into the DSM. So, it’s not one of the first things you learn about. But it’s specific, and it’s uncommon, and it’s strange, and the gentleman I just met proved to me it’s definitely a thing.

It’s almost always the case that a person with Cotard’s delusion has had an injury in the brain. That injury is usually a stroke. A stroke is when a blood clot gets lodged in a blood vessel, and that causes part of the brain to die because blood can’t get to it. The brain needs blood. It needs the oxygen; it needs the cleaning; it’s bad to have the brain be deprived of blood. We referred to this deprivation of oxygenated blood as an infarction. It’s the same infarction that we talk about in myocardial infarctions or heart attacks, a more general term. In the brain, we call this a stroke. In medicine, we call many things the same things and many other things. One thing being called many things is one of the reasons medicines are confusing.

But thinking you’re dead, insisting you’re dead, nothing being able to convince you that you’re not dead—that’s confusing as well. It doesn’t matter what you call it.

“How can you be dead? You don’t know you’re talking to me?”

“Yes, of course, I’m talking to you.”

“And dead people don’t generally have conversations with a medical student?”

“That’s true.”

“But how are you talking to me?”

“By answering your questions,” he said.

“Dead people don’t get up and talk, usually.”

“Well, there was that one guy—what’s his name?”

“Do you mean Jesus ?”

“Yeah, Jesus, he rose from the dead. It’s happened before.”

Socioculturally, my gentleman with Cotard’s delusion had a point.

He also had a series of small strokes throughout a deep region of his brain. These are called lacunar infarcts. There’s that infarction word again. A lot of important brains are in very small areas of space. The deeper you get in the brain, and the further back in the skull towards the neck, the closer to the brain stem, the more ancient the brain structures, and the more crucial they are to hold our shit together.

When these structures go, because they are cut off from blood, they die very quickly, and if the cortex is still there, it’s the storytelling stuff. It’s the part of our brain that puts things together. And it has to make sense of what’s happening, but not in a way that makes any sense. It just has to tell a story. The cortex is tasked with “higher order” issues than breathing or ensuring our eyes track together. That higher-order stuff is storytelling.

The story doesn’t have to make sense.

Our brains tell us stories. We believe the stories we tell ourselves. It’s part of the invisible lubrication of the human experience—we believe first, and make sense second, all the while telling ourselves we are right the whole time.

There wasn’t any reasoning I was going to do with this gentleman, who had a brain that, on a brain scan, showed four or five small areas of dead brain, thanks to strokes that happened both in the past and some more recently.

He had a heart condition called atrial fibrillation. He also had a hole in his heart, but he was born with it. We’re all born with a hole in the heart. Some of us have a hole closed. Some have that hole stay open. This person had a shortcut from one side of the heart to the other, and through that shortcut passed the clot that formed when his heart didn’t contract like it was supposed to.

The heart muscle at the top of the heart—the atrium—was wiggling rather than contracting in an organized way. That led to blood sitting there for a little longer than it was supposed to, and some of that clot flew through this little hole, the wormhole that let a clot bypass from one side of the heart to the other.

This little hole in his heart became a hole in his brain. And that tiny hole in his brain and the other small holes caused by prior clots led to a strange conversation.

It led to a hole in his reality. He was dead. That was the story his cortex was telling himself. It was utterly, completely believable. And he couldn’t tell himself otherwise. And he couldn’t tell a medical student otherwise. And he wasn’t alone in having this problem.

Dear Readers: This is recycled from another project—forgive me if you’ve read a version of this before. I spent much of today on an FDA submission and a podcast, just for your enjoyment, but I also wanted my dear readers to have something to show for it. That’s before I go back to bringing breakthrough treatments to the world at Radial on Monday. Therefore, this article will have to do its best on a Sunday Night.

© Owen Muir, 2025. Hard at work, making podcast edits.

Our society has made a deal with the devil: abandon people suffering from suicidal ideation to not have to think about it ourselves. We have created a cult of abdication of duty. We reliably scapegoat those who cross this River Styx. The helpers are not to be helped. Helping sick people places a burden on psychiatrists, and we don’t help them with this burden. In this way, we can decry suicidality and stand against death and sorrow without the risk of contaminating ourselves by standing too close to it. What if I could catch the thing ? We seem to be saying to ourselves. Try telling someone you’re a psychiatrist on a first date. I have. I ended up married to another psychiatrist.

In my newsletter, I sometimes write as the pseudonymous narrator O. Scott Muir, MD—a playful nod to the late author David Foster Wallace and his character in the posthumous book The Pale King. Mr. Foster Wallace named this protagonist David Wallace. The reason I have that slightly absurd character, for the purposes of narration, and the reason David Foster Wallace is not the one writing this chapter are the same: Suicide is a beast. It killed DFW. I avoided using my actual name online because I have been harassed for years. Yes, it’s creepy.

I haven’t told my story to anybody in its entirety. Maybe one or two people have ever heard it. In fact, other than my psychiatrist, mom, wife, and editor, very few people even know the truth of this next statement:

Among the people who struggled with suicidal ideation as a child is . . . me .

The public does know that this is an academic topic for me. I’m the coeditor of Adolescent Suicide and Self-Injury: Mentalizing Theory and Treatment . It’s a scholarly book, a guide to psychotherapy for suicidal youth (and families) using a modality—mentalization-based treatment, MBT. This modality is (frustratingly) left out of most American discussions of what might help suicidal people. What most people don’t know is that I didn’t pull that academic book together only because of abstract curiosity. I have a personal interest in the topic. The following is a bit about my “why.”

I remember watching TV when I was younger, and one of the common shows for me to watch was Behind the Music . It was the VH1 show, which was the only reason that I ever watched VH1. There was always a moment in every one of these identically structured biopics about Vanilla Ice or Milli Vanilli. In that moment, the artist would talk about how things got so bad that, at their lowest, they “even thought about suicide.”

This struck me as absurd. The reason is a bit dark. I had a hard time imagining someone only thinking about suicide once . In my childhood, I thought about suicide constantly . As far as I know, I have been thinking about suicide since about the age of four, when my mother reported that I first came to her and said, “Mom, I hate myself and want to kill myself.”

From that point on, suicidal ideation was a relatively constant companion. I wasn’t always depressed. I wasn’t always in the pits of despair. But it was a frequent occurrence. Sometimes the thoughts were more casual. “I wonder what it would be like to . . .” Sometimes it was more urgent—like finding my hands around my neck at age six until I passed out. As a pediatric sub-specialist, I would later learn that anyone who holds their breath too long will pass out, but later be fine. Sometimes, though, my suicidality was frightening, like when I was sixteen and drove at seventy-two miles an hour towards a telephone pole. This crash was averted by the paroxysmal squeak squeal woooooonk squeal of a police siren. I pulled over. The officer walked up. I broke down in tears while he talked to me about the dangers of unsafe driving. I was crying because what was unsafe was my own mind. The speed of my red Chevy Cavalier was not the issue. I realized then that I had to talk to my mom about it.

This led to my mom making a series of phone calls. My original visit was with a psychiatrist who gave me Zoloft. I woke up the next morning shaking on the floor, and for the next two weeks, I was an utter maniac. I later learned that this was an episode of mania. The reason I had all those suicidal thoughts and brutal paroxysmal lows? I had bipolar disorder.

Furthermore, my mother likely had those genetics on her family’s side. This was determined after virtually every family member was enrolled in a study at the National Institutes of Mental Health. We found that several members of my family had had some pretty significant problems with psychiatric illness, including my paternal grandfather, who died at the end of the noose when my father was five years old. I only recognized, on the day my father died, that he must’ve had post-traumatic stress disorder his whole life. His mother’s instructions were to cut my grandfather’s body down from the beam upon which his corpse was hanging. This beam was in the basement of the house where he grew up. Straight, still, stiff, quiet. He wouldn’t move out of that house until he went to college at age eighteen. Thinking back on this now, I realize that my dad spent a lot of time in the basement of the house in which I grew up, mostly drinking. Okay , always drinking.

This family history was my inheritance. My mom struggled. My older sister, Alison Muir, died a few years back of an opiate overdose on the floor of her bathroom. It was unclear whether this overdose was intentional or not. But it was surprising since she had already attempted suicide, leading to an ICU admission for a liver transplant. The liver transplant never ended up needing to happen. I was always pretty sure she was going to kill herself on purpose, not by accident. Suicide is a big deal. Of family secrets, it casts a remarkably long shadow. I will never know what happened at the end of my sister’s life. Trying to figure it out would only lead to misery.

I was lucky enough (which so many are not, I now recognize) to get help, and that was pretty good. My mom, Vita West Muir, who began her working life as a medical editor, has for the past forty-odd years worked as a music promoter and not-for-profit executive director. In her Rolodex, she’s got a bunch of jazz musicians. It’s a literal Rolodex. The kind with paper cards. One of those paper cards had the number of Denny Zeitlin, MD. I’ve never referred to him with the doctoral letters before now. In addition to being Dr. Zeitlin, Denny, as I knew him, is a remarkably gifted jazz piano player, and both my mother and I were devotees of the jazz piano trio as a musical format. Denny is among the most gifted players and band leaders in that format, but he’s also a faculty member at UCSF in the Department of Psychiatry. What do most people do when they have a psychiatric crisis in the family? They call the first person they know in their life whom they can talk to about it. If they’re especially lucky, it’s someone who has any idea at all what to do. Dr. Zeitlin picked up the phone from my mom because he had played at the jazz festival she ran.

By the time I was seventeen years old, Frank Ninavagi, MD at Yale, made the definitive diagnosis of bipolar disorder. By twenty, I was at the end of my junior year at Amherst College, and frankly, much of my time there had been a struggle. I had made a fortunate decision very early on never to drink or use drugs. I still haven’t had a drink or used a recreational drug. This is not the easiest decision for a teenager in his first few years of college. But, as the doc at the NIMH research study I was later enrolled in said, “That was a very good decision; you would be dead otherwise.” She may have been reinforcing positive health behaviors. I also think she was correct.

Towards the end of my junior year, I had already been forced to take two extensions on finals I couldn’t finish because of depression. It was tough to know what to do. I was taking medication every single day. But there was one key element left out of the medical process of informed consent: If you take medicine for a psychiatric illness, it should work . You should actually feel better . If it doesn’t work, you need to be prescribed a new one, or do a new thing, or something. I had taken medication for years, and frankly, a lot of it. It didn’t help . Or, at least, I was not a well college student. My episodes continued unabated. Though I had listened to the doctors about the risks of side effects, I didn’t have a sense of what it would feel like to get well until many years later. What "well" feels like was not addressed. This is not a trivial omission.

I wanted to die. I wanted it a lot. I thought about it all the time. I finally told my mom at twenty, and she told Dr. Zeitlin. His advice was practical: Find the best research hospital near you, find a study on the problem your kid has, and enroll your kid in that study. Pronto.

© Owen Muir, 2025, my house in CT…now.

I lived in Connecticut at the time, and the proximal hospital with a strong research program around bipolar disorder was Columbia University at the New York State Psychiatric Institute. There was a study running, led by Ramin Parsey, MD, that I was suitable for. I would get free treatment for six months as part of the study. And, as Dr. Zeitlin explained to my mom, “They’re going to refer Owen to someone good afterwards.” They’re not “going to let a study patient drop.”

I first read David Foster Wallace’s Infinite Jest while I was in that hospital. They actually hospitalized me for the study evaluation in the summer between my junior and senior year of college, the same year in his life that David Foster Wallace would later place the action of the fiction The Pale King . The eponymous semi-lead character in that book, David Wallace, took that year off to work at the IRS processing center in Peoria, Illinois. At that time in my life, Infinite Jest was a new book. It was long. And, it turns out that psychiatric hospitalization, particularly when you’re not actually that cripplingly ill at the time but instead need to get a series of tests done for a study, is boring. So, having a long book by an author like David Foster Wallace was a great way to pass the time. This was the year 1999. Notably, psychiatric hospitals, to this day, are more or less still in the Nineties. No phones, no computers, no internet. It’s just you and a book, and maybe other patients to talk to.

I didn’t know it then, of course, but I had treatment with some of the stars of the field, including receiving a lumbar puncture at age twenty from J. John Mann, MD. I subsequently received six months of care from Dr. Maria Oquendo, who was among the most prominent researchers in suicide and bipolar disorder. She ran the general psychiatry residency program at Columbia. She was the president of the American Psychiatric Association. It’s kind of bizarre to think that the person who came up with the suicide rating scale I was evaluated with was the same person I saw for that problem. I suspect that many of the patients I’ve seen, some of whom have since become mental health professionals and even MDs thereafter, think of me similarly.
