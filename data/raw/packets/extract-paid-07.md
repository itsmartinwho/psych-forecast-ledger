My dear friend Kelly Fiore M.D., pictured above, I have known since I was 18 She is featured in two of my podcast episodes!

And…this one…

However, my characterization of the celebration as having a causal relationship was inaccurate. It turns out, the family gathering I had been invited to was a wedding. It was a wedding between a Psychiatrist, and former supervisee of Dr. Fiore, and his new bride:

So the joyful celebration in question? It was highly correlated with the presence of the writer of an extremely niche Healthcare newsletter. The relationship was not causal. As I have covered previously, this type of error in inference is at the very heart of misunderstandings in health:

There was an unobserved variable that accounted for both the presence of this writer and dancing with joy. It was that the event was in fact a wedding. It was full of psychiatrists. This explains all of the above observed variables. It was an an inaccurate statement on my part to suggest that the joyous dancing was because of my presence.

Weddings feature dancing. People invite people they know to their weddings. Psychiatrist tends to know a lot of other psychiatrists. The joyous celebration was correlated, but not causal in nature as it relates to my presence.

I regret the error. I would also like to wish the happiest lives together to Dr. Mike and Anna Nakhla. Thank you for having me.

Causal inference in statistics ends up mattering a lot. Thank you for reading.

As a cutting-edge psychiatrist, I frequently am asked to comment on the ceremonial use of psychedelic compounds. I am proud to announce that I have nothing to say—as a doctor— about the spiritual use of psychedelics.

This is not because I have nothing to say about it. It's because, in my role as a physician, there are several things about which I have nothing to say. I get to talk about medical stuff as a doctor. Medicine is a quirky beast. The difference between assault with a deadly weapon and Medical care is the combination of 1. Licensure (which is regulated by states) 2. Standards (which are determined by doctors) 3. And ethics of practice (which are determined by doctors and are not the same as what is legal).

Many things happen in the world that are important to a doctor or medical practice. Drowning in the ocean: medically significant. It’s not medical care, however. I don't have much to say about the ocean as a doctor. I like to go swimming. That is not a regulated activity. You don't need a license to take a dip in the Atlantic. You don't need ethical standards to determine whether you can or cannot use flippers.

Once you have water in your lungs and our pulled out of the ocean by a lifeguard, you get anywhere near an ambulance or a Hospital; then, at that point, assuming you're in a state in which I am licensed to practice, I might have something to say about it.

Water was served at the dinner table. Similarly, I could comment. That's good for your health. You should probably drink that. That is a lifestyle thing. I couldn't prescribe water at dinner. I could recommend it, at best.

If salty water is in a plastic bag, and it is connected to some tubing, and that goes into your arm, and you're in a hospital, boom. We're talking about medicine. Now, the rate at which that water flows into your veins and its subsequent impact IS medical care. I have to write an order on a computer. There is a purchasing decision around saline will be available. There will be liability associated with too much or too little.

All of the above examples are water. And some amount of dissolved solute. The difference between tap water at dinner, water in the ocean, and IV fluids, despite all the water, is that some of them are medicine; some of them might have medical importance. Not all of them are medicine all of the time.

The context matters.

The same is true of psychedelics. I am not a shaman. I am an ordained minister. But my faith tradition does not include psychedelic medicine. And thus, I leave the stewardship of shamanism and shamanic practice to actual shamans.

Most of the human experience isn't therapy. It isn't medicine. And it shouldn't be. That would be shitty.

Anyone who has spent any time at a hospital will tell you that is not the place you want to spend most of your time. The goal of every doctor is to get you out of a hospital. We want you on the beach. We want you to have a good time with your family. We don't want to see you for medical care unless it's to prevent more harmful medical care.

People can have spiritual experiences. People can have fun experiences. You can have fun experiences at a church. You can have spiritual experiences under the North Dakota sky.

The vast majority of good things that are therapeutic even aren't therapy.

The same is true of compounds that can induce experiences in some contacts that can be spiritual. In other contexts, they can be fun. In other contexts, they can be profoundly healing and subject to vastly different regulatory frameworks.

The context matters. We need to respect that context as meaningfully different.

I had a patient (not really) named Jeremiah (not a real name), who was 14 years old (not a real age). He was in the hospital, but he hadn’t said anything to any of the doctors. He hadn’t said anything to me. He’d only talk to one person in the entire building, who was a member of the recreation staff, and would take the kids out for basketball. The particular person Jeremiah spoke to is pretty good with kids. But it’s limiting for me, as a doctor, to assess anyone based on what they’re telling someone who isn’t me, when you’re never around. However, in this situation, that’s what I had to work with.

Jeremiah swallowed sharp objects. Repeatedly. Jeremiah had single-handedly trained an entire generation of local gastroenterology fellows. Every other day, a sharp object, and God knows where these came from, would go down Jeremiah’s throat. This would require a trip to the operating room, he would go under sedation, and they would try to retrieve the sharp object before it would puncture his intestines or stomach, again.

At the time I was going to see Jeremiah, I had recorded a podcast. Since I knew he likely wouldn’t talk to me, instead, I had a conversation with myself in front of him. I played the podcast I had recorded, which was an interview with a colleague talking about her experience of depression. I listened to it, and then I had a conversation with the character, out loud.

“Lara didn’t tell anybody. She was suicidal for years. She was really struggling. When she finally told me about it, she hadn’t really told anyone before, because she had been afraid of what doctors would think. Now we’re both doctors, so maybe that’s why she finally told me."

"I wonder if this is the situation you’re in, Jeremiah? I wonder if you’re worried to tell me what’s going on that’s really getting to you. I worry about you swallowing that stuff. I don’t know why it’s happening. It’s really hard for me to not know.”

Jeremiah gave me a little smile, then spoke to me for the first time: “Thanks. I’ll see you tomorrow.“

But tomorrow came, and I didn’t see him. He was in the medical hospital again for swallowing a sharp object. Two days later, I did see him again. I was called to the unit while he was screaming. His limbs were flailing, and he was clawing at the walls. A staff member was trying to hold his hands from clawing something, anything, off the walls.

“Jeremiah, I don’t get it, what’s wrong?!” I said as I walked up to the situation.

“They’re trying to stop me.”

“They have to stop you.”

“Swallowing things makes me feel better!!”

“Wait, so we’re trying to stop the only thing that makes you feel better?”

“Yes!!!!”

“Wow, that’s totally messed up. No wonder you hate this hospital. Here we are, forced to stop you from doing the only thing that helps you feel better?”

“Yes!!!! Yes!!!!”

“This is an awful problem to have. We can’t let you swallow things. But we’re getting in the way of something that makes you feel better. No wonder you hate it here.” I said. He calmed down a little bit. And then a little bit more.

The next day we had a full conversation, over a meal; a meal that included no sharp objects. He didn’t swallow sharp anything for the next week. He didn’t swallow anything the next month. He was discharged a month and a half later. It has been over a year since he was in the hospital.

He wasn’t expecting me to just acknowledge where he was at. Sometimes the acknowledgment of what’s happening, right now, is the most surprising thing you can say. It can really get people out of their own heads in a way that you can’t do when you’re just trying to stop them from doing something crazy. The reason people do things that are outrageous is because they don’t feel understood. When they feel understood they stop. When you try understand something that seems nonsensical to you, you create a shared connection that is desperately needed. You mentalize– to imagine their mind, however imperfectly, and then check if you got it right–and they feel like you put in the effort to understand them.

Having conversations out loud lets people understand what’s in your mind. This is without people having to wonder or worry. It's the strategy that I'm teaching in this book. Because it takes the pressure off. In kids like Jeremiah, it’s too dark in their own mind to imagine someone else might care.

A while back, I wrote a guide for coworkers to help them understand “what the heck I was talking about.” What follows is that guide, with some light rewrites, I hope it proves useful.

Sound Smart When Discussing Research with Your Friends

Here are the terms used to describe research studies and what they mean.

Efficacy: Does the treatment work in a select group of people? (Almost all treatments are approved based on efficacy only).

Effectiveness: Does the treatment work in the real world?

Statistical Significance: Statistical significance refers to the likelihood that the difference in the data (like differences between groups or relationships between variables) is not due to chance. This is often evaluated using a p-value. If the p-value is less than a predetermined threshold (often 0.05), the result is declared statistically significant. However, statistical significance doesn't tell us anything about the magnitude or importance of the difference or relationship. A small, unimportant difference can be statistically significant if the sample size is large enough.

Effect Size: Effect size, on the other hand, is a measure of the magnitude of the difference or relationship. Unlike statistical significance, it is not influenced by sample size. Effect sizes can be very helpful in understanding the practical or clinical importance of research findings. For instance, a medication might significantly reduce symptom severity compared to a placebo (statistical significance), but if the reduction is very small (small effect size), it might not make a meaningful difference in patients' lives.

In short, statistical significance addresses the question, "Is it likely that there's a true effect or difference?" while effect size addresses the question, "How big or important is the effect or difference?" It's important to consider both when interpreting research findings. The Muir-Skee Lo Emotionally Corrective Equation was built to solve this problem for general audiences.

Placebo vs. Sham: A placebo is an inactive substance or treatment that looks just like, and is given in the same way as an active drug or treatment being tested. The effects of the active drug or treatment are compared to the effects of the placebo. On the other hand, a sham is a procedure or treatment that is intended to mimic as closely as possible an intervention but does not confer any real treatment. It is generally used in clinical trials for devices and surgical interventions.

FDA Approval vs. Clearance: FDA approval is required for new drugs and new, or “de-novo, medical devices. This process involves rigorous testing and review to ensure safety and effectiveness.

FDA clearance, on the other hand, is a lower-level review process that is used for medical devices that are considered to be a lower risk or that are substantially similar to a device that is already legally on the market.

opinion corner:

This makes Medical Devices practically less expensive to bring to the market as they require fewer studies to get clearance.

Randomized Trials: Randomized trials, also known as randomized controlled trials, are considered a gold standard in clinical research. In these trials, participants are randomly assigned to either the treatment group or the control group. This random allocation helps to eliminate bias and ensures that any differences seen in outcomes are likely due to the treatment itself and not other factors.

Open Label: An open-label trial is a type of study in which both the researchers and participants know which treatment is being administered. This is in contrast to a blinded study, where the participants and/or researchers do not know what treatment is being given.

Single Blind: In a single-blind study, the participants do not know whether they are receiving the active treatment or the placebo, but the researchers do know.

Double Blind: In a double-blind study, neither the participants nor the researchers know who is getting the active treatment and who is getting the placebo.

Cross-Over: A crossover study is one where participants receive a sequence of different treatments (or a treatment and a placebo). This is different from a parallel study, where participants receive only one treatment.

Kaplan-Meier: The Kaplan-Meier method is a statistical tool used to estimate the survival or longevity rates of a population in the face of varying conditions.

Case-Control Study: A case-control study is a type of observational study in which two existing groups differing in outcome are identified and compared on the basis of some supposed causal attribute.

Case Report/Case Series: A case report is a detailed report of the symptoms, signs, diagnosis, treatment, and follow-up of an individual patient. A case series follows than one case.

Prospective vs. Retrospective Studies: In a prospective study, individuals are followed over time, and data about them is collected as their characteristics or circumstances change. Birth cohort studies are a good example. Retrospective studies look backward and examine exposures to suspected risk or protection factors in relation to an outcome that is established at the start of the study.

Withdrawal Designs: A withdrawal design, also known as a reversal design, refers to a research design involving the removal of a treatment to note whether it has been effective. Researchers implement the treatment, withdraw it to see if a problem returns, then reimplement it to see if the problem decreases again. It's often used in behavioral studies.

Lamotrigine in bipolar depression was approved with this kind of study design, For example.

Within-Subjects Design: In a within-subjects design, each participant serves as their own control, reducing the impact of individual differences and external variables. Because each participant undergoes every experimental condition, differences in results can be more confidently attributed to the different conditions rather than individual characteristics. This increases statistical power.

Downsides include carryover effects (where exposure to one condition affects responses to subsequent conditions) and order effects (where the order of conditions affects results), which need to be considered and controlled where possible.

Blinding: Blinding exists to minimize bias. In blinded studies, participants, and sometimes even researchers, don't know which intervention is being given. Blinding can be single (where either the participant or the researcher doesn't know the treatment being administered) or double (where both the participant and the researcher are unaware of the treatment being administered).

Sham Control in a Device Study: Sham control in a device study serves the same purpose as a placebo in a drug trial—it acts as a control. The sham device looks and feels like the real device but doesn't have the active components or elements that are believed to cause the effect. For example, in a study testing the efficacy of a new type of electrical stimulation device, the sham control device might look identical and produce a sensation but not deliver any actual electrical stimulation.

Opinion Corner:

What Makes a Good Sham: A good sham control should be indistinguishable from the actual treatment of the study participant. It should mimic the actual treatment as closely as possible in all respects except for the active component. Importantly, it should not do anything itself - it shouldn't inadvertently provide a benefit or harm.

Placebo Control: Placebo control is an inactive substance or procedure that appears identical to the actual treatment but doesn't have a therapeutic effect. It's used in clinical trials to determine the effectiveness of a new treatment by comparing outcomes in participants who received the treatment vs. those who received the placebo.

Opinion Corner:

What Makes a Good Placebo: A good placebo should look, taste, smell, and be administered the same way as the active treatment. The participant shouldn't be able to distinguish between the placebo and the active treatment. Just like a good sham, a good placebo should not have an effect itself. This is obviously not always possible!

Breaking the Blind/Unblinding: Breaking the blind, or unblinding, is revealing which participants received the active treatment and which received the placebo or sham. This is typically done after the study is completed and data are ready for analysis. Unblinding can be partial (where only certain team members get access to the treatment assignments) or complete. It's generally avoided until necessary because it can introduce bias.

Sometimes people can figure out which experimental group they are in all on their own. Because it’s very obvious (imagine blinding parachutes vs sham parachutes).

Mirror Image Studies: Mirror image studies are a type of study design where the same group of patients serves as its own control. For example, the study might compare a patient's outcomes during a period of time before treatment to the same patient's outcomes during a period of time after treatment. This type of design can be useful when it's difficult to find a suitable control group.

Mirror image studies can be useful when external control groups are hard to find or may not be comparable.

Non-inferiority: A non-inferiority study, also known as a non-inferiority trial, is a type of clinical trial in which the goal is to demonstrate that a new treatment or intervention is not substantially worse than an existing, or "standard," treatment or intervention.

Non-inferiority trials are typically used in situations where it would be unethical to have a control group receive a placebo. For instance, if an effective treatment already exists, it would not be appropriate to withhold that treatment from some participants in the trial. Instead, the new treatment is compared directly to the standard treatment.

The term "non-inferiority" refers to the fact that the goal is not necessarily to show that the new treatment is better. Rather, it is to demonstrate that it is not significantly worse.

In order to claim non-inferiority, the trial must specify a non-inferiority margin. This is the smallest difference between the effectiveness of the new treatment and the standard treatment that would still be considered acceptable. The non-inferiority margin is a critical aspect of the trial's design and must be carefully chosen based on clinical judgment and statistical reasoning.

Randomization: A method to determine causation, not just correlation.

Reliability: Do evaluators agree they're looking at the same thing? (Calling purple “Jeff” and asking 100 people to say Jeff when they see purple would be reliable).

Validity: Does the assessment represent some real underlying construct or biology? (Jeff isn’t the same as actual Purple, even if everyone is willing to say it).

Assay Sensitivity: This is about finding out if a trial failed because it was conducted improperly or if the drug doesn't work. This question can be answered by comparing the results of a new drug and an established drug against a placebo.

P-values: The infamous p<0.05 is considered as the threshold for a statistically significant result. However, it merely indicates a 5% chance that the difference seen is due to random variation. In multiple comparisons, the p-value needs to be adjusted downwards to avoid erroneous results – a practice often known as "p-hacking".

Inter-rater Reliability: Symbolized by the Greek kappa, this measure tells us the likelihood that two experts agree on a diagnosis. A kappa of 1 indicates perfect agreement, while 0.5 is no better than chance.

Statistical Powers That Be

This is the probability that a statistical test will correctly reject the null hypothesis when the alternative hypothesis is true. In other words, it's the likelihood that if there really is an effect or difference, your study will be able to detect it.

The power of a statistical test is influenced by several factors:

• Sample size: Larger sample sizes generally increase the power of a study because they reduce the impact of random variation and increase the likelihood of detecting a true effect.

• Effect size: Effect size is the magnitude of the difference or relationship you are testing for. Larger effect sizes are easier to detect, so they increase statistical power.

• Significance level (Alpha): This is the threshold for determining when we “reject the null hypothesis.”— that is any difference seen is assumed to be due to chance. A common choice is 0.05. If multiple comparisons are done, this level needs to be adjusted.

• Variability in the data: More variability in the data can make it harder to detect a true effect, reducing power.

If a study is underpowered, it may fail to detect a real effect. On the other hand, overpowered studies may detect statistically significant effects that are not practically or clinically meaningful.

Your Mental Health Alphabet Soup:

• MDD: Major Depressive Disorder

• GAD: Generalized Anxiety Disorder

• OCD: Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder

Things that need a bit more detail

1. MDD (Major Depressive Disorder): MDD affects roughly 25-30% of people at some point. Unfortunately, it's rarely diagnosed accurately. It increases general medical spending by 44%, according to the Validation Institute. The inter-rater reliability for this condition is the lowest of any diagnosis, only slightly better than a coin flip.

2. TRD (Treatment-Resistant Depression): This is the tricky part - most depression can be classified as TRD. Studies show only 30% of MDD cases recover with the first tried drug, and each successive trial only results in about 10% remission. TRD is “great to study” as placebo response rates drop to zero as the number of failed oral meds increases.

3. OCD (Obsessive Compulsive Disorder): With a 2-6% population prevalence, OCD is a common condition and often resistant to treatment, this has similar benefits for study design to TRD—low chance of placebo response makes for easier study design.

FDA and Trials:

The FDA's process of approving treatments is an essential part of mental health research. Here's what you need to know:

Pre-clinical: The computer modeling and animal trial stage, where many potential treatments fail to progress.

Phase 1: Small-scale trials to determine if a new treatment is safe in healthy volunteers.

Phase 2: The stage where the dose window is determined, and efficacy is measured—a little— in a modest number of humans.

Phase 3: Large-scale human trials against a placebo or sham. Two successful trials at this stage are required for FDA approval. One is required for clearance if there is a "similar” device with De-novo approval.

Phase 4: Post-marketing data collection - what happens when the treatment is given to many people?

Treatments can either be FDA-approved (which requires two successful randomized controlled trials) or FDA-cleared (which requires one successful randomized controlled trial and proves the device is similar to prior devices).

Code-Switching

DSM-5 TR: Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 5th edition, "text revision.” It's a research manual but often used in clinical practice, despite its flaws. This manual costs about $25 million to revise. The APA writes the DSM. It owns the DSM. From a financial standpoint, the APA is a publisher, not a professional society, by a factor of a lot. If you include the massive financial investments managed by APA and the $45m HQ they own, the APA is really a publisher and money management organization. These financials are public—you only need to ask. This is not to impugn the work they do for either the DSM or Professionals. It is to say financials are important to consider.

ICD (International Classification of Diseases): This is a standard diagnostic tool for epidemiology, health management, and clinical purposes. It is used to classify diseases and other health problems recorded on many types of health and vital records. The current version in use in the US is ICD-10-CM, while the rest of the world has transitioned to ICD-11.

F-codes: ICD-10 codes for mental health diagnoses start with "F."

CPT (Current Procedural Terminology): These codes are used to represent services and procedures performed by physicians and other healthcare professionals. They are developed and maintained by the American Medical Association and are a branded product of this organization. It is “what you do” based.

HCPCS (Healthcare Common Procedure Coding System): This set of healthcare procedure codes based on the CPT. Level II codes are used for non-physician services like ambulance rides, durable medical equipment, and certain drugs and medicines.

SNOMED CT (Systematized Nomenclature of Medicine—Clinical Terms): This is the a comprehensive and precise clinical health terminology product globally. It's used for electronic health records and enables the consistent, processable representation of clinical content in healthcare systems. It supports a wider range of healthcare activities, including analytics, research, and policy development.

LOINC (Logical Observation Identifiers Names and Codes): LOINC is used to code many of the non-procedure parts of a patient's medical record, like lab test results and clinical observations.

NDC (National Drug Codes): These are unique product identifiers for human drugs in the United States, including over-the-counter medications, prescription medications, and some medical devices.

DRG (Diagnosis-Related Group): DRGs are used primarily for billing and reimbursement in hospital settings. Each DRG represents a "package" of services provided during a hospital stay, and hospitals are reimbursed a fixed amount for each DRG.

However, billing procedures may distort the reliability of diagnosis data - only specific diagnoses are covered, influencing which one is charted.

I was a serious enough photographer for a long time. I like using “large format” cameras. Particularly, I was really drawn to the use of 4 x 5” cameras, and for the unfamiliar, these are cameras were the film is 4 x 5" --that's a big piece of film! It leads to really compelling photographs. I also like the process. It's difficult.

Subscribe now

These are the cameras that you see an old pictures with a bellows, and a hood, and the person standing behind it. Traditionally, there is a lens in the front, and a film plate in the back.

I fell in love with the use of Polaroid positive/negative film: you take a picture, you get a Polaroid positive. You also get a negative you can print from later. This seems like the best possible world to me--- you didn't have to wait to know what your picture look like, and you didn't have to take another picture with different film after you took the first one with a Polaroid.

However, Polaroid positive negative film had a disadvantage: it was very slow (Iso 25 — for photography nerds). This means you need more light per unit time to get a picture, and so if people move, things get blurry. Generally, this means a very good lens, that took very quick pictures with a wide aperture to let in more light!

I had a camera, it went in a huge box. It was heavy. It required a tripod. It required a special back to put the film in, it required an expensive lens, everything about it was clumsy and heavy.

So if you're taking this camera somewhere, you're really leaning in to how hard it's gonna be to take a picture, because you wanna get it just right.

I took this camera up with my family to my aunt's house, in upstate New York.

I wanted to take a picture of my dad. I took the camera out of the case, and unpacked everything that came with it. One thing was missing. The lens.

I could've given up and not taken any pictures at all. But I decided, on the spur of the moment, to do some thing that was even more archaic then everything in that box already: I improvised…based on the history of photography.

A lens on a camera is a way to get more light in in a shorter period of time. You don't actually need them. You can just use a tiny pinhole.

Is exacerbates the problem of having slow film! You don't get to know what you're taking a picture of, because it takes so long to take the picture, you only get to see it after it's already done. No adjustments. No framing anything up. No micromanaging. Dad had to sit very still.

On the upside, it doesn't require any lens at all. So I took the front of the camera, and I took a pan for my aunt's kitchen. I took a pin. And I put it through the tin pan, making circles till I poked a tiny hole. I taped that on the front of the camera. I then put a piece of tape over the pinhole. That was my shutter.

This photograph took 4 1/2 minutes. It's the last photograph I took before he died.
