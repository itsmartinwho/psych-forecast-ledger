Yesterday, in my almost-only one-human-penned leading health-parody-regulatory-enthusiast-newsletter, I wrote about potential FDA Issues related to psychedelic drug development and advertising.

And today, as if magic, the FDA has released new Guidance on Psychedelic Drug Development.

And, readers, get excited…it features…

An Open Comment Period!

You can read the draft guidance document here.

The irony is that I edited this picture together featuring myself (left), noted Research Scientist Nolan R. Williams, M.D., and Psychedelic Anonomyous Characters together from other images in which we were together in Brooklyn for real— but were insufficiently “trippy.”

Bangers only might as well be the FDA guidance for FDA Guidance. Questions like these new clinical trials being considered for a medicine from the Plant Gods? Consider if they are made of:

Mplant material, algae, macroscopic fungi, or a combination of these, the investigational product may be considered a botanical, as that term is defined in the guidance for industry Botanical Drug Development (December 2016).

The firey guidance continues…you are not going to believe how off the hook the FDA is willing to be:

Although current psychedelic drug development programs are exploring single-dose or intermittent-dose treatment paradigms, most of the conditions being studied to date in these programs are chronic. Non-clinical studies to support chronic or chronic-intermittent dosing should be provided if the treatment effect is not durable and repeat dosing is expected. Sponsors should determine the most appropriate dosing paradigm (e.g., dosing intervals) for each animal species in the repeat-dose toxicity studies to support their intended clinical studies.

The chronic! Conditions... Who knew that Dr. Dre was telling the truth about being “in the lab with a pen and a pad” and that the “damn label” he was attempting to complete work on was a pharmaceutical label for a de novo approval?

It just gets more exciting from there. Check out this regulatory guidance on cardiac adverse effects monitoring:

Long-term exposure to 5-HT2B agonists may induce cardiac valve stiffening. Currently, FDA recommends that sponsors exclude subjects with preexisting valvulopathy or pulmonary hypertension from multiple-dose studies of drugs with this mechanism until this risk can be better characterized.

This is just prudent, yo. Given that we might have only one shot, and one opportunity to comply with every regulation we ever wanted, it seems trial design will need to embrace the most robust safety standards. This is not without its costs.

The most critical issue, at least to my eye, from a scalability perspective, is that for the clinical trials, the FDA is—in draft guidance— leaning both having two monitors1 across the board—one with a full independent license— and:

Observation by two monitors for the duration of the treatment session

A healthcare provider with graduate-level professional training and clinical experience in psychotherapy, licensed to practice independently, serving as the lead monitor. Examples of such professional credentials include the following:

• Clinical or counseling psychologist (Ph.D. or Psy.D.)

• Psychiatrist or another physician (MD or D.O.)

• Master of Social Work (MSW)

• Licensed Clinical Professional Counselor (LCPC)

• Licensed Marriage and Family Therapist (LMFT)

• Psychiatric Nurse Practitioner (Psychiatric NP).

Shaman-trained individuals must obtain full licensure as M.D., D.O., MSW, LCPC, LMFT, or N.P., or obtain a BA and have somehow completed a prior year of clinical work in a mental health setting. I expect open comments to be left on this requirement. I’m kidding a little bit, but not that much. Medical Chaplaincy exists as a designation in the VA system, for example.

Much like the FDA Guidance, I consider this newsletter on that…

Contains Nonbinding Recommendations

When Dorothy awoke, the sun was shining through the trees, and Toto had long been out chasing birds around him and squirrels. She sat up and looked around her. The Founder, still standing impatiently in his corner, banging out emails on his phone to generate more traction, waiting for her.

"We must go and search for water," she told him.

"Why do you want water?" he asked.

"To wash my face. It has to be clean after the dust of the road, and I have to drink, so the dry bread will not stick in my throat."

"It must be inconvenient not to have that problem solved for you by a disruptive health technology innovator," said the Founder thoughtfully, "for you must sleep, eat and drink, all without the difficulties in those processes having been disrupted with platform solutions. They left the cottage and walked through the trees until they found a little spring of clear water, where Dorothy drank and bathed and ate her breakfast.

I have never included content or “trigger” warnings in my writing because I don’t think trigger warnings are a good idea1 writ large. This piece will address child abuse. It is an exception to the rule. Do not read any further if this topic is too disturbing.

Before I knew what love was, I thought I was in love.

I was 16 years old. I had been in love once previously.2 Ok, twice.3

I learned what it was to love and what it was to hurt at the same time. It was under the thrall of confusing feelings about the same person. This is the story of how I sorted out, after many years, what happened to my heart when I fell in love with Catherine.

I'm going to use a pseudonym for Catherine. Her name is not Catherine. I know a number of Catherines. It's very hard to disambiguate Catherines—we have the C vs. K dichotomy, an errant terminal “y,” and little else. This attempted anonymity? It is as quixotic as it is pointless.

Catherine was a high school classmate. I remember when her “being beautiful” mattered for fuck all. I have worked with brutally traumatized people for thousands of hours as a psychiatrist. I learned the price of beauty, especially when one is a young woman, is often awful.

Beautiful women, depending on their disposition, often lead lives bizarre. From my perspective, for those marked with remarkable symmetry, beautiful is a psychopathology trap for deeply unwell and predatory men. Beautiful women, particularly when young, are given the kind of attention reserved for celebrities— but at least celebrities know why someone's gawking at them.

Catherine was beautiful. In her case, however, the predator was not a man. It was her stepmother.

Sexual abuse of children by mothers or stepmothers is rare. This is a shockingly rare thing to happen. This is not to say women do not do awful things to children. It is not to deny mothers can do awful things to children. The existence of medical child abuse, for example, is almost entirely a crime of mothers. The kinds of brutality visited upon children by deeply unwell often narcissistic, obsessive, self-absorbed, hideous moms are myriad. Other kinds of child abuse, particularly sexual abuse of children, are much more often perpetrated by men.

Adults abuse children more often than we would like. Uncommon perpetrators, however, don't mean it doesn't happen. Is it a clue that there is something even sicker and more uncommonly foul afoot in the hearts of the uncommon predators?

Most adults do not abuse children. Many children are abused. The ways in which children are abused are horrifying to consider. The ability to prevent the abuse of children requires serious people to think about the ways in which this happens in order to prevent it. I choose to seriously address the abuse of children so that it can be prevented, which includes rare cases with rare perpetrators.

I had to go through a process to figure out what the hell to do—at 16 years old—about being told the person I was in love with, who was beautiful, was hurt by an predatory woman. The woman was her stepmother. An irony of this love story? The adult, Rick, who supported me? He was a pedophile, also, I learned years later. He never did anything inappropriate with me.4 He was the most supportive teacher I had in high school.5

I talked to Rick, my official advisor, in very general terms, about the fact that I was feeling troubled. His support was gracious and nuanced.

Catherine had a secret.

I realized now, at this moment in the writing of the first draft, that I can’t write this story and keep that secret. All high school classmates could identify Catherine. It’s a trap…and that moment of horror I have preserved for you, like a body outlined for ages in ash in the flash that ended Pompeii.

All our high school classmates and educators know. There is nothing I can do to stop that. It already happened.6

I had strong feelings for Catherine. I let them sit, in my chest, for months. A year. A little more. I was under the impression that keeping things secret was a good idea. Later, I would learn, as a psychiatrist, that secrets are toxic. They drive you up the fucking wall.7

Catherine was a musician. I am a terrible musician. I wish—every day, still—I was a better one. She was a piano player, and singer. She would practice a lot, and inspired me to practice more. The Rachmaninov passage…little lilting error, stop, pause for one and…, repeat. Even her fuck ups were done in the rhythms of the piece.

I would sit on the floor. We had small practice rooms, in my high school, which were a rare private areas for students. They were accessible only by musicians. Many people—who were not myself or Catherine—would have their first awkward sexual experiences in those rooms. She was as gracious with the keys with others, disconnected and eager, as she was with her time together with me.

Catherine would practice piano and she would sing. I would sit on the floor. Self-conscious—was I a creepy unwanted person? I would just sit there, quietly, longing. I didn't know the difference between being want-able for the purpose you wished and being needed. I was needed for Catherine. In retrospect, I wasn't wanted. I was never going to be what she wanted.8

I listened, and I did a good enough job. I was a good friend—bullshit. Honestly, I was too wrapped up in my mind to be able to tell. This was the age when my “depression” really started showing itself as bipolar disorder. In the coming weeks, I would have my first manic episode. The pressure was on, in my brain. I thought, because I did not know any better, that I was in love.

One day, Catherine turned her body, sharply, by 90°, on the piano bench. It was kind of smooth rotation possible with a low friction black skirt on a piano bench. The shirt she was wearing was Silver. Silky. It was probably made of rayon or polyester?

“I haven't told anyone else this,” she said.

“What is it?” I replied. Her tone was cold.

“From the age of six through the age of 12, I had a sexual relationship with my stepmother.”

“Oh…” I replied. I don't know that I've ever felt so sick to my stomach, or that I ever will again.

“And then she stopped. I don't know why she stopped. She never told me why. She is still with my dad. He doesn't know. It is why I am at boarding school. I can't stand to be around her,” she said with all the emotion of someone in their thousandth deposition.

“Oh, God,” is all I can remember responding.

Victor Frankenstein and His Monster: Misery Hates Company

Victor Frankenstein and the monster he creates share many qualities. Both of them are eloquent and are exquisitely sensitive to their emotions. Most importantly however, they are both miserable bastards who spend a substantial portion of the story bemoaning their fates.

As soon as Victor creates the monster he begins to regret it. The monster was so horrible that “[he] passed the night wretchedly” (p. 43). It was the first of many. His joy on finding the monster gone from his home is short lived however. For when he returns home and finds his younger brother murdered and Justine put on trial for the crime, Victor is correctly convinced that the monster most have done it. He states that, “During the whole of this wretched mockery of justice, I suffered a living torture” (p. 66). Little did Victor know this torture would continue unabated for the rest of his life, indeed he spends the first night in a state of “unmingled wretchedness,” (p. 70) which the reader can’t help but note is beginning to become quite a theme.

The beginning of the monsters life, on the other hand, was filled with hope. As he learned to read he had many revelations, for example from Plutarch he was “elevated…above the wretched sphere of [his] own reflections” (p. 113). And although he was from the get go just as wretched as his creator, from these books he “felt the greatest ardour for virtue rise within me, and [he also felt] abhorrence for vice” (p. 114). This appreciation for virtue lasted just as long as his hope for friendship for his human neighbors did, which, in all fairness, wasn’t very long at all. As soon as his neighbors see him they beat him with sticks, and the monster forgot all about his distain for vice and “I, like the arch-fiend, bore a hell within me, and finding myself unsympathized with, wish to tear up the trees, spread havoc and destruction around me, and then to have sat down and enjoyed the ruin” (p. 121). This is exactly what he decided to do and, “[bending his] mind towards injury and death,” (p. 123), he torched the house of his erstwhile “friends” to the ground. Then, in his first fateful step on the road to the misery-fest that is his relationship with Victor, he meets and proceeds to murder Victor’s younger brother: “I gazed on my victim and my heart swelled with exultation and hellish triumph; I exclaimed I too can create desolation; my enemy is not invulnerable; this death will carry despair to him and a thousand other miseries shall torment and destroy him” (p. 127). And thus it began.

The monster and Victor subsequently meet in the mountains of Switzerland, and in his persuasive eloquence the monster convinced Victor to make him a bride. Upon accepting this task Victor begins in earnest to be held hostage by his delicate constitution: “Every thought that was devoted to [the creation] was an extreme anguish, and every word that I spoke in allusion to it caused my lips to quiver, and my heart to palpitate” (p. 144). It is at this point however that the distinction between Victor and his monster becomes clear. Upon the monster’s arrival Victor realizes the folly in his course of action: “I thought with a sensation of madness on my promise of creating another like to him, and trembling with passion, tore to pieces on which I was engaged” (p. 151). Unlike his monster, Victor, when confronted with unpleasantness decides to do the right thing, and not take the easy way out by granting the monster’s wish for fear that yet another monster would be a horrible thing to unleash on the world. The monster, in contrast, reacts to this unpleasant (for him) turn of events by promising to ruin Victor’s life, which he promptly does just a few pages later with the murder of Victor’s friend, Clerval. Victor, predictably by this point, manages to get yet more miserable when confronted with the monster’s latest murder: “The human frame could no longer support the agonies that I endured, and I was carried out of the room in strong convulsions” (p. 161). And even as he recovers from his “sickness,” he “was absorbed by a gloomy and black melancholy that nothing could dissipate” (p. 165). For good measure, Shelley lays it on with a trowel, and describes Victor as, among many similar things, “a shattered wreck-the shadow of a human being. My strength was gone. I was a mere skeleton, and fever night and day prayed upon my wasted frame” (p. 167). Even in his never-ending wretchedness Victor again decides to do the right thing and he “formed in [his] own heart a resolution to pursue my destroyer to death” (p.182). In yet another parallel between Victor and the monster, Victor finds himself taken “by a maddening rage” (p. 182), which is also clearly the monster’s favorite emotional response.

Curb Your Enthusiasm for Large Language Models
What Larry David could have taught AI developers
OWEN SCOTT MUIR, M.D.
AUG 29, 2023
∙ PAID
8
3

The under performance of large language models was predictable. An issue with large language models is that what we say, on the Internet, has an audience of other humans. Or at least had an audience of other humans.

The language was generated by one group of humans, and intended for other humans. There is an assumption built into our pre-ChatGPT communication: human minds will be interpreting what we read.

When humans are communicating with other humans, we have two methods with which to communicate. The first is the explicit words we use. The second is the implicit models of the minds of our readers, and their presumed reaction to the words.

We're almost always calculating these two things at once. Humans think about whatever we said, and whatever we meant. There is some assumption of plausibility of error built into the assumed understanding of others. Error is an understood risk to both the writer and the reader. This uncertainty forms the substrate of our communication. Humans get that other humans might not get it.

That is not what I meant!

Sometimes we use this for humor, sometimes we use that as a scam, sometimes we fret about whether misunderstanding will be the case, but the intentions behind the writing? This is crucially important for our understanding of any words. The language alone is insufficient.

Artificial intelligence decided to, more or less, ignore this.

They “scraped” all of our language, and built the predictive models about what word comes next.

These machines, built on words without intentions, are exceptionally good at lies. And fabrications, of course. Much of what we mean isn't in the words that we use. It's in our assumptions of the minds of others. Those are not on the printed page! There is an entire layer of “metadata,” which is deeply human, but implicit, and not written. The assumption behind extremely large data sets for large language models is that they were just figure it out. But they didn’t.

This metadata—what we meant—is absent. The robots didn't have access to it. So people spent billions of dollars scraping what we said, and ignoring the fact that what we meant was a crucial component of whatever we wrote in the first place.

I am going to use Larry David, and a conversation he had with his TV therapist, on the show Curb Your Enthusiasm, to make the point:

Therapist: “What is interesting to me is that I find you're focused on the number of women who apparently are very interested in you. And yet, Larry, you seem preoccupied.”

Larry: “I'm just wondering, have you ever noticed the disparity in these two chairs? Your chair is so much better than this one. This is not a comfortable chair at all. Did you buy these chairs?”

[if I was a Therapy supervisor, And this was real transcript from a session, I would have very different guidance for Junior therapists than I would if I understood the above was from a TV show which was making fun of psychotherapy.

The context really matters here, because either this is a session doing what sessions do, or it's an extremely well done satire of the process of psychotherapy, but those are opposite understandings! Robots just scraping the text would not know the difference. To laugh, or to cringe…]

Therapist: “ Yes, I bought both of them.”

Larry: “You picked out both of them out”

Therapist: “yeah.”

Larry: “What was going through your head when you picked out this chair? You picked out a great chair for yourself. That's a gorgeous chair. I would have that in my house. But this one, it's not comfortable. I'm constantly shifting from side to side. You have the comfortable chair. You have taken the good chair.”

[if this was a therapy session, I would be directing the trainee reviewing the video with me: “Larry may have some unconscious processes about the process of therapy. He is upset with what's happening in the session, and instead of focusing on the content of his life and the content of the psychotherapy, he's decided to focus on the chairs, their inequity, and attacked the therapist about them.”

There are a whole series of interactions I would recommend as a therapist. However, if the context is actually a scripted TV show, or even an improvised TV show, where the very process of psychotherapy is being satirized, there's really nothing I would change about it because it's spot on. It's really funny. It's either a predictively sad psychotherapy session where somebody is struggling with narcissism and not able to directly confront their issues…or is the TV show making fun of that very thing, in a way that I find quite helpful. And so I either have a recommendation, or no recommendation, depending on the context.]

Therapist: “That's interesting. You are the... The first and only patient who has ever said anything negative about that chair.”

Larry: “I don't find that interesting at all.”

[The actual context here is this is a joke. The audiences in on it, the characters are not.]

Therapist: “I do, and that's my job, to find things interesting. Interesting… with a chair.”

[this is the only part of this transcript that is so clearly written for TV that I think it stands out as not necessarily realistic therapy.

Larry, as a writer, breaks character a little bit in going this far, but it's also just hilarious. Again, if you're training a large language model, you're going to miss the fact that this is a joke, because it's not obviously different from the text itself.]

Larry: “I don't know what you're writing over there.”

Therapist: “Chair issues. I think there's something deeper.”

[Also really tongue-in-cheek satire, but plausible in some psychoanalytic contexts, which is what makes it funny]

Larry: “I don't think you're writing anything.”

Therapist: “I could show you.”

[I know this is a comedy TV show, but this is almost perfect example of what we call teleological mode, in mentalization-based Treatment psychotherapy. ]

Larry: “Huh?”

Therapist: “ I could show you.”

Larry: “You're a nice man, but do you do that much between you and me?”

Therapist: “Don't you think that I do a significant amount of help to you?”

Larry: “May I be honest with you?”

Therapist: “I hope that you can always be honest with me.”

Larry: “I'm often never honest with you…”

“I am often never honest with you” might as well be the unofficial slogan of LLMs today.
