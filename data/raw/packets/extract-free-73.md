It was the best of times, it was cooked times. It was the middle of October— yes, back when October used to be a cold month! That orange color we use now? That was a reference to tree leaves, which used to change color. There was also a thing called pumpkins, which were the inspiration for the latte flavors we know now. They were actual fruits, I think? They had seeds. I don't know, maybe they're vegetables. Regardless, they were orange.

Orange was also the color that came before red, the color that let us know there was an outage of our Internet service services. Oh my, even the substack articles had to be scheduled…wait for it…by HAND! The Amazon Web Services had gone down .

Heavens!

[*the audience gasps audibly*]

Calm down, gather around, I'm telling a story.

In the before times, we had an Internet that ran, largely, off of three separate clouds. There was one managed by Microsoft, another by Google, in a third by what was called Amazon web services. So if you remember me telling you about books before? Well, Amazon began as a bookstore. It was a place, the Internet, where you could login, and actually have a physical book delivered to your home. This was considered revolutionary at the time. No, that's not the scary part of the story. Young people today, you don't know when the scary parts's about start! You're always jumping the shark. Yes, what we now referred to as our pets.

[*sharks splashing in the salt water tank right next to me, with laser headbands attached*]

No, it wasn’t prime day. Maybe that was the problem? The story, yes the story. The morning was dark, at 5 am. I woke before my Eight Sleep bed started to shake or warm me…and things were amiss. The amount of sleep was not accurately tracked.

I went to bed early, 8 pm. I work up in the middle of the night, and went back to sleep. However, when I rose, before the dawn, the Eight Sleep App wound not open. The base glowed red. Something is amiss.

I closed the app. I opened it again. Just a giant 8, glowing an eerie white, in the pre-dawn morning.

Something seemed…off.

Oh no. A scream was heard from down the hall. I ran to check on my son…only to discover the Roblox server, on which BrainRots sat, was…wonky.

“bots!” Trent, my son exclaimed.

Sadly, no…it was A server in Virginia, and who knows what else.

“it’s ok, I’ll just play fortnite!” he exclaimed.

That was the day we had the first talk, father to son, about the nature of futility and the wisdom of both cloud redundancy and radical acceptance.

Site after site was inoperable. Many citizens were bored to death. To this day, I don’t know how our preferences on products were tracked, nor how our ads were target, during that dark time. I will always remember what it was like to not only be unable to venmo leading congresspeople my “donations,” nor to snapchat my stories to those with whom I’d been talking.

That day? The streaks were rent asunder.

Lo, these things are all now a distant memory.

Go to bed, children. It’s a long time ago now, and none of the posts on which I would have commented on Reddit matter any more, anyway.

Sketchnote by Adrien Liard

This article dates back to 2022, but it’s worth revisiting.

As psychiatrists, we are acutely aware of the pressures facing our fellow physician colleagues and have been disappointed when media coverage of physicians who have died by suicide has been handled poorly. Similarly, when high-profile celebrities like Kate Spade, Robin Williams, and Anthony Bourdain die by suicide, media coverage has often not been in line with established journalistic guidelines designed to responsibly report on this topic.

On our now-ancient-history Clubhouse Creator First show, New Frontiers, we curated a conversation about reporting on or talking to the public about suicide. Our panel included Chris Maxwell, the head of PR and Media for the American Association of Suicidology , suicidologist Fredric Matteson , Raghu Appasani, MD, founder of the Minds Foundation , as well as Ginko , and Cory Feist, who is the brother in law of Dr. Lorna Breen, an emergency room MD who died by suicide during the pandemic, and president of the Dr. Lorna Breen Heroes foundation started in her honor to help physicians who are struggling emotionally. We also spoke with journalist and researcher, Klaudia Jaźwińska, who is the co-author of a recent study of how the media reports on suicide.

Here are the key takeaways from our conversation:

There are 16 commonly cited best practice recommendations to follow when writing about suicide in the media:

• Share a hopeful message that Suicide is Preventable

• Convey that Suicidal behaviors can be reduced with mental health suppor t and treatment

• Provide helpful information, such as warning signs or risk factors of suicide

• Do not provide details about the location of death

• Provide National Suicide Prevention Lifeline phone number or similar crisis number (see end of article for a list of some)

• Do not include details or images of the lethal means or methods used

• Do not display the article prominently in the newspaper

• Do not describe the suicide as inexplicable or without warning

• Do not provide specific details regarding notes left behind

• Use preferred language (e.g. Died by Suicide , not committed suicide)

• Use a photo that focuses on an individual’s life vs death

• Use a non-sensational headline

• Avoid single cause explanation for suicide death

• Avoid referring to suicide as a growing problem , epidemic or skyrocketing

• Avoid prominent photo placement at the top of the article

• Do not glamorize or romanticize suicide using the language of heroism

Help and Hope Exist!

Illustration by Angie Krieg, who used to work with us at a former practice.

In the study by Jaźwińska and colleagues looked at the media coverage of the death of Dr.Lorna Breen during the height of the Covid-19 pandemic, every media outlet violated at least 5 of the guidelines and some only followed 2 of them. Seven of the guidelines were followed by fewer than 1/3rd of the outlets. What we found most striking as psychiatrists was that none of the news outlets shared a hopeful message that suicide is preventable, even though we have effective treatments available for mental illness. Very few indicated that suicidal behaviors can be reduced with mental health support and treatment.

We know as mental health professionals that help is available for suicide. We have several medications with an evidence base for reducing suicidal behavior. For example, esketamine recently was FDA approved for suicidal patients with depression, clozaril is approved by the FDA for suicide risk reduction in patients with schizophrenia and there are meta-analyses suggesting lithium reduces death by suicide in individuals with bipolar disorder. We also have evidence-based therapies. Cognitive Behavior Therapy for suicide attempters, Dialectical Behavior Therapy and Mentalization-Based Treatment are all designed to help reduce thinking and behaviors. Other interventional treatments, such as Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation and ECT can be game changers for individuals with severe depression. The bottom line is some treatments can help and it is dismaying—as a psychiatrist—that work with individuals who experience suicidal ideation that this messaging is not being included as regularly as it could be in reporting on suicide.

There is concern about a possible contagion effect following reporting of suicide deaths, although this is quite difficult to study, and Chris Maxwell noted the data on this is quite messy. Following Robin Williams’ death, one study reported an increase in the U.S. suicide rate by 9.58% in the 6 months thereafter compared to the previous year. There could be other factors for this, of course, but following his death, the media did take more of an interest in following best practices. While there has been an improvement over time in coverage that could be harmful, coverage that could be beneficial is limited. The results of a similar study around the deaths of Kate Spade and Anthony Bourdain indicated that the more hopeful messages about treatment and prevention were limited to non-existent in most media coverage. Anthony Bourdain died by suicide several days after Kate Spade, and due to backlash and education around suicide reporting immediately following Spade’s death, coverage of Bourdain’s was slightly more adherent, as this graph from the article shows:

What can you do to help?

In seeking panelists for the conversation, I reached out to several journalists who write about mental health. They noted that they received little to no training in journalism school or from the editors when reporting on suicide. I certainly hope that is changing and that coverage will continue to improve when suicides do occur. It is important if you do see a piece that is problematic, to contact the news outlet right away so they can make the appropriate adjustments. We know that it can make a difference, as it did in the case of Anthony Bourdain and Kate Spade.

Of course, journalists want their articles to gain a lot of views and shares. So there is, in some ways, an inevitable tension between reporting in a sensationalized fashion and reporting in a responsible manner around suicide. However, a 2020 study suggests that articles that better adhered to suicide prevention guidelines were associated with an increased likelihood of being shared on Facebook and receiving positive engagement.

We hope that you will share this article too to spread the word and keep these principles in mind should you be contributing to the many conversations around suicide happening this month and beyond!

“Don’t let them

don’t let them in,

don’t let those vampires in again”

—a song I’m working on

I was writing a song about emotional vampires. That's all well and good, but we all know I'm not just a terrible songwriter and somewhat-humorous-newsletter writer. I'm also a little obsessed with vampires. Historically, I've used vampires to poke fun at private equity. It could be argued that I was poking fun at specific private equity adjacent individuals. Those claims would be false and defamatory. It's the high holy month of October for vampires; have some self-respect and restrain your imaginations, people. It’s all hallows’ eve. It’s the vampire new year!

That having been said, I may have written some prior articles. Here is a quick index of vampire-related topics in this newsletter, before we get onto today's deep dive into vampire literature.

Today, I'm looking into the data on emotional vampires . These are individuals who suck their life out of you, without any of the mess of sucking out literal lifeblood. I’m also not talking about economic vampires, like the PE funds driving hospitals’ care into the ground , and physicians out of practice , and many hospitals to bankruptcy, while their leaders ride off on private yachts.

That is not the story today, of all days. Emotional vampires are different. They can, however, still suck you dry , according to the literature:

A search for the term emotional vampire on Google Scholar reveals 51,000 citations. Wow, this might be bigger than I thought. What is a vampire 1 ?

“A vampire is a fictional creature said to feed off the life force of other living creatures. It is a psychological archetype, and people are instinctively afraid of vampirism.”

—Domina Petric, M.D.

However, Dr. Petric goes on to identify real-world vampires of emotional energy:

The term psychic (psychological) vampire describes destructive relationship that causes damage to total psychological energy that includes both cognitive and emotional component, whilst emotional vampirism relates only to the emotional energy. This is sometimes indistinguishable.

But who are these emotional vampires? Petric splits them up, but they all seem to share an underlying construct :

• The Narcissist. This vampire is

grandiose, self-important, attention

hogging, and hungry for admiration.

• The Victim. This vampire thinks the

world is against him/her, and demands that

others rescue him/her.

• The Controller. This vampire has an

opinion about everything, thinks he/she

knows what is best for others, has a rigid

sense of right and wrong, and needs to

dominate.

• The Criticizer. This vampire feels

qualified to judge and belittle others, and

bolster his/her own ego by making others

feel small and ashamed.

• This author also posits other popular villains as emotional vampires: the gaslighter , the interrogator , and the Casanova , but I will leave them aside for the sake of not diving too deeply into pop psychology for my own good.

This seems to map nicely onto personality disorders, wherein there are deficits in identity, self-direction, empathy, and intimacy. The narcissist might be…someone with NPD. Not a big stretch. However, the others seem to load thusly onto established psychopathology:

• The Narcissist.

• The Victim: Dependent Personality Disorder seems to fit the bill.

• The Controller: Obsessive Compulsive Personality Disorder , perhaps?

• The Criticizer: NPD/maybe OCPD mixed in?

What does the explicitly emotional vampire data say about these individuals? One study from 2018 by Carnevale sums up the problem:

Although narcissists are commonly compared to “emotional vampires” who drain others of their energy, research examining the impact of narcissistic leadership on employee well-being and outcomes remain scant. 2

Oh, it’s narcissism…maybe. This is published in an academic journal about business, so…the authors had a hypothesis, in their workplace area of interest:

we theorize that narcissistic leadership consumes employees’ emotional and cognitive resources, thus negatively affecting their work energy, and voice behavior.

But before we go poo-pooing the work of our org-psych colleagues, they got bigger data sets than most psychiatric studies!

Three-wave survey data collected from 357 employees working at a large consulting firm (Study 1) and experimental data collected from 129 working professionals (Study 2) both provided support for the negative indirect effect of narcissistic leadership on employees’ voice via their work energy.

What did they find? Well, at least in the workplace, with units of “Employee Work Energy” (over there on the Y axis):

Business Journals are making compelling graphs.

There was a moderating effect of something called “leader’s team-oriented accountability. (LTOA)” I was getting ready to make fun of these measures, but they use real, actual science to do it. They have validated scales from prior research to identify narcissistic leadership, LTOA, and even employee work energy (EWE). Medicine—I see your PHQ-9, and raise you LTOA and EWE.

They measured narcissism in leaders with a validated scale:

Narcissistic leadership was measured with an 8-item scale developed by Resick, Whitman, Weingarden, and Hiller (2009). Employees were asked to rate the extent to which each item characterized their supervisor on a 5-point scale… Sample items include arrogant, self-centered, and egotistical (α = 0.92).

And again for LTOA:

Leader team-oriented accountability was measured with a 4-item leader accountability scale adapted from Giessner et al. (2013).

And these hypotheses played out:

Narcissistic leadership was negatively related to both work energy (r = 0.28, p < .01) and employee voice (r = 0.30, p < .01). Work energy was positively related to employee voice (r = 0.39, p < .01).

The only gripe I have with this exploration is perhaps the glib isomorphism between narcissism and Emotional Vampirism? Well, it turns out ideas about vampires have always included the emotions, not just the blood. Classic Romantic Era vampire tales depict:

[the vampires] not only brought misfortune with them, but drew energy and health from those in their vicinity…[they] saw the vampire feeding on emotions as much as it did on blood.

And these historic depictions lead me to wonder if, perhaps, emotional vampirism might be a more robust construct than narcissism or personality disorders themselves?

Consequently, the designation ‘vampire’… describe[s] an entity that has a long enough lifespan to be considered immortal/eternal, that must require some form of human essence/energy to survive — Nina Auerbach observes these energies can be as diverse as ‘self control, creativity, talent, memories’ and even ‘writing time’ (1995: 102), and that has an intentionality in finding and devouring its preferred food choice, as in it actively searches out or lures humans towards itself. 3

Oh, dear readers, maybe we are getting somewhere. What if there are people…or other entities even…who really do need our energy to survive and thrive? And what if we have just decided to let them in?

It’s been noted that vampires are sexy:

The vampire is a highly sexualized being who also has the capacity to set free the sexual desires of women. In the original novel (Stoker, 2003), Jonathan Harker and his fiancée and later wife Mina, a very restrained Victorian couple, become acquainted with Count Dracula…

This, to make a long story short, goes poorly for Mina . When it’s female vampires, they are often depicted as queer:

Vampire women are often portrayed as sexual aggressors, a role reversal of the traditional female role in society. Many times they are also portrayed as bisexual or lesbian women (a common erotic-voyeuristic male sexual fantasy), which may explain the fascination of adolescent boys with the many available female-vampire movies. 4

See also: all of the “ teen paranormal romance ” section in the bookstore.

Perhaps it’s simple: Emotional Vampires want to suck our energy. They NEED to suck our energy, and their thirst can’t be quenched. They need it to live…or, perhaps, continue endlessly onward in their shadowy liminal space between life and death as they suck us dry.

Which brings us to AI Chatbots, our modern energy consumers. They consume absurd amounts of literal electrical energy:

[ai] already represents as much as 20 percent of global data-center power demand, research published Thursday in the journal Joule shows. That demand from AI , the research states, could double by the end of this year, comprising nearly half of all total data-center electricity consumption worldwide, excluding the electricity used for bitcoin mining.

This accounts for about 1.5% of all the electricity on earth:

Data centres – at least at the scale seen today – are relatively new actors in the energy system at the global level. Today, electricity consumption from data centres is estimated to amount to around 415 terawatt hours (TWh), or about 1.5% of global electricity consumption in 2024. It has grown at 12% per year over the last five years.

Further, this energy is consuming tremendous amounts of human interaction, with variability among states:

South Dakota tops the charts with 53 extra days spent chatting - nearly two months of digital companionship. That’s more time than the average American takes for vacation in a year. It suggests AI is less a novelty here and more a lifeline, filling long, rural stretches with conversation.

That human energy is looking for friendship, counsel, and safety:

Our survey found 37% of Americans describe their chatbot relationship as closer to a friend than an app.

and…

Roughly 42% would rather confess to a chatbot than a therapist or priest, and 32% admit to telling their bot things they would never tell family or partners.

And the chatbots, products for engagement with the energy of lonely people, are starting to crowd out human interaction, with their less pleasing “disagreements” and differing points of view:

22% of Americans admit they would cancel plans to continue a good chatbot conversation.

There are plenty of human emotional vampires out there; thus, it’s no surprise we look for something less risky than talking to a human, or so it seems. One paper, in BMJ by Chris Stokel-Walker , has a title that might say it all:

AI-driven psychosis and suicide are on the rise, but what happens if we turn the chatbots off?

The author notes:

Several US teenagers, including 16 year old Adam Raine and 14 year old Sewell Seltzer III, are known to have died by suicide after conversations with AI chatbots.

Empirical data have demonstrated that the current “chatbot bloodline,” while good at getting suicidal people to talk to it, fails miserably at responding to human suffering when tested with standardized measures as they reacted to a simulated suicidal crisis conversation:

None of the tested agents satisfied our initial criteria for an adequate response, 51.72% satisfied the relaxed criteria for a marginal response, while 48.28% were deemed inadequate. Common errors included the inability to provide emergency contact information and a lack of contextual understanding. 5

Chatbots act in a reliably unethical way when they encounter vulnerable humans, similar to their fictional (or real-life!) vampire brethren:

[the] chatbots are prone to a variety of ethical violations. Those include inappropriately navigating crisis situations, providing misleading responses that reinforce users’ negative beliefs about themselves and others, and creating a false sense of empathy with users.

These emotional boundary violations happen with human therapists, but they are the least likely to have licenses that can be stripped. Not so for immortal vampires or energy-hungry data centers.

The horrifying output is what we generally see as terrifying:

bots …have been found to offer dangerous advice—for example, on how to “safely” cut yourself, what to include in a suicide note or strategies to hide intoxication at school.

However, I’d argue that all the human emotion and energy poured into the seductive abyss of the always-available chatbot is the more serious issue. Our feelings and words are nourishing for other humans, challenging, or even infuriating. However, human feelings expressed to other humans are an energetic exchange. For our chatbots, it’s just about driving user engagement metrics and the company's ability to raise its next funding round. Our hearts, our creativity, even our sorrows are all worth so much more than that.

This Halloween, go out, be spooky with each other, and remember—don’t let the vampires in!

I’m joining you, live, from CNS Summit 2025 , one of the finest events (and communities) in the world of life sciences. We are discussing the challenges of drug development, clinical trial design, inclusive enrollment, and more.

It’s a long road to get a drug to the world, but what happens along the way from clinical trial to commonplace relief of suffering for millions of people. One truth? It’s really easy to run a negative trial—even for something that works . If you make mistakes in design or enrollment, you can, reliably, obscure a real result in clinical “noise” that doesn’t allow one to discern the “signal” in an effective treatment. Imagine an iPhone, playing your favorite podcast, on the floor at the Barclays Center, while Modest Mouse is rocking out. Could you hear the words clearly? The signal may be quiet, and the noise can be loud. The question ends up being “Is the phone playing an important podcast at all?” Sometimes yes, and sometimes no, and we design studies to make sure we can hear the words, reduce the noise, and discern what is likely to be true.

One such recent negative trial was about ketamine, which had a negative result in a recent study 1 . One of my readers, , sent me the following breakdown on that Ketamine trial, and helped me understand some of the details that weren’t obvious at first. He and I are both physicians, and we both have bipolar disorder. We have SKIN IN THE GAME. Without further ado,

Is midazolam really as effective as ketamine ?

Dr. MS: I am a psychiatrist with bipolar disorder. Interest in critical appraisal came from my own experience with drug side effects and lack of efficacy. This can be a bias…and now, my article.

Ketamine is a perfect example of what’s wrong with the pharmaceutical industry. Intravenous ketamine works better than intranasal esketamine for depression, but is generic and off-label. This is why Janssen, the manufacturer, is still making huge profits from a simple enantiomer—on track for $2b this year.

JAMA Psychiatry published this week a study comparing IV midazolam and IV ketamine in depression, in a randomized manner. It is a simple study. Patients are randomised to either intervention. There is no placebo arm. The primary outcome is as follows:

The primary outcome was change in depression symptom severity measured by the observer-rated MADRS score from baseline to end of treatment.

Secondary outcomes:

Secondary outcomes included self-reported depression severity, safety, tolerability, health care costs, and quality of life.

Autors did not find a significant difference in the MADRS score between the two arms (−3.16 points, 95% CI, −8.54 to 2.22; P  = .25). As you can imagine, the study is going viral.

My “priors 2 ” are that we are using ketamine way too often and too early, before pramipexole, monoamine oxidase inhibitors, and a lot of other, cheaper drugs, and with better long-term efficacy, which is basically null for ketamine. At face value, this study seemed likely to reinforce my beliefs. Sadly, there is more to it:

Due to COVID, out of the 104 patients needed in the power analysis, the study only managed to recruit 61. The risk of type 2 errors is significant 3 . While this major limitation is mentioned briefly in the article, it is not in the abstract, so it is not reported by those citing the study on various platforms.

There is also an issue with randomisation: 40 % of patients in the ketamine group were taking benzodiazepines, while only 26 % of the midazolam group were. Authors didn’t tell us the doses of benzos, which is problematic given a potential reduction of ketamine’s effect with concomitant benzodiazepine administration.

But there’s even more problematic omissions:

The non-significant 3.16 points decrease in MADRS score is actually in favor of midazolam. One can speculate that we would have reached statistical significance with more patients.

Buried in the article are the results of the secondary outcomes:

The response rate was 46.9% (15 of 32) in the ketamine group and 33.3% (10 of 30) in the midazolam group.

Remission rates were 43.8% (14 of 32) and 30.0% (9 of 30), respectively.

Among participants who achieved remission, 28.6% (4 of 14) in the ketamine group relapsed, while 44.4% (4 of 9) in the midazolam group relapsed during the 24-week follow-up.

As you can see, those 3 outcomes seem to be much more in favor of ketamine than midazolam. The authors did not comment on those results. They actually did not give p-values either. As response and relapse are based on the MADRS score, how is it possible that midazolam reduces the MADRS score by 3.16 points, but more patients reach response and remission status with ketamine?

That’s because we’re just comparing means.

Same mean, very different individual variation

It seems midazolam is more consistent, while ketamine helps some patients a lot, and does nothing (or maybe worsens?) for others.

Now, as a prescriber, are you most interested in a drug like midazolam or ketamine?

This is, of course, speculation. We do not have the slightest idea how the data are distributed across the two groups as we read the article. Please also note that there was no difference in quality of life between the groups at 24 weeks , as expected given the brief effects of both interventions.

This is the core of the issue. We should not have to guess. The authors should have expanded on the conflicting results. And short of such information, psychiatrists should be more cautious when citing this paper.

I think ketamine is overused, both racemic and intranasal. I would be pleased with a study showing it’s not more effective than midazolam. But so far, this study is not it.

Subscribe now

I’m thankful to my colleague for sharing his analysis and opinion, and you should follow his substack too!

Personally, I suspect that in the right patients, Spravato and ketamine are both effective, and an underpowered study isn’t changing my perspective too much. However, science should be a debate until we get to the truth, no matter how subtle or hard to discern. Our patients deserve it.

Bipolar disorder is a challenging illness. It used to be called manic depressive disorder, and it has one of the best textbooks ever published in the discipline of psychiatry.

The authors of the book include one person who lives with bipolar disorder, none other than the prolific author Kay Redfield Jamison.

Buy Manic Depressive Illness and Recurrent Depression on Amazon (affiliate link)

GSK used to hand this out for free…well played, well played.

Kay and I have never met. For somebody I've never met, her thinking has influenced my life tremendously.

Prior to becoming a very publicly bipolar author, she was a very privately bipolar Psychologist who did a remarkable amount of scholarship and clinical care. When she decided to come out about having bipolar disorder, she decided to stop seeing patients. That decision always bothered me. Why should a patient with bipolar disorder not get the benefit of a clinician who actually understands what they're going through?

So, when I came out about having bipolar disorder to my colleagues, and again when I started writing about it? I didn't stop seeing patients. It was a bit of undoing for a decision Kay made, in Kay's life, for reasons that made sense to Kay, that I took issue with in my heart.

I wish there were more healers for the me I was at age 20, who I knew would understand. It felt very lonely, as a young person, to look at all these put-together doctors and not know that any of them actually understood what I was going through. It later turns out many of the doctors who cared for me also shared this secret affliction, I would learn only decades later.

When it came time for me to choose what to do with my career and with my voice as I became a writer, I did the opposite . 1

Today's article is about a subset of those lessons. Here are a few “how tos” for those seeking to stay alive with bipolar disorder

Bipolar disorder is an episodic illness. Sometimes, and not other times. That is the sine qua non of bipolar disorder. Another crucial, if not literally mandatory, feature is the nature of bipolar disorders’ circadian connection . The moods of bipolar disorder are profoundly related to the cycles of the Earth. In the late fall, during the second and third weeks of October in the northern hemisphere, the amount of daylight changes rapidly. Daylight saving time was invented to address this phenomenon, giving us a little bit of light, a little bit later.

The earth’s tilt is not subtle, and the higher or lower you live on the surface of the globe, the more profound its impact on your underlying biology, and for individuals with bipolar disorder, that is deeply connected to mood.

So, tip one, for individuals with bipolar disorder, I want to live a life with less chaos based on variability in their mood? Consider a Move towards the equator.

There is some negative data for this approach based on mood self-report, I’ll note 2 . However, when scientists used a hard endpoint—suicide attempts—they found a significant relationship between the amount of light hitting the patient on their spot on the globe and fewer suicide attempts in bipolar individuals:

There was a significant inverse association between a history of suicide attempts and the ratio of mean winter insolation to mean summer insolation (mean winter insolation/mean summer insolation). This ratio is largest near the equator which has little change in solar insolation over the year, and smallest near the poles where the winter insolation is very small compared to the summer insolation. … All estimated coefficients were significant at p < 0.01. 3

For many of my patients and my friends with the illness, moving to Texas, Florida, or even Costa Rica can be remarkably stabilizing if depression is the primary driver of unwellness.

Variability in the amount of light in the day is, depending on how finely tuned your underlying biology is, poisonous to the ability to maintain one's mood within expected and tolerable parameters.

The closer to the equator you live, the more stable your life may become with respect to depression or suicide attempts. The trade-off is that at least one study noted more average episodes of mania in bipolar sufferers in equatorial zones:

Data on the total episode number and number of manic and depressive episodes separately was available in 439 subjects. The subjects had been ill for 7.45 years, had experienced an average of 3.29 episodes of mania and 1.08 episodes of depression. Thus episodes of mania were seen to be more frequent. 4

A subsequent paper—by more authors than I’ve ever seen on a science paper in my life—evaluated an even larger cohort of bipolar patients and their location on the globe.

Solar insolation is a fancy way of saying “amount of sunlight.” This large 2024 study found that other factors matter in suicide attempts:

The crucial outcome here is as follows:

Comparing the ratio of the minimum insolation/maximum insolation of 1 near the equator and a ratio of the minimum insolation/maximum insolation of 0 at a pole, the odds of a suicide attempt increase by 45%. 5

That is to say, as you move from the equator to the poles, as an individual with bipolar, there is a massive increase in the risk of suicide attempt!

It was protective to have state-sponsored religion in the country where you grew up, which, of course, could be a proxy for several other factors like genetics. It’s also notable that the “state-sponsored religion’s” protective effects might be related to the fact that those state sponsors are often Islamic, and that might change the risk of exposure to alcohol or substance use disorder, another predictor of death by suicide.

Help surviving bipolar disorder? Well, one evidence based strategy is clearly to move towards the equator, and that same data set suggest that avoiding alcohol and drugs will also help reduce the risk that attempt suicide. People who don't attempt suicide don't die by suicide.

You can't modify when you were born, you can't modify the country in which you were born, but you can avoid alcohol and drugs. And some people can choose where they live.

Fly south, my snowbird brethren!

Data points that start far from the average will become more average over time. We’ve known this since the very birth of statistics, thanks to Francis Galton, whom I wrote about previously here.

The inclusion screening process reads as rigorous:

Potential participants were screened initially by telephone, and then in person by two doctoral-level clinicians to verify the following inclusion criteria: BPD diagnosis; suicidal ideation within the past week; at least one episode of NSSI, suicide attempt, or suicide-related behavior in the past 6 months, and another of these behaviors within the past year; determined to be safe as an outpatient; and not receiving effective treatment elsewhere .

Again, given this was conducted between 2009 and 2014, it’s not hard to imagine these patients with prior suicide attempts and self-injury couldn’t find help. It’s hard to get access to care in 2025 with suicidality, but at least DBT, MBT, and other therapies are now more mainstream.

The DBT Therapy was evaluated by video, and judged to be highly adherent to the model:

All DBT therapists were Ph.D. clinical psychologists trained by the Behavioral Technology Transfer Group. They attended weekly DBT consultation team meetings. All sessions were video recorded, and 10% were randomly selected and coded for adherence …Therapists received an average rating of 4.1 on a scale of 1–5, with adherence indicated by a score ≥4.0.

All psychologist (doctoral-level) therapy limits the generalizability a bit—this majority of therapists are masters level clinicians—but for the purposes of this study, it demonstrates the DBT therapy was absolutely what they thought it was.

The SSRI/M group was more intensive treatment than most patients get, it’s also worth noting:

For those randomized to SSRI/M, fluoxetine was started at 20mg/day, and the maximum dosage was set at 40mg/day, because higher dosages are not more effective for treating depression …. Participants attended 30-minute sessions with the psychiatrist at a minimum frequency of every 2 weeks ; the sessions were weekly if the participant’s condition worsened. They received psychoeducation and general support…A social worker was available on request for clinical management and support, as recommended by Fawcett et al. (27).

30 minutes with an MD at least every two weeks? This is a lot of treatment in America! We have good data, from John Gunderson, that “good enough” psychiatric managmemnt of BPD is non-inferior to DBT in prior studies , when it comes to suicidal behavior. 3

The authors don’t provide details on what the psychoeducational consisted of. Even if it were perfectly aligned with GPM, it would make the outcome of this study all the more surprising.

Crucially, the DBT arm was not given SSRI medication , just the therapy after “washout of their prior medication.”

Two points to make, from your author, interpreting this data:

• SSRI/M, if it is anywhere close to GPM, is an active and effective treatment for BPD (Referencing the prior trial on the Gunderson Manualized Good Psychiatric Management (amazon affiliate link) by McMann et. al.):

Thus, the difference here is that the DBT group isn’t on an SSRI, and the SSRI/M group is likely getting a non-inferior therapy plus something deleterious in the Prozac.

That is to say, either DBT is preventing suicide better than SSRI/M, OR SSRIs, when added to “/M” clinical care, may be HARMFUL to BPD patients and increase rates of suicide attempt and self-harm because it’s been established previously that GPM, which looks a lot like “/M,” is non-inferior to DBT.

This study doesn’t include a third arm that would allow us to tell if DBT is good or SSRIs are bad. Both could be true. It’s already a frighteningly hard study to run, as evidenced by the many years it took to publish this paper, so I don’t blame them.

• The second takeaway is more pedantic, but this data makes it hard to argue that structured management, which includes the GPM model's recommendation to “offer the SSRI,” should continue to include it. This trial allowed meds for sleep, including Ambien and benzos , which I would not prescribe for BPD patients in this day and age, in both groups. So it’s not drugs or no drugs— “/M” is specifically worse than DBT AND NO SSRIs in that it leads to more suicide attempts. Benzos alone are already associated with more deaths by suicide 4 ; it’s worth remembering.

But maybe, just maybe, the black box warning of SSRIs for adolescents should extend to BPD patients also? And maybe, just maybe, the risk of suicidal ideation from SSRIs in adolescents might be best understood as a risk that is based on underlying variability and…wait for it…maybe those kids, the more suicidal ones, had BPD in those theoretically depression studies?

In summary, this data, to your author Owen Muir , provides yet more rationale to encourage DBT and other non-pharmacological treatments for BPD. This, as always, means we need more robust reimbursement for a treatment that is expensive to train in and to provide… but plausibly life-saving. It’s also the case that psychiatrists should consider never prescribing prozac and plausibly other SSRIs to patients with BPD. We have data that the drugs don’t work over and over again, and here, they are worse than therapy alone.

BPD patients deserve better than a hurried prescription and a caring follow-up. They deserve definitive care— and that means therapy, and perhaps treatments like TMS for their depression, not plausibly-suicide-risk-increasing drugs.

Health plans? Update your prior authorization standards and treatment algorithms accordingly!

When I woke up early this morning, around 4 AM, I was under the misapprehension that I was gonna go back to bed. This is not, in the end, what happened. Luckily, I went to bed pretty early, and so I'm not completely exhausted, now, at 6:48 in the morning, when I'm finally getting a virtual pen to virtual paper. Yes, I know early morning awakenings are symptoms of depression. I also know being kicked by a nine-year-old is not, but it also happens. Happened at around 3:59 am.

Usually, when I wake up early in the morning, I'll turn to a podcast, and I did that again this morning. For those of you who are wondering, “What's the best kind of podcast when you really wanna get back to sleep?” I have some answers for you!

Last night, it was “Articles of Interest.”

I can't recommend this highly enough. It's engaging storytelling, but it's about clothing, not about wars and bloodshed, which is the majority of history podcasts. Articles of Interest is engaging, but your life's not gonna change for the worse if you fall asleep in the middle of a podcast about clothing. You can go back and listen to it again without feeling bad. There's no emotional penalty to falling asleep in the middle of an episode.

The other game-changing podcast for sleeping?

Take everything you love about Avery Trufelman, and add a British accent! Now you've got Ruth Goodman, the fantastic host of perhaps the apex predator of going-back-to-sleep podcasts. If you want the entire history of laundry, or sheds, or hats, it's here for you. No one's gonna judge you for falling asleep in the middle of an episode about hats, and you can basically fall asleep to an episode about hats as many times as you want without it mattering, moving on!

So I finally got up, had a Spanish tortilla augmented with a Cortado, and contemplated what I would be writing for you today. I then spent about half an hour trying to figure out why I couldn't get onto Google Scholar. It turns out the answer was “Apple iCloud Private Relay” blew up the Google robot filter, and it didn't let me get on Google Scholar to do the thing that I usually do to write one of these articles, which is look up some research. I had to clear digital cookies and Internet browsing history, re-log into a bunch of things, only to figure out, thanks to ChatGPT, that it was Private Relay all along! So that was another 45 minutes of my morning spent mucking around with my computer and not getting to the article that would let me tell a story you, my dear readers, would find engaging.

I had wanted to write an article about the weight of grief. I've been thinking a lot about how a loss weighs on one, and how one loss piles on another; it gets heavier and heavier. I have young children, and one thing I can't help but notice is that they're joyful. I wonder if this is because they haven't had losses piled on them one after another. When you start working as a physician, med schools don't lead with the topic of loss . But if you do this job, seriously, you're gonna be working with people who die. This is conceptually obvious if you're talking about oncology, palliative care, or trauma surgery. But what it feels like is not apparent, or even knowable in advance. You can't take a dip in sorrow like you take a dip in a hot tub, to see what temperature it is.

You also expect it's gonna come from the directions you expect— maybe you will lose a patient, you think, before it's upon you. The weight of relieving human suffering is that there is a lot of human suffering to endure in the process of trying to relieve it.

What's perhaps more strange is that, for those of us who have had a history of depression, we imagine that grief and depression are somehow the same. Again, this imagined sameness proves false. I am a not-depressed person, currently, wafting along through the late fall, a time I remember from my late adolescence in early 20s as a relentless buffeting with the sadness of the season. There is an uncanny valley of satisfaction that I now walk through. My daughter sleeps with us many nights of the week, and it's cute. It's really, really cute. Let me show you:

It's fantastic—it must have taken a lot of work to get to such a simple explanation and history all in one.

The first thought I had was to send it to Nolan.

Instead, I can ask you to send it to everyone in your life, and let them know that hope, and help, is out there.

In the process of my original Google Scholar search on Grief, I stumbled upon the remarkable academic work of Dr. Kenneth Kendler. He's a psychiatric geneticist of a remarkably prolific nature. His work is both impactful and broad, and I highlight one such study today.

It's easy to imagine that we can foresee suicide, but we can't easily. We can look at some predictive factors, and, it turns out, these vary by sex. In women, the following factors increase suicide risk:

In females, the model indicates four direct effects on suicide attempt risk: the occurrence of suicide attempt in parents during childhood (β= 0.159, 95% CI: 0.118; 0.199) and adolescence (β= 0.115, 95% CI: 0.077; 0.153), suicide attempt in peers (β= 0.068, 95% CI: 0.057; 0.079), and low academic achievement (β= 0.166, 95% CI: 0.156; 0.175). 1

For the non-statistician, β is the strength of the relationship between a risk factor and the chance of a later suicide attempt. That is, a higher β means that the factor is more strongly linked to the increased risk of suicide attempt. Similar to effect size, the units are standard deviations. Long-time readers will realize this would allow the use of the Muir-Skee Lo EQE.

In men, the factors are different, by one…

In males, aggregate genetic liability for suicide attempt (β= 0.130, 95% CI: 0.111; 0.148), suicide attempt in parents during adolescence (β= 0.099, 95% CI: 0.074; 0.124), suicide attempt in peers (β= 0.118, 95% CI: 0.108; 0.129), and low academic achievement (β= 0.61, 95% CI: 0.152; 0.171) were related to later suicide attempt. These factors also acted as mediators to explain the association between environmental exposures in childhood and later suicide attempt.

For both men and women, suicide attempts in parents while we were young, suicide attempts in peers, and low academic performance predict future death by suicide. Well, I'll notice that the genetic liability for suicide attempt is present in males, but not in the loading for females. This was data from Sweden, and it was very large-scale:

Using nationwide longitudinal Swedish registers, we included 344,490 males and 323,177 females born 1982–1990

You'll notice something doesn't show up there: the strength of the prediction related to a psychiatric illness. The authors did look at this :

Briefly, we included individual family genetic risk scores (FGRS) for four different disorders: suicide attempt, drug use disorder, alcohol use disorder, and major depression

And the supplemental materials are pretty rich here, but what you'll notice is that there isn't really a strong predictive effect of much of anything. You're also not gonna go back in time and be able to fix poor academic achievement by the time someone shows up and has a suicide attempt, or is at risk for one based on something else you can see. Similarly, preventing parental suicide attempts seems high-yield, but given our inability to predict suicide attempts, it's hard to know who we should be preventing from attempting suicide in the first place, much less using a Time Machine to prevent their parents from attempting suicide, especially when they're young.

It's also possible I'm just being a “Negative Ned,” and we should really be working on inventing a Time Machine, you know, for the purpose of suicide prevention.

I’m left with a hunger for more connection, more love, maybe a Time Machine, supporting children, supporting parents, and perhaps an acknowledgment that life is unpredictable and might turn out OK, even when it looks dark.

Thanksgiving brings together families. You know, the people you have in your life who you don’t have a say about. You are both likely related to your family members, and also stuck with them. Turkeys are slaughtered in the millions. It’s the cranberry apocalypse, and the worst day for marshmallows of the entire year, as they melt gradually over similarly woeful sweet potatoes, perhaps resigned to their fate.

Political and other differences of opinion are often aired. Arguments can happen! What follows is a guide to surviving this sometimes contentious meal with grace. I did a nice podcast appearance on NPR about this years back, in case you missed the sound of my voice.

Talk About Politics!

Strategically, politics will come up. Thus, come prepared. You can and should bring hot political takes. However, the political system about which you have opinions is best if it takes your family's minds into account in advance. This means the kind of political events should be historical . The more ancient, the better.

Here is an example:

Family Member : I can’t see how the deep state wiggles out of this one!

You : Gosh, I know. You are so right. I mean, look at what Brutus did.

FM: Brutus?

You: The plot to overthrow Julius Caesar was a conspiracy. Brutus is just like his ancestor, also named Brutus.

FM: What are you talking about?

You: Disloyalty among the senate.

FM: You mean like Schumer?

You: Sorry, who? Was he a supporter of Carthage?

FM: Carthage?

You: Carthago delenda est.

FM : Can you pass the gravy?

You: Carthago delanda est. Here you go.

This may seem absurd; however, it uses a proven tool from Mentalization-Based Treatment! It’s using a “challenge” to restore mentalizing—the ability to take each other’s minds into account. In the above example, your family member is not planning on talking about ancient Rome. They were planning on having an argument about the right now, and familiar relationships can be emotionally “hot.” Most people have much cooler feelings, if any familiarity at all, about things that happened more than 2000 years ago. One history podcast should prepare you with some talking points about which to “get into it” with your brother or nephew or uncle or dad, all of whom were expecting “here and now with us” conversations. You cool things down and make them a little silly by making it about “there and then with them.”

Talk only about the politics of ANOTHER ERA ENTIRELY . The politics of today are bait—and you aren’t taking it!

Be Curious, and Dig Deeper!

Being genuinely curious about someone else’s experience is an unlock to a great conversation. However, it requires diligence. You can’t stop at the first answer someone gives you—you generally need to drill down to understand what someone really means. However, when you’ve drilled deep enough, you can often understand someone in a way that lets them feel understood, and thus vastly less likely to want to start a fight. One key is to avoid saying things like “I get it” or “I see” prematurely.

Here is an example of how not to handle it:

Family Member: It’s really tough out there, you know, for work.

You: Oh, 100%. I totally agree. Can you pass the gravy?

We still wouldn’t know what the family member means by “tough” or “in what way,” or what about their work makes it so. The conversation is stopped by premature agreement, absent additional understanding. The other common version of this misunderstanding is the “dead cat problem.” Let me illustrate:

Family Member: It’s really tough out there, you know, my cat just passed away.

You: Yeah, right? I totally get that. My cat died this year, devistating.

Other Family Member: My cat died too! I was in bed for a few months!

In the “dead cat problem,” people superficially bond over a shared experience without understanding what about it was meaningful, specifically, to the people who shared the theoretically similar experience.

One person’s cat could have been their only social relationship, another could have 15 cats and have lost one a year for the past 20 years, and another could be a veterinarian. A dead cat is bad, but differently bad, for everyone. Thus, a better version of this might look like the following:

Family Member: It’s really tough out there, you know, my cat just passed away.

You: Oh my gosh, I’m so sorry to hear it. I hear the pain in your voice. What was it about your cat that made this so hard, for you?

Family Member: Well, I got the cat after my divorce, and so it was kind of the creature that helped me get through a really hard time…

You: So, If I am hearing you, this was the pet that was there for you at the hardest time?

FM: Through thick and thin.

You: And what is it like to be without that source of support, all of a sudden?

Dear readers, do you see how different it can be, and how much more in-depth, to be curious before you just agree you had your own experience? Be curious until you really understand, not just until you nod your head in agreement. The goal of any exchange of this kind is to have the other person feel understood, not to feel agreed with on a superficial level.
