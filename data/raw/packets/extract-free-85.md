I have the unbelievable pleasure, recently, to meet Dr. Keith Grimes. He's a brit, and I love Brits. He's a technology futurist, and I'm married to one of those. He's gracious with his time, and my audience benefits from that. In this article, I interview him about how to incorporate AI into clinical practice, and how he brought his clinical practice to the evaluation of artificial intelligence for uses in healthcare. I came away with a roadmap to start integrating artificial intelligence in my clinical practice, ended up may help you think about integrating it in your life, too.

Keith Grimes, M.D., is the founder and CEO of Curistica, a health tech consultancy that helps companies and health systems show that the clinical AI they build, buy, or deploy is actually safe. He spent twenty-four years as a primary care physician in the NHS before leaving practice in 2022. He lives in Seaford, on the south coast of England, near what most people think are the White Cliffs of Dover but are in fact the cliffs at Seaford.

Owen Muir: Introduce yourself!

Keith Grimes: I’m Dr. Keith Grimes. I’m the founder and CEO of Curistica, a health tech company that helps with clinical safety, data protection, and AI product strategy for people building, buying, or deploying clinical artificial intelligence. That’s on a background of being a primary care physician. I was that for twenty-four years. I don’t practice anymore. I practice my medicine through product, not patients these days.

Owen: Depending on whether someone is reading the transcript I turn around and edit up, or listening to your voice, they’re going to get different cues on what’s true. A metaphor for what we’re talking about. Where do you live?

Keith: I’m from Aberdeen in Scotland originally, but I currently live on the south coast of England. The nearest landmark anyone would know is Brighton. I live in a town called Seaford. You will recognize where I live, because any time they show the White Cliffs in the UK, it’s actually in Seaford. If you watch a movie with a bucolic seaside picture of the UK and there are white cliffs in the background, it’s generally about fifteen minutes that way.

Owen: You have the real White Cliffs.

Keith: We do. Dover, because it has a lot of ferries coming in and out, is slightly grubby looking. The White Cliffs of Seaford also turn up in Assassin’s Creed Valhalla , fun thing. Beachy Head, and things like that.

Owen: Twenty-four years is a long time to practice. What does someone outside the UK need to understand about the NHS to make your story make sense?

Keith: The NHS is awesome but challenging. People in the UK are always worried we’re trying to make UK healthcare like America, and people in America are always worried they’re trying to make US healthcare like the UK. The truth is we are at two ends of a very broad spectrum.

You pay out of general taxation, so no money changes hands at any point. You go in to see a primary care physician or a hospital, and there are no bills. I was always delighted when American patients would see me in my practice, and they’d be scrabbling around looking for where to pay, and I’d say, no, it’s all covered.

In primary care, the UK has really deep coverage. Primary care does ninety percent of the appointment workload, and it’s done under a capitation model. If you’re a general practitioner, you get paid per head of patient per year, so whether they see you a thousand times or not at all, you get paid the same amount. There’s a lot of incentive to manage patients well, and you tend to have a long relationship with your patients — the cradle-to-grave record.

The downside is that primary care acts as a gatekeeper for any secondary care. Unlike in other countries, you can’t just go to a cardiologist or a dermatologist. You go to your general practitioner first. If you do need specialist care, you’re referred on, and the UK, like many places, is challenged. There can be long waits.

Owen: There is also a whole parallel system of private care.

Keith: Yes. It’s small, about four or five percent of healthcare in the UK. People can pay for private insurance or just pay out of pocket. Some employers offer private medical cover. There’s been quite a lot of growth recently, particularly for common procedures like cataract surgery and hip replacements, because waits on the NHS can be many months or into years. Care is still predominantly provided in the NHS, which always acts as a backstop. A lot of urgent and catastrophic care, or cancer care, is managed within the NHS, whether people have private cover or not.

Owen: Even if you use the private system, you’re probably also an NHS patient, because basically everyone is.

Keith: Absolutely. You can swap back. That sometimes causes headaches. There are accusations of people jumping the queue if they can afford it. A patient sees a GP, the GP says you may need to see a cardiologist, but it’s not so urgent, so there’s a bit of a wait. The patient pays to be seen privately, gets investigations done, and is then handed back to the NHS, sometimes with medications that aren’t on the formulary, or instructions for further investigations that haven’t been covered. There’s a bit of tension. The data doesn’t flow back in any meaningful way beyond letters and PDFs, which is crazy. People will have blood tests done in the NHS, go off to private care, and have them all repeated, or vice versa.

Owen: The cradle-to-grave record is not integrated.

Keith: There are two sides to it. The private sector is still small, so copies come back and get integrated into your main record, but it can be slow and fractured. The bigger issue is that the NHS is not one thing. It’s a constellation of different providers operating to core contracts, and for primary care, they’re all essentially independent businesses.

Owen: Lay that out for me. I had no idea.

Keith: Most general practices are run under the partnership model. These are unlimited liability partners operating as independent businesses, or independent practitioners. The time was, that allowed them a great deal of flexibility. That was decades ago. What’s happened is that there’s a core contract, the General Medical Services contract. It specifies certain core services they’ll provide, and for that, they get a certain amount of money per head of population they register within a certain radius. On top of that, there are bonus payments for the Quality Outcomes Framework, certain core disease priorities. And there are items of service. Partners can opt into other services, which sometimes pay more. It’s mostly from that single core contract, renegotiated every so often, which causes as much gnashing of teeth as the central bodies specify new things, or changes in pay, with an equally representative body arguing back.

Owen: Is everyone a sole proprietor, or are some working in larger groups, all contracting with the NHS but not employees of it?

Keith: Partners are tied into a partnership agreement and take their pay from the pool of money. They tend to employ their staff: nurses, pharmacists, salaried doctors. Some doctors work as salaried employees. In secondary care, hospitals are big organizations that employ their staff. If you’re a surgeon, you’ll be employed for a certain number of hours a week, and you may do private work outside it. Primary care is still very much partnerships.

Owen: Take us into the details of practice and how you decided not to do that anymore. The product is your patient now.

Keith: I’ve always loved technology. My earliest memories are of the technology of the time in the seventies and eighties. It seems rudimentary now, but it’s been my real joy and passion. Back in the late eighties I wanted to write computer games, but at the time that was seen as a bad career choice. Maybe it’s a bad career choice now, I don’t know. I did pretty well at school. I don’t come from a medical background. None of my family are doctors. I was given an ultimatum of being a lawyer or a doctor. I definitely didn’t want to be a lawyer, and I liked medicine a bit. I went into it, and I’ve loved the science and practice of it, but the technology was always poking through. All the way through university in the early nineties, at the dawn of the internet, I was always trying to find ways to get technology into it.

I’m a builder and a tinkerer. My approach to medicine has always felt like that. I like solving problems. I like solving problems with people. I was a practically minded general practitioner. In general practice you may also not know, a lot of them operate personal lists. If you’re registered at a practice, you’re seen by the same doctor. Patients gravitate to the kind of doctor that works the way they need. My patients took a functional approach: I have a problem, I need someone to do the diagnostics and fix it. As opposed to some doctors who lean into maternal health, or mental health. I was very much, let’s measure things, work out what’s going on, and try something.

Owen: What was the moment that led you to break with practice?

Keith: The first thing was the idea that it could be possible. Once you get into medicine, there’s this idea that you start as a sort of protean physician, then find your specialism, then get into a narrower and narrower track, and at some point you’re that part of the machine, and you keep the engine of medicine going.

For me, it never felt comfortable. I always wanted to use technology, and there was no specialism for that, no way of fitting it in. I was constantly frustrated, because I saw all these advances. Patients would come to see me, and I understood there was a way to help them, but it wasn’t available on the NHS. No one was building it. If I asked for it, the answer was inevitably no.

In 2014 I went to Exponential Medicine in San Diego. I paid, I took time off, and had the full glitz and glamour, jetpacks-on-the-moon type talk. That was impressive enough. What changed my thinking was speaking to the people there. As I talked about all the ideas I had, instead of saying it’s not going to work, they said, that sounds amazing, what are you doing about it? I can introduce you to someone. They weren’t waiting to do stuff, they were just doing it. That lit the fire.

I managed another few years in practice, and I got very burnt out trying to balance the two. I spent about five months off work and had my dance with the horribly impressive illness that is depression. As I came through it, I realized I couldn’t wait around anymore. It just so happened that a company called Babylon was looking for a more senior clinician to help the junior doctors building their AI products. I got on board, and at that point there was no turning back.

Owen: Give us the brief paragraph version of Babylon.

Keith: Babylon was founded in 2013 and disappeared in 2023. It grew very large. At one point it was listed on the New York Stock Exchange, valued at four point two billion dollars under a SPAC. I think that was the thing that did for it. Telehealth company at a time when telehealth was seen to be radical and, according to some doctors, kind of criminal. Early changes in artificial intelligence introduced chatbots for symptom checking. Our founder, Ali Parsa, had a big vision and a way with words that led him to win many friends and many enemies. He was a provocative character. Babylon grew at one point to serve nearly seventeen countries globally, and started to serve in the US offering value-based care.

In chasing this incredible growth, at the point that venture capital and easy funds dried up, spending too much and not bringing as much in, even though they were undoubtedly bringing a lot in, led to it folding and being acquired. I left slightly before the end.

Ali, with his vision of delivering accessible, affordable healthcare to everyone on earth, brought together some fantastic folk. That was the big thing for me. I’d been a techie doctor, but at Babylon I met product people, designers, engineers, commercial, marketing. It felt like landing back in university again with some really amazing people. That’s where I had my first exposure to the world of product, and had my head swiveled.

Owen: You weren’t just a doctor anymore. You were a member of a corporate enterprise.

Keith: It made me realize some things about myself that had been undernourished in the NHS. I actually quite liked being in a competitive commercial environment. My dad wasn’t a doctor, he was an oil man, and spent a lot of time traveling the world and working with American companies. Little did I realize that all the family gatherings we were taken to were business meetings. It felt like a very natural fit. I’m a gamer. I like the idea of competing with other people. Within the NHS there is a kind of collaborative competition, but no one wants to fail. In the commercial setting, everyone wants to succeed. The idea that we were doing something new and challenging, and working out how to do it, was something I felt I hadn’t experienced within healthcare at all.

Owen: So you had the moment, and that meant you had to start your own company?

Keith: Not quite. While I was at Babylon, I had the opportunity to study an Executive Masters in Medical Leadership at Bayes Business School in London. Led by Professor Amanda Goodall, who is the originator of the theory of expert leadership — the radical thinking that organizations function best where people who are experts in the core activity of that business are running it. The evidence is exceptionally strong. Banks work best when run by bankers who’ve spent their life in banking. Legal firms by lawyers. Universities by researchers. And, oddly enough, hospitals by doctors. America knows this. In her original study, the top forty-nine rated hospitals in the US had a doctor as CEO. That is very much the exception in the UK. The idea of general management expertise was roundly challenged by her work.

Her course was about getting more doctors to be CEOs. Essentially like an MBA but with a lot more medical tilt, over two years, while I was at Babylon. All the things I needed to know — because I’d been in positions of leadership as a doctor, but naturally, because you get more senior and they put you there — were all explained to me. Because I was hanging around with the health tech and entrepreneurial environment, I started to think, I can give this a go myself.

I came out of Babylon at the end of 2022 because things were getting difficult, and they weren’t building things as much, and I didn’t like that. I started out as a consultant, but the reason I started the business was that the blend of doctors who understand compliance — is it safe — and technology and product, is extremely thin on the ground. As much as I love going in and helping folk, I’d like to do it at scale. I just love the work. Three years in, it’s hard. Anyone who runs a business knows that. But I’m doing something amazing.

Owen: Did you co-found with somebody else, or was it you as the sole founder?

Keith: Just me. I’ve got some team working with me, and I’m still the solo founder. Something I constantly think about. Yusuf Scratchi, a public health consultant, works on clinical safety. Erica Warp, a neuroscientist by background — who I pointed at your podcast immediately after meeting you — works on product. Other members too. Small team. I’m still doing a lot. I’m building all our products for a start, though Erica has now jumped on board. Just before I came on this call, we were mangling with agents and what GitHub’s up to.

Owen: Give us the one- or two-line summary of the point of your company. What is your product, and who’s your customer? Then take us to how Claude Code changes the need for that for everyone.

Keith: At its simplest, the question is: you built something, is it safe? That is literally it. Is it safe? Not, is it compliant? Does it meet regulation? Just, is it safe?

We work with people building technology and help them build and show it is safe. And we work with people deploying technology — hospitals and practices — who’ve been given something they’re told is safe. We help them answer: is it safe, and does it stay safe? In the UK there’s a standard called DCB. I won’t go into it, but it’s a structured way of doing that, so there are bits of paper at the end of it all. I’m not about paperwork. I’m about doing something meaningful. That’s what I’m loving about doing this with AI. No one quite knows how to do it, so there’s a lot of discovery and making it useful.

We do this as human experts, but we use a lot of AI to assist us, including agents. My long-term hope is that AI is unlocking capability for everyone. People are rushing to build stuff, and it may not be safe. Curistica’s mission is to find a way that anyone can build what they want and our technology will make sure it’s safe. Thereby unlocking the ability for people to serve their own healthcare.

Owen: Your product is tools to evaluate the safety of AI-enabled healthcare products?

Keith: In part, yes. In short, our service is going into companies or organizations, helping them understand what they built, what it’s used for, where it can go wrong, how they stop it going wrong and how they can evidence it, and writing that up. The same on the deploying side. Then making sure that when it’s running, you catch anything that goes wrong and update your understanding of the safety. It starts with humans doing that. We use AI to accelerate the quality and speed of what we’re doing, and we’re moving towards AI on its own doing it.

Owen: Outsourced QA for safety, for AI in healthcare.

Keith: Yes, that’s part of it.

Owen: You have ongoing relationships with your customers. You’re keeping their product safe over time, because the technology is changing under their feet.

Keith: Absolutely. And it’s delightful. It’s thrilling, because, as anyone who uses AI knows, this is a technology that just shifts and moves, and forces us to rethink everything — including how do we show something is safe. That’s the lovely bit. We can rethink and say, well, we did it this way, we don’t have to do it this way anymore.

Owen: Who’s your customer?

Keith: On the manufacturer side, usually the chief product officer. Oddly enough, not usually the chief medical officer. It’s the people building health tech products who have a realization that there are standards and regulations they have to meet. They’re also often looking at the fact that doing it the traditional way is expensive, painful, slow, and mostly involves people saying no. We take a product-informed view: they say what they’re building, and we say, right, let’s help you achieve that. Let’s help you build the right parts of your product. Let’s help you foresee what evidence you need to show that it’s safe.

Owen: And hospitals and health systems.

Keith: Hospitals and health systems are very comfortable doing governance, maintaining the quality and safety of doctors, drugs, and physical devices. They seem lost when it comes to the digital and AI side.

Owen: No autoclave for your AI.

Keith: None. No one understands what the different kite marks or different logos mean. They have a vague understanding that it should be a device, shouldn’t it? What does that mean? It can seem cryptic. The important thing is, it’s in part making sure they’re compliant, but it’s more about getting them comfortable with the basics of what’s going on, and whatever they bring in, having a way to deal with when things go wrong and how to improve. It’s just extending governance across to the tech.

Owen: Just as we have material safety manuals, infection control, the chain of infection, wash your hands for thirty seconds. We figured that out for germs. You’re figuring it out for AI.

Keith: And having to figure it out in a practical and pragmatic way. Most general practices are pretty busy. They’ve got staff that haven’t been taught any of this. It sounds like another painful thing to do. I always start with the same thing. If you can’t do anything else, if you’re using a technology, there are some very basic things you need to do. Number one, read the manual. Number two, know how to identify when it goes wrong. Number three, know what to do if it goes wrong. Number four, know who to speak to if it goes wrong. Those things will cover a huge amount of the problems of any technology. It’s just not happening at the moment. People think of technology as being somehow very different to everything else.

But it’s something where you can read the manual and understand it, and take appropriate safety measures, and act within your competence. Which is what we expect all professionals to do.

Sadly, at medical school — I don’t know what it’s like in the US — but even as of this year, you can go to medical school in the UK, it’s a five-year course, and come out in five years’ time, and there will be no compulsory core component that covers clinical safety, AI, digital health, or data science. A junior doctor in 2031, unless they’ve done optional courses, will have had no training in those things.

You can practice medicine today and spend the whole day and not prescribe a single drug. I guarantee you can’t get through a single day without using technology.

Owen: I’m going to do some speed-round questions. What is software as a medical device?

Keith: A medical device is a product that does something that would ordinarily take a clinician. If it’s written in software, that software is a medical device.

Owen: What is definitely not software as a medical device?

Keith: Always a bit argumentative, but if it’s not informing diagnosis, management, treatment, or prognosis of disease, it’s almost certainly not.

Owen: Give me an example of something that is not a medical device but is still technology.

Keith: An appointment booking system.

Owen: Your favorite software as a medical device?

Keith: I quite like fully autonomous. There’s a company called Skin Analytics. They have a product called DERM. It was the world’s first Class 3 device that, without the need for human intervention, can rule out the likelihood of skin cancer from a photo.

Owen: What happens when you put a human in the loop of a product like that?

Keith: Interestingly, the performance gets worse with a human in the loop. This is the whole point of DERM. They had to demonstrate performance greater than that of an expert dermatologist across all skin tones. That, plus additional evidence, got them to the point where they could be autonomous, which is the highest status. It’s often not used that way, because people feel uncomfortable. But we do see evidence that in certain specific settings, a human on their own has a certain performance, an AI on its own has a certain performance, and adding a human in the loop degrades the overall performance. I don’t think that’s happening much right now, but.

Owen: It’s a little bit like when Luke was flying to fight the Death Star. There’s a choice — you could use the computer or the Force, but combining them wasn’t the right answer for Luke.

Keith: The truth is Luke should have actually just not gotten into the X-Wing at all, and let the computer run it. But that wouldn’t have made a good film.

Owen: Luke, use the AI, Luke. Don’t bother with the Force, it’s error-prone. That’s why Red 3 crashed.

Keith: Meanwhile Obi-Wan has disconnected the actual controls from the thing, so it’s piloting itself at that point.

Owen: He used the Force to just take the human out of the loop, and now we’ll be safe.

Keith: Yeah. In certain cases.

Owen: The point is, it’s an empirical question. With the DERM example, you put a human in to double-check the AI and the outcome is, on average, worse.

Keith: Yes. Check the papers from DERM to see exactly what they say. Introducing a human means they may countermand the instruction from the system, and that can lead to degraded performance.

Owen: I noticed this when I was in medical school. When I was taking tests, I actually checked whether checking my answers meant better or worse test performance. I’m a fast test taker, and I’m not perfect, I make mistakes, but it turns out when I went back and checked, three or four times just to see, I did empirically worse on my examinations than when I just answered them and left the room before everybody else. When I stopped checking, my performance improved. Second-guessing was empirically worse than just answering the question. I finished tests in a timeframe that baffled my colleagues. There was one person who was faster, and only in medical school. In every other setting, I took Step 1 in six hours. Step 2 is an eight-hour test I took in four, on both days. I took my psychiatric boards in forty-five minutes. My child boards in thirty minutes — an eight-hour and a four-hour test, respectively. I just taught myself that if I go back and recheck, I’m not going to know a thing I didn’t know.

Keith: You might move yourself away from something that was obviously more correct. That works for you. I was constantly told to go back and check things, and I think I benefit from a bit of reviewing. We each have our own model to understand the performance of. And AI is its own weird model too.

Owen: From your perspective as a physician, if an LLM came in as a patient, what would your assessment be? The LLM comes in and says, doc, I’m making some mistakes, I need some help.

Keith: Interesting one. The first thing to say is, I prefer confabulation to hallucination. It seems to be a closer fit — making up facts to fit the situation they found themselves in. I’d probably be checking their liver function and their B12 and thiamine and everything like that.

Owen: The markdown files for human functioning?

Keith: Yeah, you’d have it in the background. What were their settings in there. Actually, you’d definitely be thinking about the memory. If you’re living in the world of agents right now, you’re spending a lot of time thinking about memory and context and how you’re handling it. It’s inevitably got me thinking about my own memory store, how I store context. It’s fascinating.

Owen: What are the best practices to give your AI life the best and most robust memory?

Keith: Right now, it’s got to be hand-rolled. I don’t think there’s anything off the shelf that’s doing it well.

Owen: Define hand-rolled for us.

Keith: I was using Claude Code a lot from the middle of last year, and as it got better, I observed how it was making a lot of back-reference to the codebase, and was spinning out lots of markdown files. I realized it was punting out all these markdown files and referring to them. OpenClaude came along and was very obvious about it. You set up different markdown files and folders that were easy to manage, about the soul and personas and so on. So I started.

Owen: Define a markdown file for the audience.

Keith: A markdown file is a text file that uses some simple formatting to turn certain phrases into headings or bullet points. It uses little hash symbols and hyphens. Much easier than HTML. If you do any coding, you’ll have written README files. It’s easy for humans to read, and remarkably easy for AI models to read. It adds a little bit of structure without going hog wild and putting in XML or JSON. AI, because of how the models have been trained, parses markdown very quickly. It seems like the obvious way to do it.

Knowing that, I started putting more and more things — files I had as PDFs, references, I was using Notion — just putting them in folders, then in GitHub repos. So when I instructed a model, it could make reference to a markdown file, and the instructions to the model to look in a markdown file would sometimes point it somewhere else. Once I started crafting that, and also instructing the model to take notes a lot of the time, its performance seemed to pick up a great deal. That was the takeoff for me.

Owen: What if an audience member wants to do something like what you did? What’s a good practice they could take away?

Keith: The AI of choice that I prefer is Claude, for a few reasons. I have less experience using the others. Download Claude Desktop and you’ll get a few flavors of Claude. Claude Cowork is essentially Claude Code, but it works in a little local folder on your computer. Get your subscription, it’s worth it. Find a local folder, call it “my Claude cowork folder.” Start by saying, I’m going to have a number of projects, and I want you, Claude, to set up a skill where any time I do a task on any of these projects, when you’re completed, write it through a claude.md file. I want you to do this every time I ask you to do a task. You can do that in the settings.

Once you do that, every time you go in and work on something, it will start to update its knowledge. You can ask it to write references. As you use Claude, you can get it connected to useful things, like your Gmail. You can ask it to assemble a reference about commonly used people you speak to, or common ways of working. I started that way. I iteratively built up, and if it didn’t work, I’d get it to work.

Owen: An example of a command to write something to your markdown file would be?

Keith: You just say, write it to the markdown file, remember this. These models are quite intuitive. There are more elegant ways. You can use skills, which wrap up common practices, like a shortcode. I have a skill that manages a more complex workspace and structure. Every time I use Claude Cowork, if I do a task, at the end of it all it’ll write a log file in a separate folder. It summarizes what I did, so it can look back not only on the conversation but on the important thing it built up.

The other thing is, I’ve got an issues markdown file. If it doesn’t do well, and it thinks it could do something better, I get it to write in the issues log. Every so often I go into Claude and say, right, let’s have a look at where you messed up before, how can we improve? Constantly trying to refine. Trying to get it to learn.

Owen: Claude, please learn what we’re doing when you’re working in Claude Code.

Keith: Remember this. Just remember this.

Owen: I’m going to read you something a colleague sent me. I won’t attribute it, because I don’t want the person to feel bumped. “I too have been joking I’m having an affair with Claude Code for the last month. My CTO set me up with all our patient data in a terminal a month ago, and I stopped eating, sleeping, recreating, or spending time with boyfriend, children, or other loved ones.”

Keith: I feel seen. I’m not quite as extreme as that, but yes. When my wife’s away working, I am guilty of taking the laptop to bed and making sure I’ve got the last prompts in before I fall asleep. Or if I’m heading out for lunch, I’ll just go back to the computer and stack a few prompts in place, so that dead time is used productively. It is exceptionally seductive, in terms of just being able to get stuff done, because all of a sudden, whatever you’re doing, your agent could be doing something productive in the background. The other thing I’ve done is, you stack things up overnight, and you wake up in the morning and look at the pull requests and see what it’s come up with.

Owen: How do you do that?

Keith: If you have Claude Code, you can use it on your phone. There’s something called Dispatch on your phone. You can ask it to work on a task, and another task, and another task. It will beaver away, and when it’s done, it submits a pull request, which means it’s finished some work and wants you to review it before it combines it back into your main work. That works on coding, but it can also work for general tasks. You have to be careful about what permissions you give it, because you can let it just go and do what it wants, and it might mess up. So put some guardrails in. But it’s mostly housekeeping. Tidying up documentation. Occasionally, if you’re feeling spicy, fixing some small bugs. I dare say I’ll let it do big things at some point.

Owen: What’s the best way for a listener to start working with AI, that’s going to be maximally safe? What’s the best starting task, the way to dip your toe in?

Keith: If you’re in healthcare, don’t start there. Ask: what is your hobby? Assuming your hobby isn’t a terribly risky or dangerous thing, and your hobby isn’t medicine, people’s hobbies tend to be low risk and high interest to them. Go nuts with AI in your hobby. You’re generally reasonably expert in it, and you’re interested, which means you’re more likely to try things. If it doesn’t work, you’re more likely to push that little bit further. If things go wrong, it’s extremely unlikely anything bad is going to happen, as long as your hobby isn’t one of a specific few things.

The Martingale System
The concept behind the strategy is quite simple – you increase your bets after every loss, so when you eventually win, you get your lost money back. After this, you start betting with the initial amount again.

The most effective way to use the Martingale system is to only bet on even-money outside bets – 1-18, 19-36, Red, Black, Even, and Odd.
