When you work with your wife, which, by the way, I do, birthdays can be difficult to schedule for celebration. Birthday parties, of course, are not just about cake or meal. They are about process. Sometimes you go out to a nice meal. Sometimes you spend time together at home and you just have some home cooking.

is my wife. She loves Sushi. But another thing she loves, I am gonna confide in you, my dear readers, is the appropriate classification of medical procedures in healthcare. She loves codes, such that novel treatments, like accelerated TMS, fits into the rubric of Current Procedural Terminology , otherwise known as CPT codes, a registered trademark of the American Medical Association.

She also, especially, loves Omakase . Maybe as much as CPT codes? But only maybe . Let's start with the Sushi.

Ikura and Ossetra Caviar at Shota , in Williamsburg, Brooklyn.

Omakase is a way of serving Sushi—it doesn't just encompass the fish on rice itself, or even the maki, which is of course fish rolled in seaweed and rice. No, it's not just that.

Omakase, in Japanese, roughly translates to “I'll leave it to you.” We might call this is chef’s choice menu, in America.

We've had some great Omakase meals in the over a decade we've been together. One of the delight of Omakase? You don't have to choose individual portions of fish. At best, you're choosing the size of the Omakase, do you want eight courses, 12 courses, or 15 pieces of the Nigiri to leave you feeling very satisfied.

You can of course also obtain Sushi at the corner deli, at least if you're in New York. You see that little plastic container? That's got sushi in it. You might even have good sushi! Piece of Nigiri, maybe a little California roll, you can grab and go. You could even buy two boxes of packaged sushi. Or, you can go to a restaurant and order individual pieces, to your heart's delight, and they bring them out all at once. You and your friend get to have a regular meal, the food comes all at once, you chow down, and if you're still hungry at the end, you can order more. There are even “all you can eat” sushi restaurants. They keep bringing you fish! 1

What's not factored into any of those a la carte offerings? The experience. Who's making the decision about what you should be served, at what time, and in what order? When you go to a regular restaurant, or a deli, and you order sushi, you're choosing the sushi you want, but implicitly, you're also choosing an experience. And you're not just dictating an experience for yourself, you're a dictating and experience for the chef. Their job is to get you the Sushi as quickly as possible, and your job is to eat it. If you need more, you order more. What what you were explicitly choosing to not have is a carefully timed meal. You're not choosing an experience. With Omakase, particularly an excellent Omakase meal, the service is really part of it.

Shota Omakase’s fish, just before it becomes Nigiri.

Boston has one Michelin Starred restaurant, and I'll use that as an example. It’s called 311 Sushi. It's in the ground floor of an apartment building, and it's literally built on top of a garden apartment floor pan. Number 311 on the street.

A delicate black truffle at 311 Sushi.

When you walk in, there's an antechamber, and they take your coat, graciously. Unless it's your time to sit, you won't be seated. This is common in many Omakase, restaurants—they have one, two seatings, and that's it for the whole night. You're not choosing one to come in, you're coming in when the time is right. There's a whole experience ahead of you. At 311, there 10 seats in the entire restaurant. They are all in a row. Here’s what the Eater review reported:

Wei Fa Chen, a Masa alum, leads the 10-seat counter and runs an 18-course, roughly $250 Edomae-style menu. Hidden in a brownstone basement, 311 opened in 2023 and became one of the city’s hardest reservations; Michelin describes the omakase as “impressive nigiri showcasing high-quality product.”

311 Sushi’s owner flew to japan to find the perfect maker of one of a kind ceramic dishes on which to serve their Nigiri Omakase.

At an Omakase meal, instead of ordering from a menu like a savage in a rush , diners trust the chef to curate a multi-course tasting meal. We leave ourselves at the whim and mercy of the chef—often, similar to medical care, they will ask if we have any allergies. After that, it's up to the chef! This Omakase meal which typically features specially seasonal seafood, magical appetizers, and a course after course of single pieces of nigiri—that’s rice with fish on it— tailored to the chef’s vision for your meal experience.

Omakase Sushi is supposed to have an ebb and flow. Omakase isn’t just about the fish —it’s about the moments between courses. It’s about the experience, the pace, the sequence. The experience is what you are trusting the chef to manage for you. It’s the set and setting of the meal that gets a great restaurant to become…unforgettable.

However, unforgettable birthday dinners don’t just happen. You need the combination of a chef’s ambition, and the right fish, team, and system to deploy each course just so. This allows, along with a credit card reader, the ability to provide unforgettable service and get paid. Every restaurant has a “explanation of benefits” of the meal at the end—they use each piece of fish as a “code” for what to bill you, or maybe they just have a bundled rate. Some restaurants don’t even allow tipping, and the entire experience is included in the tab.

Medical care isn’t that different. Some health professionals are closer, in business model, to a corner deli. Fast, “cheap,” good value per dollar, and they can ring you out quickly so you can “eat and run.” Other medical care is more of a production— Brain Medicine specialists need to perform complex, multi-step procedures. With accelerated TMS, for example, this can include multiple stimulations in a whole day, but the time in between matters too! That inter-session interval is when your brain gets to rest and prepare to change more—and a licensed physician is typically available the whole time to manage what can be a complex experience. Floods of emotions, adverse effects, even surprising joy can well up, and healers need to be available when the change can hit you all at once! Once a day 3- 40 min brain stimulation, on the other hand, works more slowly, and doesn’t involve physician supervised time between treatments.

If we want different medical experiences, like we want different meals, we’d need to have a way to “put them on the bill,” as it were.

The ability to describe the procedures, and submit them to payors for reimbursement, is not a “practice by practice” endeavor. Imagine if all sushi billing was standardized by the Asian Meal Association (“AMA”), and in the Edo period, for example, it was decided that all menus could only include once piece of fish at a time. Bills must be submitted by nigiri piece only, with fearsome Samurai standing ready to claw-back inaccurately tabulated meals. Moreover, a particularly tyrannical Shogun could decide that all tuna should be priced the same! They could, in theory, set standard menus and prices. Whence, then, the more rare and prized Toro (or fatty tuna) portions?

This is what happens in healthcare, in case you are missing the point, or something close to it. Individual doctors can’t just chose to include “inter-session intervals,” with on site MDs, in their billing, without the intervention of the AMA. That is the medical one, not the theoretical sushi menu dominating AMA mentioned above! See, dear readers, how important disambiguation is?!?

We, as healers and patients, want to have the ability to choose what quality of medical experience to provide, what a course of treatment includes, and that just isn’t possible without the right flexibility on the menu. Some practices want to offer all-day treatment with TMS. Others want to offer only once-a-day treatment. Yet others feature fMRI ! Others still will use EEG to calibrate treatment.

How treatments like SAINT TMS work, from a slide deck of mine.

We need the ability to submit a bill. And, like the citizens of Edo Era Japan, we have to submit to the will of the royal court—except in this case, it’s a committee, called the CPT editorial board. Their AMA doesn’t pay for Omakase sushi, I’m guessing.

Just as even Emperors need to hear petitions, on occasion, so to do we, as “interested parties” get to register and comment on proposed coding changes to the CPT code set. Thus, as treatments change, the code set, that allows for novel treatments, can also evolve.

Here is how you do that, this year, for the AMA’s proposed updates. It’s a time when our voices, interested as they are, can be heard.

The Call to Action

You are asked to consider what makes sense for your practice: coding that supports à la carte or omakase. That’s what the code-writers are actually deciding.

Both proposals go before the AMA’s CPT Editorial Panel (the group that decides what gets a billing code, and therefore what insurance will pay for) this September. There’s an open comment window right now, and it closes August 10.

Here’s the mildly annoying part. You can’t just click a link and read the proposals. They aren’t posted out in the open. To see them, you register as an “ interested party ” first (yes, that’s the actual term). And an interested party is not just a TMS doctor. It’s anyone with a stake in this: clinicians, patients, families, device makers, researchers, clinic administrators, you. Registering takes a few minutes and commits you to nothing. It just gets you through the door.

Once you’re in, you can read both proposals for yourself, the omakase one ( Tab 92 ) and the à la carte one ( Tab 53 ). Then you decide whether you want to say anything at all.

And “anything” is the operative word. You do not need to be a coding expert. Any reaction is useful, and the most useful one is the honest version: if this became the way these treatments get coded and paid for, how would it actually play out for you and the people you care about?

Register, read, and weigh in by August 10 here: https://www.ama-assn.org/practice-management/cpt/cpt-editorial-panel-meeting-interested-party

Disclosures: I’m a co-founder of Radial, where we deliver accelerated protocols, plus advisory roles at Ampa Health, and I’m the CMO at Neurolief, makers of an at-home Neuromodulation device. I’m not here to tell you what to write. I’m telling you the door is open for you to tell the AMA what kind of menu options you’d like available.

My birthday wife, Carlene MacMillan, M.D,. in Boston for CTMSS 2026.

This article is a repost from 2022. The Tate brothers were arrested the other day in Miami, and so it's time to bring this out of the vault for my readers. This was originally written after Andrew Tate's run-in with Greta Thunberg, an Internet character (and person in real life) of her own complexities which I'm not going to get into here. The issue of narcissism in individuals that seek the spotlight continue to be salient, as they have been since ancient times. It's also relevant that these two are facing serious charges for crimes against women that we should not brush aside. This was written before those serious charges were brought, before Twitter became X, etc. That being said, article…

Given the happenings, which are positively Shakespearean in character, I’m going to begin this article with a a key definition:

• Narcissism is a personality trait. It’s defined thusly:

“Personality qualities include thinking very highly of oneself, needing admiration, believing others are inferior, and lacking empathy for others.”

This is distinguishable from plain old grandiosity, where someone believes themself to be great or important, in that a key feature of narcissism is the belief that one is better than others and the entitled to admiration because of that special status.

The thing about empathy is that having too little of it makes it really hard to imagine the minds of others in a way that’s productive for you. Too much narcissism, with too little empathy, can take something that’s a personality trait, and have it tip over into the realm of a disorder.

People with narcissistic personality disorder are impaired. That’s what makes it a disorder. It causes impairments in their life.

Usually, this is hard to explain to people. Today it’s not. A narcissistic impairment looks a lot like somebody trying so hard to one up a teenage girl that they might have alerted authorities to their location when there are outstanding warrants for their arrest.

Our antihero is a man . He’s pretty clear on that.

His name is Andrew Tate. I can’t make a diagnosis for a man I haven’t met. I can talk about presumptive diagnoses for Shakespeare characters and make comparisons.

I know, I know. I was expecting Macbeth to be older also. So, the young Thane of Tate 1 , he’s a world champion. And he’s here to help us escape the Matrix. Because, you know, nothing says great financial advice like a guy who’s been kicked in the head so many times he lists “Media Personality” as his job in a Twitter profile. But wait, there’s more:

Apparently, the red pill is available to all of us. And like Morpheus before him, he’s here to help us escape from the matrix. He’s got the secrets to wealth creation. He’s so good at wealth creation that he doesn’t seem particularly concerned with the fuel costs for his car. And if there’s one thing I know about wealthy people, it’s that they’re not concerned about cash outlay in the slightest. They really don’t mind the store when it comes to expenses. They also love investing in a rapidly depreciating assets. In the case of Mr. Tate, he’s happy to tell a teenager flaming him on the Internet about his extremely fuel inefficient car collection:

Just as in the plays of William Shakespeare, tragedy is timeless, and the themes repeat throughout history:

Shakespeare wrote the majority of his tragedies under the rule of James I , and their darker contents may reflect the general mood of the country following the death of Elizabeth I , as well as James' theatrical preferences. [1] Shakespeare, as was customary for other playwrights in his day, used history, other plays, and non-dramatic literature as sources for his plays. In Elizabethan England there were no copyright or protections against plagiarism , so characters, plots, and even whole phrases of poetry were considered common property.

The tragic character of Andrew Tate, in the grand tradition of William Shakespeare, may fit better in the mold of “a problem play”—in that like some of Shakespeare’s finer works, it’s not strictly speaking only a tragedy or comedy, it’s definitely both. The tragic flaw of the Shakespearean tragedies is usually that of hubris. People with enough narcissism, such that it becomes a disorder, fall victim to this regularly. This is what is disordered for people with Narcissistic Personality Disorder (NPD). They do things that are bad for them. They do things bad for others.

Andrew Tate is a gross and awful character. Watching him get this kind of comeuppance is just delightful.

Not All People With Problems Will Be Sympathetic

However, not all people have to be nice to be worth of our empathy.

As a physician trained in psychiatry, I am aware that the genetics for personality disorders and the social environments that make those into disorders giving rise to behavior don’t require everyone suffering to be a sympathetic character.

When we’re talking about mental health, and talking about suffering, we usually focus on the suffering of the individual.

However, if we limit our understanding of psychopathology to mood disorders like depression and we limit our scope of empathy to only relatable individuals, we miss out on the bigger picture.

We benefit if we have empathy for people like Andrew Tate. We don’t need to have sympathy for him. That piece of dirt can just choke on his criminal and hateful manipulation of women and die as far as I care .

Narcissistic personality disorder can cause severe problems—for everyone. Some people are mean and nasty. And those mean and nasty people can do less harm in the world if they get help for the underlying problems leading to the meanness and the nastiness too.

What to Do?

The evidence base for these disorders is limited. We don’t have specific treatments for NPD yet. Our group has published on what attitudes health professionals have toward NPD. These attitudes indicate that mental health professionals aren’t familiar with how to treat NPD, on average. However, with more training, they become more interested in treating the condition. They also find the work more rewarding the more they understand the population.

We have even published on the impact of NPD on response to neuromodulation with TMS . Even profoundly biological interventions, like transcranial magnetic stimulation, suffer from misconceptions around their efficacy in those with personality disorders (our work indicates depression is treated just as effectively with and without personality disorders). But much more work needs to be done to get more evidence based treatments.

We need psychiatric treatments for individual suffering just like we need burn trauma ICUs. Ignoring NPD is like ignoring fire safety and not funding fire departments.

We have heroes in our midst. Women like Greta Thunberg recognize hateful behavior, and don’t allow it to “pass.” Greta recognized that the way we interact with somebody needs to be consistent with our understanding of their mind. She quite clearly understood that this person she was addressing wasn’t gonna handle humor well, and so that’s what she gave him. Her sick burn for Andrew Tate was also her gift to the world.

Narcissistic personality disorder is a disorder for all of us 2 . The grandstanding of Andrew Tates should be mocked. And the people suffering should be helped.

We ignore narcissistic personality disorder and it’s treatment at all of our peril. Unlike Andrew Tate, ignoring a whole domain of dysfunction in our research budgets is no laughing matter.

Dear readers, I’ve been a sceptic for too long. It’s easy to be sceptical as a psychiatrist—the flowers will be lour’d upon you! I’ve been using Graymatter’s Health PRISM EEG-fMRI neurofeedback system for a few years, it’s true. There are EEG leads on there. I saw a lot of EEG-related technology in Beijing. It’s possible I’m looking at an EEG in this very photo from my hotel room in China, below:

Owen Muir in China with the CNS Summit Community.

But have I been measuring the brain with EEG regularly? Well, no, again! I’ve been content with my fancy fMRI scans alone, for SAINT , and giant brain healing magnets at set frequencies, like deep TMS with SWIFT .

There are even videos of me talking about TMS, like this one below, with friends like Colleen Hanlon from BrainsWay, and heroic patients who chose to speak publicly, too.

But one thing I haven’t routinely done is measure the brain itself as part of clinical care, as companies like Firefly Neuroscience are making possible. That may need to change, is the outcome in my personal brain as a result of this interview. I speak, at length, with Dave Decaprio, their president and COO.

The EVOKE system provides multimodal analysis of brain function, including Quantitative EEG and Event-Related Potentials. From the Firefly website:

At Firefly Neuroscience, our technology is built to measure and interpret brain function in a clinically meaningful way. The Evoke™ system combines quantitative EEG (qEEG) and event-related potentials (ERP) to capture both baseline brain activity and cognitive response during structured tasks.

This data is analyzed against a proprietary normative database of over 191,000 EEG and ERP sessions, allowing clinicians to evaluate patterns relative to age-matched expectations and track changes over time. The result is a structured, interpretable view of brain function that can be used alongside clinical evaluation to support earlier insight, more informed treatment decisions, and a clearer understanding of patient progress.

I haven’t used it in clinical practice yet, but Dave makes a compelling case, and at this point I think it’s only a matter of time. Brain Medicine, as an emerging specialty, brings change. That change needs to be measured. EEG might be one such tool.

I hope you enjoy today’s podcast! And thanks to , , along with Dave, for their guest spots.

—Owen

This story is about a pair of winners—a nurse and a patient. I was simply an observer.

By the time I passed through the locked door between the hospital proper and the children's comprehensive psychiatric emergency program, there were already upwards of 40 people standing around. Doctors, nurses, security guards. I counted later, and it was actually 38. Your brain remembers these things wrong, reliably.

The security guards looked particularly unsettled. There were four of them. At Bellevue, the guards are all hospital police . So they’re dressed like police officers, they stand like police officers, and kids respond to them like they’re police officers. None of the names you will read are real. Details have been changed for privacy.

“Nooooooooooo!” The shriek from within room one hurt my ears, and I was 2 locked doors away. It has a combination of anguish and adolescent tonal instability that is both heartbreaking and ear rending.

I moved quickly, and gave my key a quick twist to the right, and then another jerk, because the door had a sticky handle.

I didn’t open it more than strictly necessary, and essentially slid myself inside, pulling it closed behind me.

A photo from China. Unrelated, but not AI slop.

The click. It was satisfying. Now whatever happens happens— but the door at least isn’t open because of me. My control of the situation, thus, was exhausted. I tried to simply be. It only worked so well.

The boy was tall, somewhere between 14 and 16, and the kind of slender body habitus meant that, to my eye, he hadn’t been in the system very long. Nobody had given this kid years of antipsychotics, yet, with the associated weight gain. He could pass for a private school kid. He had straight hair. It was unkempt, and puberty plus terror or rage made sure I could smell how much he was sweating.

I turned my head about 15° to the right, and I saw the Director of nursing. He was a short man, and I say that as a short man.

The room in which this young person was pacing, relentlessly, back-and-forth, was spartan. If bright white got sick to its stomach, that would be the wall color. There was a window that had an extremely fine mesh metallurgical cover that once would have been called a grate. This safety device had clearly been clawed at by prior frantic children.

The wooden bed frame was bolted to the floor, there was a thin foam mattress on it, but didn’t have any sheets at this time, and they were two straps, made of red nylon, one towards the foot, and one 3/4 of the way towards the head. The slight scent of chlorhexadine used to scrub down the mattress when the ER ran out of ammonia because of a budgetary crisis presented itself, tucked under the sweat. There was a pillow. That’s all that was in the room. Bullshit excuse for a window. Extra long twin bed. Bare mattress. The nylon straps had what we’re supposed to look like not very menacing buckles.

This was the upgrade, probably mandated by the joint commission of endlessly modernized horrors, for the four-point leather restraints that used to be standard at the beginning of my training. But nobody here was in any way confused about the intent of the bed, or the straps upon it. They were designed to take the arms and legs of that child, pacing in that room, there, who looks terrified, frantic, anxious, and was screaming the word, no, and give him no choice but to lay uncomfortably still.

And what turned out to be 38 people were crowded out in the hall, watching this robust young person, smelling this robust young person, fearing for this robust young person, fearing for themselves, as a child screamed, over and over again.

“Don’t fucking touch me. Don’t you fucking touch me. Don’t you fucking touch me. No. No. No. No”

If a metronome had post traumatic stress disorder, and a history of psychotic mania, it would be this kid.

I felt a hand on my shoulder. The Director of nursing move closer to me, and, without taking his eyes off the young person, said, clearly and quietly in my ear: “ you’re the child psychiatrist on call?”

“Yes.”

“Good. You’ll do great. The nurses already have pulled up a PRN. I don’t think he’s gonna need it. Let me go in there first.”

Juan, without waiting for a reply, because frankly we worked together enough to understand that I knew my place, smoothly walked from the hallway, pressed down the door handle, that only pressed down for safety reasons, opened the door to the child’s room, and entered. He stood, with the door open, and his feet, angled at 45° to the kiddo. This is the most non-threatening angle. His position was breathtakingly in keeping with all training videos, but his stance said more than just precision. One knee bent, slightly. Contra-posto—Michelangelos, David, if he was a nursing administrator and 20 pounds overweight from all the overnights.

I was a first year fellow at the Nyu/Bellevue school of medicine child psychiatry fellowship program. Managing psychiatric emergencies in the middle of the night has been my job for some time, because we do an ungodly amount of emergency psychiatry notations in the name of education. Most of the sickest kids in New York, come through the space, and we have tried to reduce the use of restraint and seclusion, physical or chemical for years now. There had been news stories about how often children got what both they and the press colloquially referred to as “booty juice”— injectable Thorazine to calm them down, in the hopes of right away.

“Hi, I’m Juan. I’m a nurse here. I’m actually the director of all the nurses for kids here. What’s your name?”

“(Mumble)”

“It’s nice to meet you.”

“Don’t let any of those people fucking touch me”

“Oh, that’s good news, we’re on the same page about that.”

“What?”

“Well, I heard you were really upset, and people are concerned about your safety, so a lot of people show up to make sure that nobody gets hurt, but that can be really scary. If you don’t know, that’s what’s happening.”

“It doesn’t fucking look like that”

“It doesn’t look like that. Not at all.”

“Well?”

“I have a better idea, if you want to hear it?”

“Sure” the kids said. Softening, a little bit.

“So, a restraint has been ordered. And I’m a nurse, so I don’t get to say if it happens or not. But what the order doesn’t say is who does that restraint. A restraint is when you lie down in that bed, and the straps, go on your legs, and then go on your arms. But the order doesn’t say who puts them on.”

“Don’t fucking touch me”

“That is kind of my point, the order doesn’t say who has to put them on , and it doesn’t say how long they have to be on.”

“A little weird”

“Kind of a loophole…right?”

“Maybe” the young man said, suspiciously.

“Do you wanna hear my idea?”

“Sure” said the boy, more firm now, his eyes a little bit wider, ready to bolt.

“Well, why don’t you just put yourself in? And then tell me when you’re ready to get out. And then we’ll let you out. You only stay in the restraints as long as you need to, for you, and then we did with the order said. Then we can get on with figuring out how to actually help you.”

“Seriously?”

“Seriously. That follows all the rules, you get to choose, nobody has to touch you, and we get this thing over with, because I’d rather just talk— bet you would too?”

“Ok…”

I don’t remember the words that were said next between the two of them. I remember, he lowered himself down, gingery, and Juan showed him how the straps and buckles worked. It was like he was instructing another nurse. The kid took it in, breathed along, now calm, and focused on the task at hand. First he put in his left leg, and fumbled a little bit with the red strap. By contrast, the right leg was in one smooth motion, and his left arm, even more quickly. He even left just the right amount of room, from what I can tell, for New York City safety standards around these things.

“I have a question” young man asked.

“Yep?” Respond Juan.

“you know how to say that thing about not touching me? I need to take it back. I need your help with this last buckle.”

“Good point” said Juan. He gently placed the buckle around the child’s wrist, pulled it, not too tight.

“So I can just come out anytime I want?” Asked the young man.

“Yes, anytime you’re ready”

“Give me a minute or two. I’m not ready yet”

“I’m on standby”

One minute and 37 seconds elapsed according to the clock on the wall. The officers had already moved themselves to the back of the room, towards the door, the nurses were no longer in a defensive posture.

“How about now?”

Juan reach down, and undid the strap he had placed.

“The rest are all you” he said to the young man.

“Thanks, I appreciate it.”

Documented in my note that night was a total time of one minute and 37 seconds, and I checked an electronic box to indicate that no PRN medication was administered intramuscularly. This was documented again, in a paper note, which will be required to also keep in the chart for compliance with the office of mental health. My signature on that form ended up being the only reason I needed to physically be there that night.

The state standard for the duration of that physical restraint was four hours— before a licensed doctor had to check, legally, for the first time, on how it was going. And then once every four hours thereafter, till it was concluded.

Juan had signed the leave forms for every nurse who was put out on disability that year after being assaulted. The pediatric psych floors had the highest rate of workplace violence against nurses that year. 37 other people could have taken the lead and kept Juan perfectly safe. Including me. The psychological safety of the person who would have to remember being restrained by a hallway full of adults isn’t recorded past the time on the compliance form.

This is the story that comes to my mind when anyone asks me about moral leadership in health care. Leaders demonstrate mastery and compassion not by avoiding risk to themselves. They act in ways that are unforgettable , so they don’t need to teach skills a thousand times—they need to show one, do one, and you can’t forget the lesson. Ever.

— Owen Scott Muir, M.D.

The key point is that the relationship between dose and effect is not a straight line. So, doubling from 20 milligrams to 40 milligrams doesn’t double the effect on the brain. Huh. And so every milligram of drug has a significant effect, like the game of musical chairs at the beginning. It’s very easy to find a chair by the time more and more of the drug is in the system; more and more receptors are occupied. So, every extra milligram of the drug has less and less effect. And that’s why you get this sort of law of diminishing returns.

And so one of the consequences of this is very tiny doses of drug, like two milligrams, Which people would laugh at and say is a homeopathic dose has about half the effect of 20 or 60 milligrams. So tiny doses like 2 milligrams have much bigger effects than you would think. You can use this relationship to try to understand what happens when people follow the kind of advice that most doctors give.

So, most doctors would recommend coming off 20 milligrams of citalopram by recommending halving the dose. And then half the dose again before stopping it is, but what your data shows is that no, essentially, stopping at five milligrams…

OM : you still have the vast majority of the effect of the drug to be rug-pulled…

MH : Exactly. You’ve got it. So you can imagine walking down. If this was a path, it starts as a garden path. You can walk very easily. It turns into a steep hill, and then it turns into a cliff.

OM : But there’s a bias here because every doctor recommending to go down from 10 to 15 is correct. They get to feel better about their role as doctors again when someone goes from 15 to 10. And from 10 to 5, things go fine. So they have this smug sense that things are going to go fine.

MH : Yes, yes, exactly. People are a bit reassured because the earlier reductions are easy . This is going fine. Yes, my doctor has got things under control!

OM : But on both sides, it’s wrong! The doctors are convincing themselves they’re doing a good job, and their patients are feeling like the doctors are doing the right thing. And there’s no data to suggest to either party that it could be a disaster…until the cliff.

MH : Exactly. So what happens at the cliff is that people jump off. They can end up in a state of terror like I was. And because most doctors don’t understand his relationship, they think they must need the drug; maybe the last five milligrams are the most important.

OM : Your depression has come back!

MH : Yes, exactly. So, they tend to misdiagnose relapse of depression or anxiety. I’m sure if I’d walked into any doctor’s office, When I was panicked, terror stricken, I’m sure anyone would have said to me, you’ve relapsed clearly, your condition’s gotten worse.

OM : I have a question for you: How long after a discontinuation or taper event can this kind of experience of horror and dread appear?

MH : It’s a fascinating question because the textbooks say it should take three to five half-lives, which is about three to five days after most drugs.

OM : Those are textbooks about drugs. Not about neurons responding to what happens later, there’s counter regulatory effects that the drugs have been doing for years.

MH : Perfect marks! Precisely. So, a study looked at the average onset of withdrawal effects in a cohort of patients. It found that it was about four weeks with a standard deviation of 13 weeks; in other words, it’s enormous. It’s a large variation, but what it’s telling you is that some people experience withdrawal effects one, two, or three months after stopping their drugs. And there are two explanations for why that might be. Number one is “the mousetrap explanation,” which is that “you drop the ball. It hits the stick that presses the steps that do the whatever .”

OM : Rube Goldberg!

MH : It takes a while for things to unravel! Number two is based on a fascinating study that posits, “It takes a lot longer for the drugs to leave the central compartment than we think.”

For example, after a couple of weeks off a medicine, you can still see it affecting specific transporters inside the brain. In other words, the brain elimination half-life is longer than the plasma elimination half-life. Between those two explanations is probably why you can have withdrawal effects that come on weeks or even sometimes months after stopping the medicine.

OM : There isn’t a “liver in the brain” to metabolize the drug. Most drug metabolism takes place in the liver for compounds in the blood, of course.

MH : Oh, you are asking if the drug must go into the blood across the blood-brain barrier and hit the liver? But there’s another interesting study that shows that the elimination half-life in the brain is slower.

OM : And that fits with data from Kapur’s lab at UCL, where he showed with PET scan data that aripiprazole has this tremendously long half-life, and its dopamine receptor occupancy one week later is the same as on day one.

MH : That drug has a long half-life.

OM : But it also hangs out in the brain for a long time. And so does its active metabolite and blah, blah, blah, blah, blah. So, there are plenty of reasons why things could hang around longer. The drugs— if the unwinding of the effects is longer? It messes with the human ability to make a causal inference.

MH : You can imagine the doctors reading the textbooks. It says mild and brief symptoms come on two days afterward. Someone’s in their office a month later.

OM : They look like death. They’re sick. And it doesn’t match the description in the textbook. So they think it can’t be withdrawn.

MH : It must be terrible mental illness, relapse, something else. You can see where the trouble comes from! They are guided by short-term studies that underestimate the withdrawal effects, which cause havoc in clinical practice! Well, this means that doctors are reading books that are not accurate for their patients. That’s huge trouble! We now have hundreds of thousands of people who have left their doctors and gone to online forums, just like me, because they find those forums more accurate and helpful than their doctors.

OM : Some people do have a mental illness—whatever that means and who are sick and could relapse.

MH : That exists. However, I want to problematize it a little bit. The vast majority of people who are prescribed antidepressants are prescribed by a GP or primary care physician; they’ve never seen a psychiatrist.

The vast majority of people who are prescribed antidepressants have mild to moderate conditions. The vast majority of people who have mild to moderate conditions see a GP or a primary care physician in the context of a major stressor: job loss, divorce, or physical illness.

OM : It’s hard to have a relapse of a specific divorce.

MH : In other words, it’s hard to have a relapse of a particular stressor in your life. Most people who are prescribed antidepressants do not have severe recurrent disorders . They have contextual stresses, which are in the past.

So, the idea of relapse , which is so prominent in doctors’ minds, isn’t often accurate, on average—the majority of starts on medicine are based on acute and non-recurring stressors.

OM : Essentially, this is my same argument about dubious “bipolar disorder.” If you treat everyone’s “bipolar disorder” with Seroquel , it works for their “mania” 100 percent of the time—because they never had the underlying disorder.

MH : Yes. Doctors get to convince themselves they did a great job of preventing the relapse of mania in someone who doesn’t have bipolar disorder.

OM : You argue that most people who are coming for help would be best served by someone helpful, listening, and supporting them psychosocially .

MH : I’m making two points. Number one, in most doctors’ minds, relapse is the horse, and withdrawal is the zebra. I think the data is clear.

OM : The overwhelming majority of people have been prescribed these medicines for mild problems that are in their lives?

MH : That the horse is withdrawal and relapses is the zebra for most people, some people, there’s some, there’s people out there with very severe conditions, but that’s not most people and do exactly what you’re saying.

The NICE guidelines in the UK, which evaluate cost-effectiveness, found that the most cost-effective treatment for even severe depression was problem-solving therapy:

That means you tell somebody what your three major problems are. What’s the first step to take for each one? And report back in two weeks on progress or barriers encountered. That’s more effective, and that’s more cost-effective than antidepressants. It’s more even cost-effective than various forms of psychotherapies.

I think that gets to the heart of the issue: people. It’s the things going on in their lives that tend to explain why people are miserable and anxious, and there are lots of ways to help them with that, of which I think only one is an antidepressant. And I believe that the way that antidepressants generally work is they numb people’s emotions.

I don’t know how much there are all these kinds of descriptions of biological effects and neurogenesis and inflammation that I was very Taken by, but I think when you ask most people on antidepressants, they say they feel emotionally restricted or numbed. And that’s why people can feel better in the short term, which is a bit different from fixing their problems.

OM : It’s fascinating that you’ve landed on the opposite end of the spectrum as I have in my career as a physician. I focus on very high-acuity individuals but have come to similar conclusions about the utility of these drugs.

In summary, if I am getting this right, in the world of primary care—where, on average, patients don’t have a severe chronic psychiatric problem—these drugs are causing one. In the world of high acuity problems, where you do have a severe problem, the drugs aren’t solving those. Riffing on what Dan Karlin once said to me, we perfected the algorithms to make these compounds as safe as water, at least initially. We have a generation of agents with the potency of water, but similar to withdrawal from water, that can be fatal, too.
