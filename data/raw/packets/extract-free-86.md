Neuroscience has advanced!

Earlier this week, I wrote about the Neurovalens FDA-approval for their at home vestibular neuromodulation device, Modius Spero, indicated in the treatment of PTSD. We have another FDA cleared treatment for PTSD today!

And, it wouldn't be The Frontier Psychiatrists if it wasn't about breaking TMS news!

So what's the deal? I caught up with the president and chief medical officer of Wave Neuroscience before the press release even went out! I was lucky enough to catch him at the clinical TMS Society annual meeting, and here is the transcript of our interview, hot off the presses!

Dr. Muir: Can you introduce yourself and tell us what Wave Neuroscience does?

Dr. Won: My name is Erik Won, and I’m the Chief Medical Officer for Wave Neuroscience. We do EEG-guided TMS, a personalized form of neurostimulation where we examine electrophysiologic patterns and stimulate based on deviations in the networks we observe.

Dr. Muir: What’s your background, and how did you come to this work?

Dr. Won: I’m from occupational and preventive medicine, but my passion for this technology came from an earlier chapter as a Navy flight surgeon. My unit had some of the first casualties of Operation Iraqi Freedom, and many of the people I served with came home struggling with post-traumatic stress. The silver lining, if there is one, is that our first FDA clearance is for PTSD.

The FDA Clearance

Dr. Muir: This clearance came through just yesterday. What exactly is the indication?

Dr. Won: The indication is broad: post-traumatic stress disorder, adjunctive use. The technology is called MERT in the label, which stands for Magnetic EEG-guided Resonance Therapy. It combines EEG with neuroanalytics to generate a stimulation protocol, which is then delivered via transcranial magnetic stimulation.

Dr. Muir: How long did it take to get here?

Dr. Won: It took a while. We had the COVID interruption, which affected the trial timeline. The RCT was 156 subjects, multi-site, double-blind, sham-controlled. Phase 1 was safety and Phase 2 looked at efficacy. We submitted under the breakthrough device designation, which accelerates review for technologies targeting areas with significant practice gaps or underserved communities. Once submitted, it was approximately 90 days on the FDA’s docket.

Dr. Muir: For our readers, why does FDA use the breakthrough pathway?

Dr. Won: When a technology targets an area with a clear practice gap or underserved population, FDA creates an accelerated review pathway. You move to the top of the queue. In our experience, they were highly responsive. When they sent questions, we tried to turn answers around within 24 hours. It was a positive, interactive dialogue throughout.

The Science and the Data

Dr. Muir: Can you describe what the unblinding moment was like?

Dr. Won: It’s truly anxiety-provoking. You’ve invested so much time, energy, and resources into data collection, and then it’s in the statistician’s hands. They want to examine it from every angle before presenting results, so in our case it took a few weeks to work through what we had. We approached the write-up the same way, trying to pressure-test every weak point and think from the FDA reviewer’s perspective.

Dr. Muir: The paper isn’t published yet?

Dr. Won: That’s correct, it’s in write-up now and we’re moving as fast as we can. But we can say: MERT is safe and effective for the adjunctive treatment of post-traumatic stress disorder, per the FDA.

What This Means for Patients

Dr. Muir: For families and individuals living with PTSD, what do you want them to hear?

Dr. Won: Hope. That’s the message. There is so much coming, including work in the psychedelic space and combination approaches. We just completed a study looking at combination therapy with ibogaine, and while I can’t share that data yet, what’s ahead is genuinely exciting.

One thing this trial made clear: the military community is a small fraction of the population living with trauma. The largest groups we’re serving are survivors of sexual trauma, domestic violence, and first responders. Thinking about what this clearance means for all of them is what keeps us going.

Dr. Muir: Is there a patient moment that sticks with you?

Dr. Won: Since we were blinded, we didn’t interview patients during the trial. But from clinics where I’ve seen the technology used, it’s often not the patient who says thank you, it’s the family member. A spouse saying, “This is the man I married ten years ago.” A child saying, “I got my father back.” That’s what carries you through the hard times.

On Industry, Mission, and the Long Game

Dr. Muir: For clinicians considering a move from direct patient care to industry, is it worth it?

Dr. Won: Sitting here now, yes. There are 13 million people with PTSD in the U.S. alone, and there’s a growing movement to drop the “D,” to reframe it as post-traumatic stress, a normal response to extraordinary circumstances. To bring relief to these communities at scale, especially when you’ve been personally touched by it, that’s gratifying in a way that’s hard to describe. But startup life is exactly what people say it is. The highs are high and the lows are very low. Nothing really prepares you for it.

My honest advice: make sure you have a real personal connection to what you’re working on. The science has to be sound, that’s non-negotiable, but when you have both the science and the mission behind you, it can push you through the really hard stretches.

I am a futurist. So was my friend Nolan Williams. When he took the big swing in his SNT clinical trial — along with myriad friends like Brandon Bentzley , Eli Cole , David Carreon , Angela Phillips , and others — to demonstrate SNT (a.k.a. SAINT, the trade name) was more effective than sham treatment in treatment-resistant depression, the big swing was changing a number of parameters compared to standard transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) all at once. His mentors, even Dr. Mark George, were, understandably, critical of this more-than-one-variable-changed science.

SAINT, as written about before , is a version of TMS for the rapid treatment of psychiatric illness, particularly (even suicidal) depression. When it works, it works rapidly. But the protocol changed more than one parameter. Brain stimulation, particularly TMS, has a lot of parameters for futzing. These include power, frequency, location, coil geometry, and the like. Usually in science we’d like to hold all other variables constant and modify one thing at a time so we can determine what matters. It is a process of iterative change, not step-function improvements.

Not the SAINT system! That is the Zeta Surgical Camera.

SAINT was the brand name for combining a number of changes into one new protocol that Dr. Williams guessed would be better by a lot, not just incrementally better by a little. At the end of the day, it wasn’t just about doing great science. It was about doing big splash science.

They combined the following changes into one clinical trial. They took TMS and:

• Increased the number of pulses per treatment (600 > 1800 puleses)

• Increased the density of treatment: 10 sessions every single day over five days, compared to once-daily treatments over six weeks

• Changed the spacing from once a day to once an hour

• Used functional MRI to determine the precise target for stimulation

• Calibrated the energy to the distance between the coil and the brain, which required the brain scan they already had

It worked.

It also left the question: which of these changes mattered most? Which mattered least? Where are the synergies, and where are the differences that don’t matter? The science we do will turn into a product, and to make it the most scalable product, we have to answer some of these questions. To generate the next, even more scalable product to help the most people, we need to tease these differences apart.

The easiest major variable to poke at was the fMRI imaging targeting piece, which seems kind of flashy. Like, is it really that much better to have a brain scan?

A new study, presented by Dr. Joe Taylor at the Clinical TMS Society annual meeting, was playfully called the “AINT trial.” You know — because it Ain’t SAINT.

Does the fMRI targeting matter? That’s what his research aimed to prove, one way or the other. The answer surprised me. What follows is my reporting on Joe’s talk, given just this past weekend.

Joe Taylor, M.D., Ph.D.

Dr. Taylor opened with sass: “We have some of the most sophisticated neuroimaging technology in the history of medicine,” he said, “and a large portion of the TMS field still localizes the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex with a tape measure.”

A literal tape measure, like clothing-tailors use.

His trial, The AINT Trial: Imaging- versus scalp-targeted accelerated TMS for Depression was designed to answer it. He enrolled 40 adults with treatment-resistant depression who met the SAINT RCT inclusion criteria.

Everyone received the same accelerated iTBS protocol: 10 sessions per day for 5 days, 1,800 pulses per session, real-time neuronavigation throughout. The only difference between groups was where the coil was pointed. This is identical to SNT in all parameters other than than targeting. That was the variable.

The imaging-based target was derived from depression circuit mapping work by Siddiqi, Drysdale, Fox, and colleagues — a publicly available pipeline integrating 14 causally-informed datasets, including lesion sites associated with depression onset, TMS sites associated with remission, and DBS sites associated with mood improvement. “All of these converge on a single circuit,” Taylor said. “That gives us causal evidence across modalities that we haven’t had before.”

The scalp-based target used the standard clinical method: anterior shift from the motor hotspot, navigated with the same equipment. The study was triple-blinded. A single unblinded research assistant determined targets and handed coordinates to the treatment team. “Participants could not guess their group assignment at better than chance,” Taylor noted. “The confidence in their guesses was essentially zero.”

Taylor described his going-in expectations with some candor. “I designed this study somewhat cynically,” he said. “I was expecting a null result. I was prepared for the fight of getting a negative finding published.”

That is not what happened.

Response rate in the connectivity-guided group was approximately 80%, versus 60% in the scalp-based group. Remission rates were approximately 65% versus 50%. The effect size attributable to targeting alone was 0.8 — large by conventional standards.

He also addressed the obvious alternative explanation: that the imaging targets were simply shifted in a consistent direction, and that moving the coil there without any individualization would produce the same result. “We entered the coordinates of every participant’s target into the statistical model,” he said. “The coordinates didn’t explain the outcome difference. The heat map showed considerable overlap between groups. The activity signal is contributing something beyond gross location.”

Taylor then introduced a metric he called the “number needed to scan”: how many patients require imaging to produce one additional responder. In this study, it was 5.

“Screening mammography requires roughly 360 scans to prevent one breast cancer death,” he said. “Lung cancer CT screening requires about 130. No other area of medicine runs double-blind trials asking whether imaging adds value to treatment targeting, because no other area of medicine seriously entertains skipping imaging when the target is an anatomical structure inside the brain.”

He was careful about what the study does and doesn’t establish. It is a 40-person single-site pilot with a one-month follow-up and no sham arm. The 42-minute fMRI acquisition is impractical for most clinical settings. The sgACC anti-correlation signal also appears less predictive in patients with significant anxiety or PTSD comorbidity. This describes the majority of patients presenting to clinical TMS practices.

“This doesn’t settle the question,” he said. “Replication with a larger sample, a sham arm, and longer follow-up is the obvious next step. But I think the burden of proof has shifted.”

So, Nolan was correct that the imaging-based targeting matters with very focal coils. He set an agenda for a generation of scientists. We all miss him every day. And we go on, to help others, and, we hope, each other too. Suicide prevention takes both science and humility. We learned Nolan never got SAINT, as confirmed by his own friends and family.

I’ll end with a call to action: Call your Congress person , and ask for accelerated TMS—including with imaging guidance, if your doctor thinks it appropriate— to be covered by all health plans, by law.

When my dear friend Amir Kalali, M.D. invites me to speak at an event, or simply to show up at any place on earth in his company, the right answer? It is yes.

Amir is a character. He is a psychiatrist by training, and has supervised hundreds of clinical trials in his life. He's also a deeply funny person, and gets away with a lot more puckishness then one might expect due to his British accent.

He is the founder of CNS Summit, a community and an event series that encourages people working in life sciences to work together, collaboratively, instead of in silos.

This attitude continues to this day, and that's why I went to China. I went in a spirit of collaboration. Before I write about what I've learned at the Summit, which is substantial, but not metabolized yet, I want to share a few observations about China.

I'm in the capital of the country. Beijing is at a scale that is massive. It’s emotional analog, I have heard among locals, is Washington D.C.

The only urban comparator I can think of in terms size is Mexico City, which has around 23 million people. Beijing has just over 22 million people. Much of the city is relatively new. I'll just take a picture of my window right now to prove it to you:

Some very old things are here as well, buried in the heart of the city, and, perhaps, in the hearts of the people.

China has billions of people, a number we don't emotionally engage with. When in Beijing, you encounter may people, in rapid sequence. You encounter enough people in a row to realize there are MORE people here than even New Yorkers, like your author, are used to processing.

There is a sense of controlled chaos, which I can best describe to only a fraction of my readers. It’s the opposite of Japan. Here, cars run red lights and grandmas make sure they can get past me on the stairs of the Great Wall. In Japan, people would die of shame for such behavior—this sense of personal fear of shame is absent in Beijing. Here, people are also restrained. However, these restrictions are the limits of human ambition and central authority. Which is not to say it’s impolite. It’s not. It’s a deeply gracious milieu, but with a sense that a rush is about to start for…something. There was a sense of safety on the edge of unease. It’s Pompeii …the week before?

To give you a sense of how gracious it was for me, I had my glasses break as I boarded the plane:

Within 30 minutes of arrival at the Beijing World Summit Wing hotel, my glasses had been whisked away, re-screwed, cleaned, and returned wrapped in gossamer paper. I was an honored guest, and my hosts made sure I knew it. And although I began to visit by noticing I was honored, I would leave feeling honored.

It’s also clear to everyone that you are being observed . There is even a visual history of electronic supervisors on the poles, as outdated cameras have more modern devices piles one on top of the other.

However, people also can leave unlocked bikes on the street. No one is going to take them—they would never get away with it? In Japan, this is policed by a sense or personal honor and fear of bone deep shame that keeps Kyoto free of single piece of trash. In Beijing, it feels like a fear of consequences—but I didn’t dare ask if this was true.

I edited myself quite a bit, verbally. I'm being careful about what I write now, too. People are mindful of their minders, and it feels…like part of the natural order.

China seems always on the brink, or brinks? Chinese history is replete with imperial intrigues, arranged murders, and fires…so many fires. These were often from lightning strikes. In The Forbidden City, a notable feature is the range of cauldrons for the emergency water supplies to put fires out:

China has on the order of 38,000 hospitals nationwide. If they read a paper, and there’s evidence to treat auditory hallucinations, or OCD, or anxiety, or insomnia, they can treat it. Thus, more data gets generated. More papers get published. More healing happens. It’s all about the price point at which authorities, no matter where they are, start worrying about how much money they’re spending. Transcranial magnetic stimulation, and neuromodulation generally, is a little bit like a genie. It can grant many wishes, but people get nervous about that possibility, if they don’t like letting genies out of bottles. China solves that problem by keeping the cost low.

I first learned about you as a recurring character when I was 16. I went to see a psychiatrist. You were news to me, then. We were stuck together.

I should apologize. I avoided you. I spent years building my life around fear. It was easier to think that you, bipolar disorder, were a villain. You could be controlled, tamed but never, ever, made peace with.

This was childish. In fairness, I was a child.

I remember the first speeding ticket, 75 in a 25. The first kiss I didn’t mean. The first night without sleep. The train tracks at 4 am. There are attitudes of others that would have been good to understand. You had lessons to teach.

Some will be uncomfortable with your grandiosity. Some will be drawn to it. I met my wife while a little “up”—thanks for that. Some people won’t keep up with your flight of ideas. I was one of those left scrambling to keep pace!

The energy, I might have been forewarned, is addictive. How much energy you filled me with and how much I could accomplish are unrelated. An unfortunate consequence of wanting to accomplish? The very drive to do so comes at the cost of never feeling like it’s done.

Anhedonia can visit triumph as easily as failure. An accomplishment is a fact, like the duration of a second—not a feeling about it.

When it comes to the expertise I built about you—that was, in the end, mostly done without your help. I became a consummate consumer of your biology, treatment, and phenomenology. I will admit I was ashamed to publish about you academically. It’s more comfortable being an expert in other people’s problems! I was ashamed, and even this letter is one which I feel uneasy about.

When the autoimmune illness—I probably had all along—announced itself, after three rounds of streptococcal infection in my mid-30s, small islands of red rash covered my body. A reason things had been so difficult, at least possibly, began to make sense.

My bipolar disorder and my “chronic tendonitis” had the same age of onset. The tendon pain was not best explained by overuse. A lot of my life had been built around avoiding you. I made decisions like English kings built castles—theoretically strategic. “We really need to ramp up our suicide prevention efforts.”

or

“Preventing suicide is really important.”

or

“Zero Suicide”

People say these things. I don't doubt that people mean them. But there are reasons, honest-to-goodness reasons, why suicide is hard to prevent. The system of care we have is faulty at a level that would be unacceptable in any other field of human endeavor.

The Math on Suicide is Merciless.

Suicide is a rare event. The population rate in the US is given in “per hundred thousand” units, cause that's how we deal with rare events. For the general population, it is around 17/100,000. Simply having major depressive disorder increases your risk less than one might think: that number is variously reported as 2,000/100,000 if you’ve had any psychiatric evaluation. Conversely, being admitted to an inpatient psychiatric unit with suicidal depression bumps that number way up, over a lifetime, to 6,000/100,000. To even get these numbers, we need Danish registry data, which falls out like this:

Thank Goodness for Danish Registries . 1

Since suicide is a rare event, even if it's differently rare in different cohorts, it's very hard to predict for any individual when it's “going to happen.” Keep in mind, the above numbers are referencing the lifetime risk, so it's really hard to know in any given moment what's the thing that led to death by suicide, versus any other moments in life, which didn't.

In clinical practice, and probably in the life of my non-clinician readers as well, hearing about suicidal thinking is pretty common. For every person who dies by suicide, over 275 people are thinking about suicide at any given moment. 13% of high school girls in the US in 2023 attempted to die by suicide according to the CDC.

So, despite suicide being a catastrophic end that we'd want to prevent, predicting the actual moment at which the end comes in the life of anyone, even somebody who's high risk, is frustratingly challenging. This turns out to be true about every death. We know, with 100% accuracy, than everyone will die. Knowing when anyone will die is, however, a very different undertaking.

Thus, the overwhelming majority of patients evaluated for suicidal ideation are unlikely to die by suicide. They're also highly unlikely to complete suicide imminently . The evaluation of suicidal patients is learning how to address those with an unimaginably high false-positive rate. Psychiatrists do not have a crystal ball. It may also be the case that when we use our broken crystal ball to admit people to inpatient psychiatric facilities, we're actually making the problem worse.

We built a world that discourages disclosure of suicidal ideation for the most at risk.

“Reach out for help, tell someone.”

“Just a few words can be the difference between life and death.”

Ok, maybe , but what happens after you find out someone is suicidal?

The process of being brought into a psychiatric hospital is traumatizing. It doesn't have to be, to be clear. But it is for many people. If you had to get stripped, searched, putting into gowns, locked in a room, separated from your family, all because you had a broken toe? Everyone would be limping.

The heroic people, in my opinion, who walk into the emergency room for help so that they can live even when their brains are screaming that they should die? They are subject to indignity after indignity. Limited visiting hours. You don't get to have a belt—well of course, not, because you could hang yourself? You don't get to have shoelaces. Of course, not, cause you could hang yourself!

This caution is not designed to be a great customer experience. Would anyone, if they didn't feel terrible, choose to go to a psychiatric hospital?

Maybe the answer is yes, but at least for myself , it's the last place I’d want to be.

I don't like McDonald's. I generally wouldn't choose to go to McDonald's, give other options. If my life was in jeopardy? I’d go get an Egg McMuffin. I don't know that I can say the same about psychiatric hospitals.

Would you go to McDonald's if you could be forced stay there? No matter how much you love McDonald's, if you even might be forced, by court order, to stay, and to eat, whatever they tell you…or they're gonna strap you down to a gurney until you relent? How carefully, then, are you thinking about the next trip to McDonald's?

Exactly.

Reducing Suicidal Means is Life Saving. We Refuse to Do It.

Feeling suicidal is often transient. Just ask an ER doctor! Just south of 10% of ER evaluations are for suicidal ideation, but most people are “treat and released.” The feeling that one wants to die can rise up, sometimes gradually, sometimes like a tidal wave, but in most cases? It passes. Putting time and friction between the thought that one should die and the act that could lead to death is a proven way to reduce suicide.

We’ve tried means restriction , that is restricting access to lethal means for people who might be thinking of it. We should do more of this. It would, however, take acknowledging realities like “ready gun ownership everywhere all the time” puts more people at risk of death by suicide, and that's just not the country we live in, here in America, even if it is the case of other places in the world.

We Do Not Measure, At Our Peril

Only 16% of psychiatrists us any rating scale whatsoever in the initial visit. Only 8% use one thereafter. Imagine trying to master flight without knowing how high you were above the ground. Or imagine trying to fix visual humanity without measuring which glasses we should stick on someone's face. Now stop, imagining, cause you're living in the mainstream of psychiatry. We built an entire field of medicine on vibe checks. It's going as well as you would imagine.

Your author loves a rapid acting treatment. Heck, we have a whole event series dedicated to them, with the next RAMHT event happening at Psych Congress, on September 14th, 2026. Get your tickets now!

RAMHT @ Psych Congress

We Put the Most Effective Treatments Last-In-Line.

Lithium has data for the prevention of suicide. One drug, Clozapine reduces risk for suicide in randomized trials. SAINT has signal that it may be able to reduce suicidal ideation rapidly . TMS and Ketamine…you get my point. The most prescribed medicines in psychiatry are benzodiazepines, which might increase risk, and oral antidepressants, which at least increase the risk of thinking about suicide in youth . We put the good stuff behind the glass, with the lock and key, and keep the dangerous or infective treatments first line.

At best, oh gosh, we're going have to wait for prior authorization! Don’t get me started.

Suicide Prevention, at Present, is a Bad Product

Suicide is not a disease. It's a final common pathway. The problem is human suffering. We are not asking the right questions. Instead of asking “how we can prevent suicide,” we should, perhaps, be asking how we can identify suffering, ameliorate, suffering, prevent suffering, and—here is my provocative bit— do so in a way that's appealing to everybody?

Preventing suicide is only hard, in part, because the math is challenging. It's a rare event that we're bad at recognizing and catastrophically bad at addressing when we recognize the risk. But we also need to recognize that many of us didn’t understand the assignment is the prevent suicide so that the risk goes down, relentlessly.

Let's make getting help for suicide a great customer experience. Then, everyone will want suicide prevention treatment. Because, and I know this sounds outrageous, but having a great experience with a suicide prevention approach would make you more likely to prevent suicide for yourself. You might even recommend suicide prevention to a friend.
