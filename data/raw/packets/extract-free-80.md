Your editor loves Ben Spielberg . He’s a contributor to this newsletter and a Brain Medicine enthusiast, in addition to his roles as an executive at Stella and Soulstice , which provides high-quality TMS training. Today, he brings us part one of a series on “how to open a TMS clinic.” Take it away, Ben!

If you’re a fellow reader of this newsletter, you’ve probably heard about a neat little tool called Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation . Perhaps you’ve also heard of ketamine, Spravato, or the litany of other psychedelic drugs lining up for FDA approval in the coming years. The aforementioned treatments fall under the category of “interventional” psychiatry, because the patient isn’t just prescribed oral medication to take home on a daily basis–rather they come into an office for non-invasive procedures. Compared to the current tools used in psychiatry for mood disorders, interventional treatments work much better, much faster, and are much safer. I built a multi-location facility dedicated to interventional psychiatry, which I sold in August 2025. In this newsletter, I’ll give you some tips on how you can open your own clinic that specializes in interventional psychiatry.

Here’s Ben on Dr. Drew’s podcast in January 2020, courtesy of the PR agency I mention

A. Obtain medical license. If you don’t have a medical license, instead you will need a really good lawyer, a lot of cash, and also someone with a medical license. This someone can be your Medical Director, which is a job, or they can own the medical entity that provides services, which is not a job. If you choose to obtain a medical license, you’ll want to attend medical school, match into residency for psychiatry, and maybe if you get lucky, you’ll learn about interventional care in your last year or two of residency. If you choose not to obtain a medical license, be prepared to open a bunch of bank accounts and explain what an “MSO” is over and over again for the next decade.

B. Find an office space. Your office space is very important, because moving offices is very annoying. Office spaces can be very expensive, and sometimes they can be surprisingly affordable. It’s always better to find an office that is more affordable than it is expensive. Our Early Fallen Interventional Forefathers have warned us–via bankruptcy filings and investor lawsuits–that the inclination to rent the most ornate, beautiful office one can find is not always the best business strategy.

When you look for an office space, you want to consider both your local competition and how close you are to your patients. In Los Angeles, people with depression are not willing to travel far. They are rarely willing to leave their homes, most of the time. They have to really believe that there’s a chance that they can feel better from magnetic brain stimulation or an infusion of a drug they were convinced was only used as an animal tranquilizer until maybe 30 minutes ago. In Los Angeles, I planned for no one traveling more than 30 minutes, which is about a 5-mile radius. The less competition you have, the larger your total catchment area can be. If you’re in a more rural area where people routinely drive long distances to seek care, you can also have a larger catchment area. However, you must also consider your service mix. Will you be offering treatments like ketamine or Spravato? Will you plan to offer drugs like COMP-360 or BPL-003 once they are FDA-approved? If so, you need to consider your location more carefully. Patients cannot drive themselves home on drugs that impair their mental status. Instead, they must pick up a friend, family member, rideshare, or autonomous vehicle (which may or may not be preferable, depending on your patient’s relationship with their friends, family, and strangers). This means that you also have to consider how expensive these rides might be for your patients.

Four important tips when choosing an office space:

• Empty space is dead space. It’s not a revenue-generating space. You want as many treatment rooms as possible with as little open space as possible.

• Soundproofing is important, and also, there’s no such thing as soundproofing. When you look at office space, walk into a treatment room and shut the door. Try to listen to what’s going on outside the door. Then, do it again in the hallway and listen to what’s going on inside the room. It will never be perfect, but sometimes it can be a lot less bad than others. You don’t want a patient on Spravato being disrupted by a therapy session or disturbed by the delightful pulsing of an intermittent theta burst stimulation protocol next door.

• Don’t do build-outs. Sometimes you will find an office with “good bones” but that needs a lot of work. Don’t do it. It will take twice as long as you think. Light work is fine: painting, installing or removing a toilet in an area with a sink, or fixing ceiling tiles are acceptable, as those will be completed in a few days of actual work. If you’re talking about moving walls, just go and find a different space.

• Negotiate your lease. In commercial real estate, you typically agree on an LOI and then sign a fairly standard version of a commercial lease. The LOI contains the important terms. Landlords will always try to get you to personally guarantee the space, but this is negotiable. Sometimes they will remove the personal guarantee in exchange for an additional deposit. Other times, they’ll remove the personal guarantee after a certain timeframe (e.g., 12 months). It never hurts to ask, but make sure to ask at the LOI stage.

C. Patient Acquisition . How do you plan on finding patients? Patients need to know that you exist, and they also need to know that interventional treatments are safe and effective options for them, which they usually do not know. How will you get the word out? You can use digital marketing strategies like Google Ads or Meta Ads, which sometimes work. If you also work at a large institution, sometimes you can spread the word to your patients and colleagues at that institution. If you’re a big part of your local medical community, you can also spread the word that way. If you’re a stranger, you have to figure out a way to get the word out. When I started Bespoke Treatment (at the time called TMS & Brain Health), we struggled to find patients early on because we weren’t plugged into our local community and patients didn’t know who we were or what TMS was. With the bank account dwindling closer and closer to zero in October 2019, I made a risky decision to put our remaining marketing budget for the year into a 3-month retainer with a PR agency. While I was mentally preparing to run out of cash and shut the clinic down, a Christmas miracle happened: our local NBC network aired a segment about us on Christmas Eve . We were flooded with phone calls, and a lot of patients with very severe depression learned that we exist, and that we had options for them that work.

Practicing MTs in the only office that would give Ben a 1 year lease in June 2019

In many ways, patient acquisition is actually the most important part of the business. A lot of patients means a lot of revenue, and a lot of revenue can solve a lot of little problems. Without a lot of revenue, little problems can be exacerbated and then feel like big problems. With good patient acquisition, you can have an imperfect intake process, a bad CRM, and less-than-stellar insurance contracts. When you’re not good at finding new patients, you need to make sure you’re being hyper-efficient while still providing a good patient experience.

There are agencies that exist that can help to figure out what the best strategy is for you. Some of them are extremely well-versed in interventional psychiatry, and some are generic marketing and advertising agencies. If you choose the latter, you’ll have to really explain what TMS is a few times and truly understand the frustrations, goals, and interests of your ideal patient deeply. You also need a backup plan, because sometimes things that work really well just sort of… stop working. I enjoy writing and puzzles, so I spent a lot of time on Search Engine Optimization (SEO), which brought patients in for years, until Google changed its search algorithm. Now, AI summaries surpass organic results, and people click through to websites less.

One tip for working with third-party marketing vendors: when vetting, I like to ask about their average client size and the price range of their clients. I do this because I don’t want to be the smallest client they serve, as it can be difficult to command attention. I also don’t love being the largest client they serve, as it might mean they’re trying to punch above their weight class. However, there are also valid reasons to ignore this advice.

Overall, it will be most frugal of you to deeply consider how your patients will find you, and what happens when that plan fails.

Community lecture to local functional medicine docs in September 2019

4. Accounting is important . When you provide medical services, you can account for income in one of two ways: you can realize income on a “cash basis,” which means that income is only recorded as it hits your bank account. This is common in small businesses and pass-through businesses, where the end profit and loss numbers flow into the owners’ tax returns. Alternatively, you can choose to realize income on an “accrual basis,” which means that income is recorded when the service is rendered.

Just to illustrate, let’s pretend you take insurance and calibrated a new TMS patient (reflecting 1 unit of CPT code 90867 at an example rate of $500). If you use cash accounting, you won’t “make” $500 for anywhere from 14 to 60 days. If you use accrual accounting, you just “made” $500. It doesn’t matter that it hasn’t hit your bank account yet. It means you do have to pay more attention to cash flow, which is kind of like blood flow if your business were a body.

If you plan on sweeping the profits out of the business into your personal bank account, cash accounting is fine. If you start applying for loans, raising money, or building a business to sell, you should use accrual accounting. It’s quite annoying and painful to switch from one methodology to the other, so make sure you understand your goals and think about accounting early.

In summary, there is much to consider when opening your own clinic. In part 2, I’ll provide important considerations and tips for staffing your interventional clinic. If you want tips outside of this, I sell a TMS certification course for business owners with templates, dashboards, and policies that I wish I had when I started my clinics.

Readers of this newsletter are familiar with my coverage of the Change Healthcare Data Breach . Many health professionals got into financial trouble because of this catastrophe. Today’s column is with one therapist who fought the law, and, surprisingly, won. This is a conversation with therapist Annalisa Hansen , LPCC, MBA, BFA. It’s also a guide for health professionals on the value of advocating for oneself in defiance of Goliath.

You will remember her from this great podcast, also.

Can you introduce yourself?

My name is Annalisa Hansen. I’m an LPCC — a licensed professional clinical counselor — in several different states. I also have an MBA in analytics and a fine arts degree, and I played French horn.

You’re the triple threat that no one expected.

The brass section represents.

MBA, master’s-level clinical degree, and a Bachelor of Fine Arts. What kind of jobs have you done with all that?

After I got my art degree — which was not the plan, by the way. My plan was actually to get an education degree. Then, we’ll just say that things happened with the administration at my college, and I needed to graduate on time because I could not pay cash out of pocket for a fifth year of private liberal arts college. So I scraped together as many credits as I had, and that ended up being fine arts.

After college, the job market was pretty bad, and I realized I didn’t have any marketable skills on a resume. I’d also missed the cutoff for the GRE. So I looked around for graduate programs in my area that didn’t require it, and I found a brand new art therapy program at the Adler Graduate School in Richfield, which is a suburb of Minneapolis. They said, okay, you don’t have a psychology background, but you do have an art background, so we’ll take you.

You trained as an artist, and that led to becoming a therapist?

I worked in community mental health in the Twin Cities right after graduation. Minnesota has an oversupply of therapists — we call it the land of ten thousand treatment centers. The alcoholism rate is second only to Wisconsin; it’s a combination of culture and long winters, and given the heavily Scandinavian population, there’s a very significant cultural tendency toward depression, seasonal affective disorder, and bipolar spectrum illness. So there have always been a huge number of psychology and therapy training programs, which means an oversaturated market. It was very difficult to get any sort of livable, post-graduate supervised experience.

I ended up doing in-home work with the SPMI population — severe and persistent mental illness. People who were either permanently disabled via mental illness or substance abuse, or both, people who truly could not function without a wide swath of supportive services.

Not really therapy — essentially amateur nursing?

Yeah. And because it was an oversaturated market and everybody needs that first gig, and your license hours have to be signed off on by both the place you work and your clinical supervisor, they have complete control over you. You end up in very exploitative or abusive working situations.

I also wasn’t getting paid for mileage, and Minnesota gets to subzero temperatures. I’d drive twenty miles to someone’s place, wait outside their door, calling them, knocking. It’s twenty degrees below zero. I have permanent nerve damage in some of my fingers and toes from being in subzero temperatures waiting for somebody to answer the door — who then doesn’t answer the door, and then I can’t count that hour toward my supervised hours.

So I finally managed to scrape together all of my hours, got my license, and a woman I knew said, once you get your license, you should apply to work at Cigna — they’re always desperate for workers because it’s a very high turnover position.

I said, okay, is it indoors with working heat and do I get access to a bathroom?

That became a perk for you?

And water. Drinkable water. The bathroom was actually the main draw.

What did you do there?

I interviewed for their 24/7 triage line. One of the things people don’t know is that most insurance companies actually do have a 24/7 crisis line for customers, staffed by licensed nurses, doctors, or therapists who can handle and triage mental health and substance abuse crises in real time. It is usually much better than calling your state or local crisis hotline. Optum has one, Cigna has one, Anthem has one, and Aetna has one.

We also did pre-authorizations for “higher levels of care” — residential treatment, inpatient hospitalization. Those have to be reviewed by a licensed reviewer, because in most states, you are not allowed to deny requests like that unless you have an equal or same-state license for whatever level of care is being requested.

Eventually, I got a few promotions and ended up as the Western US network market manager for the behavioral health network at Cigna, where I maintained, grew, or remediated our behavioral health network for the Western US. And in one of my UM/UR roles, Cigna paid for an MBA through the University of Hartford. My focus was analytics and healthcare analytics, because my job was all about: how do I get these network numbers to stack up? Where do I find providers? What nooks and crannies can I shake providers out of?

Then COVID hit, and I left Cigna to go into private practice. And something that I realized very quickly is that I have way, way, way more insurance UM/UR compliance and parity law knowledge than the average bear. That was actually quite shocking to me coming out of insurance land. Most therapists seemed very intimidated and very scared of insurance and how it works. I really want to educate people about it, because knowledge is power.

And then Change Healthcare happened to all of us. What’s the summary from your perspective?

Change Healthcare includes an electronic data interchange — an EDI. They are the largest EDI by volume for the entire United States. If you have an electronic health claim in the US — dentistry, chiropractic, physical therapy, behavioral health, or hospital claims — there is a greater than 50% chance it runs through Change Healthcare’s data servers.

Think of them like a toll bridge. Change Healthcare owns the bridge that everybody needs to use. And they didn’t just build a bridge — they acquired all the smaller bridges. Hundreds of smaller companies over the years, all rolling into Change Healthcare’s data systems.

They’re essentially a public utility, but private.

Yes. It’s as if somebody said, you know what, it’d be really good if there was a bridge here, so they built one, and then everybody started using it, and then they acquired all the other bridges. And the data systems they used were quite old and quite out of date, and they knew this. United was also aware of this when they did the $13 billion acquisition.

After the takeover — and this is scuttlebutt from people I know, so we can’t confirm it — United did not want to update or maintain the servers, and they were actively laying off senior staff. Particularly in the security team.

Annalisa Hansen.

They laid off most of the security team, as I understand it.

Any legacy employee from before the takeover — United didn’t want to maintain their contracts or keep paying them. So they laid off the entire security team, basically anybody with that old institutional knowledge. It was a ramshackle, bare-bones operation owned by the largest healthcare company by market cap in the world.

They had been told through security audits, over and over again, that they need to enable multi-factor authentication. Everybody uses it — you use it for your bank, your Starbucks account. And United Healthcare did not want to deal with system downtime or spend the money to enable two-factor authentication on one of the most critical healthcare data systems in the entire country. A system that has clinical notes, your diagnosis, your entire medical history, your Social Security number, and is the pharmacy benefit manager for all active duty military, including their diagnoses and home addresses.

So what happened?

On February 12th, 2024, a ransomware group — ALPHV, also known as BlackCat — breached the network using the credentials of a low-level employee found in a group chat that advertises stolen credentials to cybercriminals. They got into a remote portal, gave themselves admin capabilities, installed malware and backdoors, and had unfettered, unlimited access to all of Change Healthcare’s data and records for over a week.

On February 21st — nine days after — they deployed the ransomware, which blocked United’s access to the Change Healthcare network. And United did not know that their entire system had been compromised for over a week until after the ransomware block happened.

More articles on this disaster, written at the time, are linked at the end.

In response, they shut down the entire platform. All healthcare claims run through this one system, this one single point of failure. If you turn that off, insurance claims cannot be paid, cannot be processed. The bridge is closed.

You thought the toll bridge with the troll was bad…until the troll went on break.

Right, exactly. The troll is just on a smoke break, leaning outside of the toll booth, refusing to let anybody through.

And then the then-CEO of UnitedHealth Group, Andrew Witty, paid the ransom — in Bitcoin, apparently to the wrong group, who said, " Hey, we don’t actually have this data, and even if we did, we wouldn’t give it back, but thanks for the millions of dollars, bye.

What happened to you specifically?

Magellan — now something else — was my primary payer. They had been, shortly before the breach, moving entirely to a third-party platform called Avality.com, which was 100% reliant on Change Healthcare. They had just sunsetted their own internal system. The timing was exquisite.

For 4 to 6 months, I received no payments from my Magellan clients. I floated about $18,000 of personal credit card debt just to get through. Office rent in Beverly Hills doesn’t stop for a data breach. My water bill doesn’t stop. I was not having a good time. I was having a Baja bad time.

My 2024 numbers: I billed 322 claims to Magellan that year. I was paid $9,465. I should have received approximately $40,000. My timely payment rate dropped from 98% across 2022 and 2023 to 30.8%. I estimate I lost over $72,000 worth of business that year.

How did you float it?

Personal credit card. And the interest was scary. The interest building up was really worrying me. I’ve always been very financially responsible — I’ve never had a credit card balance that high. It kept me up. It was terrifying.

Optum/Change also offered what they called “bridge loans” — short-term, interest-free loans to float your business until you were, quote, made whole. I read the terms. “Made whole” to them meant turning the system back on. Then they wanted all the money paid back within 45 days of the system coming back online, whether or not those underlying claims ever actually got paid. That has led to practices going bankrupt, many providers getting chased by debt collectors or having their credit destroyed, because a lot of them still haven’t gotten paid for those missing claims, and yet they owe a debt to Optum. I am very glad I did not take that loan.

What were the insurance companies actually doing while this was happening?

Magellan told providers they couldn’t submit paper claims and would have to wait for the system to come back online.

That was false .

Under Medicare rules, I am allowed to send a paper claim whenever I damn well feel like it. They have to accept it. They might not like it, but they do have to — it is the law. So I was submitting everything by paper, by mail, by fax, keeping photocopies and scanned copies of every single form.

Magellan said they didn’t have a fax number. That’s not true — they have a fax number. One person told me they had shredded all of the claims I sent via fax. That’s also illegal.

What Magellan was doing instead — and I am alleging this — is they were abusing the LOI process. The “lack of information” process. Under the California Insurance Code, the Knox-Keene Act, and federal ERISA, claims should be paid within 30 calendar days unless the insurance company lacks sufficient information to determine payer liability. If they don’t, they send a lack-of-information letter back to the provider asking for more information, and that buys them an additional 60 calendar days to research the claim.

These letters can only be sent by physical mail. There is no online portal to view them. If you don’t respond within 60 days — you were on vacation, you missed the letter, you lost it — the insurance carrier can administratively deny that claim, and they don’t need a licensed reviewer to do it.

Approximately 69% of all of my claims after the data breach received LOI letters. I had not changed anything in my billing process. Nothing had changed from the years when I had a 98% clearance rate. And they were saying, oh, you forgot a date of birth — I have photocopies proving I didn’t. You forgot your tax ID. Your NPI. All completely spurious, all wrong, all fabricated.

But because they did that, it buys them 60 days. Then I have to resubmit, and usually I’d get the claim denied administratively — “you have billed this claim too many times” — and then I’d have to file a formal appeal. A formal appeal gives them 365 calendar days to determine payer liability. Which is why I have claims from July 2024 that were paid in February 2026.

Did they pay with interest?

No.

There’s a law about that. Prompt pay — 2% per month compounded , in New York anyway.

Oh, you sweet summer child. If they paid interest, that would be an admission that they paid the claim late.

So, you eventually decided to take them to court?

Yes. In California, you can sue in small claims court for up to $6,250 as a business and $12,500 as an individual. And you cannot use lawyers in California small claims. Both parties have to show up for themselves.

Now, every single insurance contract ever is going to have a mandatory arbitration clause. Typically, if a provider wants to sue an insurance company, we have to go through mandatory arbitration first — where you hire an arbitrator from JAMS, usually a retired judge, who charges $500 to $800 an hour. The entire idea is that it financially drains the individual provider before any resolution is reached. I can’t afford to pay somebody $800 an hour, especially if the insurance company is being as obstinate as possible.

Small claims bypasses mandatory arbitration. No lawyers. No $800-an-hour arbitrators. You walk in, you present your case, they present theirs.

And?

I won. Full damages.

Magellan had made a very low-ball settlement offer earlier in the process, but it was so low that I didn’t believe it was sufficient. Change Healthcare didn’t engage at all. When the court date came, they had no representative present.

There’s no check in the mail yet — they have 30 calendar days to appeal — but I won.

What should readers of this newsletter actually do?

A few things:

First , look up the small claims process for your home state and county. Dollar limits vary. Some states, like California, bar lawyers on both sides; not all do.

Second , document everything, starting now. Every paper claim, every LOI letter, every denial, every appeal, every fax confirmation. The LOI process is specifically designed to exploit providers who don’t keep records. Keep every piece of paper. I know it’s onerous. My home office is a disaster. But if you don’t have those documents, you’re sunk.

Third , file paper claims within 30 days of service. Insurers cannot legally refuse a CMS 1500 form. They may tell you they can. They’re wrong.

Fourth , know the language. Change Healthcare insists on calling what happened a “cyberattack,” as if they are the victims, rather than a “data breach,” which implies negligence in protecting the data. That distinction has legal meaning, and it’s why they insist on it.

Fifth , the bridge loan was a trap. If you took it and are now in trouble, look into the class action being pursued by Seger Weiss LLP.

What’s with that case?

And finally , there’s a federal ruling most providers don’t know about. On December 19th, 2025, Judge Donovan Frank, who is overseeing the multidistrict litigation for the Change Healthcare data breach in the District of Minnesota, ruled that all out-of-state litigants are released from the MDL and may sue in their home state under their own state’s laws. Previously, Change had been successfully arguing that all lawsuits had to be consolidated in Minnesota. Judge Frank ruled: “No, if you want to bring a lawsuit against Change Healthcare in your home state, go for it .”

The gates are wide open.

Correct. If you want to sue in California, sue in California. Texas, sue in Texas. And I’m seeing a lot of cases out of Texas — they seem very salty about this.

Rather than a class action giant shark attack, think of it as being stung repeatedly by a million tiny bees. Class action takes years. Small claims court is much faster.

One of the unspoken features of medical training is the depth of the relationship relationships we create in the process. Medical training happens throughout the process of medical school, followed by residency. Residency is a busy time, you are working as a physician, but learning to become and attending. Many of the lessons are from senior doctors, but most I would argue or actually from both your slightly more senior colleagues—”near peer” mentors, and, at least in my case, Nursing staff. I did my general psychiatry residency at the Zucker Hillside Hospital, in Outer Queens, New York. This is a good program, but it's not a famous program—many psychiatric programs these days promise Work-life balance, and Hillside, at least at the time, was not one of these. It promised what I craved—working endlessly with a gazillion patients. One of the more formative experiences I got to have was in my role as a Senior Psychiatrist on call. This was a moonlighting gig where you got paid a little bit of extra money to work six more hours in the night. You became the doctor for the whole hospital. These shifts were open to attending attending physicians, and to 4th year residents. On call with you were two junior residents, a first year and a second year. You had to learn to manage them, and the entire hospital, no matter what was happening. One of the more impressive junior residents I ever worked with was the author of today's article. is a gifted healer. He was also always generative, thoughtful, and I'm thrilled to see him here on Substack. I take a kind of paternal pride, or maybe a bigish brother pride, seeing my former junior resident writing for the rest of the world. I promise you, I didn't teach him much. Before I ever walk into the waiting room or click “start meeting,” the appointment has already started.

A few minutes before the hour, I am at my desk reading whatever the patient chose to put on paper. This is my first exposure to their world.

Sometimes it is minimal.

“Anxiety. Need refill.”

Sometimes it unfolds across years. Medications. Hospitalizations. Losses. Frustrations with prior doctors. Occasionally it reads like a personal essay that has been waiting for an audience.

I read it carefully, but I do not treat it as the whole story. It is a first draft. It reflects what felt most urgent or most sayable at the moment it was written. People edit themselves before they arrive. What they lead with, what they soften, what they omit. That editing is meaningful.

I walk to the waiting room to meet the patient. Some are already watching the door. Some look anywhere else. Some stand and extend a hand before I finish saying their name. Others move slowly, as if conserving something. The waiting room is an in-between space. It sits between public identity and private disclosure.

When we step into my office, that threshold does not disappear immediately.

I begin with simple questions. Allergies. Medical conditions. Neutral but necessary. They allow both of us to settle into the room.

What happens next is less scripted and more sensed. If someone seems like they have been holding something in, I ask what brought them here. If there is guardedness, I may move further back into history. If there is uncertainty, I widen the frame. Tell me about yourself. Where you grew up. Your family.

The first appointment is calibration. I adjust pace and depth based on the person in front of me.

There is a balance between listening and directing. Patients need space to speak, but the conversation also has to move toward clarity. The pace cannot be predetermined. It emerges from what unfolds in the room.

Emotional tone guides the movement. If something feels immediate, I slow down and stay with it. If something feels distant, I approach it gradually. When words and emotional tone do not align, that discrepancy becomes clinically important.

This is where the shift occurs. From description to understanding.

Description identifies symptoms.

Understanding situates them.

It asks how they developed, what maintains them, and why they are presenting in this particular way at this particular time.

Good treatment follows from that level of clarity. Treatment is not chosen by label alone. Two people may look identical on paper and require entirely different approaches.

One person’s anxiety may reflect longstanding patterns of avoidance. Another’s may follow a recent disruption. On paper, both read as anxiety. Clinically, they are not the same problem.

Clarity about cause, context, and pattern makes the difference between selecting a treatment and tailoring one. Without that clarity, treatment risks addressing what is most visible rather than what is most central.

That is why the first appointment is not about producing answers quickly. It is about defining the problem with enough precision that the next step is guided by understanding rather than urgency.

This is the work that happens before any prescription is written.

Dear readers,

Today is a most auspicious day. The temperature has warmed. There is coming in like a lion , and a lamb-esque exit on the horizon. That might be April? Regardless, the seasons are changing. What was “psychiatric care” is changing, too.

The language has to change! No longer can we consider fiddling with medicines a job. That is right—psychopharmacology is already dead, it just might take a while to stop wiggling.

“Chemical imbalances” are the midcentury version of the four humors—a pleasant and reassuring explanation for observed experiences. All models are wrong, some are useful, my teachers like to say. So too with “chemical imbalances” fixed with chemicals. These will go the way of humoral imbalances, addressed with bloodletting.

I have a new framework to unveil. It’s almost certainly wrong in some details, but already more useful for healing our patients—just look at the 78% remission of depression rate in “treatment resistant depression” seen in the recent SWIFT trial I co-authored.

How did we get the best outcomes in clinical trials for traditionally “difficult-to-treat” conditions? We used a different way of understanding the problem.

Flight is difficult to achieve with whirly-gigs and just “jumping higher.” Planes with fixed wings turned out to be a game-changer. As we better understood physics and aerodynamics, we took to the air, and flight became commonplace.

Anesthesia changed surgery—it was possible before, but so limited by the inability to alter consciousness selectively that it was rare and horrifying.

In the suffering of the human mind, understanding has shifted from neurotransmitters and chemicals alone to networks of brain circuits. Hundreds of years of scientific research have brought us to this point. We found evidence that strokes in certain areas of the brain can present with the same deficits as strokes in other areas. This led to the idea that a brain circuit, in which those physical regions participated, might exist. Advanced brain imaging lets us see this happen in real time on fMRI.

© Owen Muir, 2026

Movements and moods are both outputs of this same brain—could it be possible that changing how a circuit fires could alter a movement? Well, this has been demonstrated in parkinson’s disease with TMS , and this was demonstrated again in Nature this past month. Pretty soon, we will be talking about “ Spatial single-cell multiomics reveals peripheral immune dysfunction in Parkinson’s and inflammatory bowel disease. ” Oh, wait. We already are . I would be lying to you if I said I know what a multiomic is, but darn it, it sounds like science!

This circuit-based understanding has moved from movement disorders to traditionally “psychiatric” disorders—we are using Deep Brain Stimulation in OCD . Circuit-based understandings are offering relief in depression. PTSD , ADHD , and more disorders are producing evidence that they have circuts than can be modulated.

I said before that all models are wrong, and some are useful. We need to beware our own ardent convictions—this time, we got it really right! Pictures of brains are very convincing, after all.

When we need a new paradigm, it’s useful to look at successful, older pillars of humanity’s most remarkable enterprise—modern medicine.

We used to have physicians and surgeons, and that was it. Centuries of work and endless philanthropy by John D. Rockefeller later ? We had a Flexner report on medical training, and the system of medical training developed by Halstead and Osler at Hopkins was quietly spread throughout the land, funded by one guy. Oh, and he funded all of public health education next.

We built a taxonomy of medicine, and in it, neurology and psychiatry were separate. We added specialties as we discovered we needed specialized training for one task or another. Some, like the domain of sleep, required multiple professionals working together—so Sleep Medicine became the domain of those who had first trained as psychiatrists, neurologists, or pulmonary physicians. Others, like Emergency Medicine, were born when the workflow—saving the person’s life in an emergency—required a new specialty. This pattern is pretty straightforward—when the domain of knowledge and skill becomes specialized to a degree that a generalist isn’t best suited for the task, a new specialty is formed.

This is happening again. It’s called Brain Medicine , thanks to Josh Brown, M.D:

Unprecedented knowledge of the brain is inevitably contributing to the convergence of neurology and psychiatry. However, clinical training continues to follow a divergent approach established in the 19th century. An etiological approach will continue to shift more psychiatric patients to the care of neurologists who are untrained in psychiatric management. At the same time, this new era of diagnostic biomarkers and neuroscience-based precision treatments requires skills not readily available to those trained in psychiatry.

It’s a new discipline, where psychiatry, neurology, informatics, neuroradiology, neurosurgery, anesthesia work with industrial-scale machine learning to alleviate human suffering by durably heal by “tuning up” brain networks.

And it’s here, already.

When things start to go badly for your body, it will be hard to understand that process from the inside. When my dad had a stroke— a lateral medullary stroke, with an infarction of the vertebral artery (the clot formed right below the posterior inferior cerebellar artery, or PICA), it was bad. These are the blood vessels your brainstem needs for crucial functions. When we think of stroke, it’s usually about how it impacts us, as people who have to interact with the person who had the infarction. “ They can’t talk.” This often translates roughly into “they can’t talk to me .”

Lateral Medullary Syndrome (also known as Wallenberg syndrome) is a constellation of symptoms that sucks for YOU, as the person might experience them, and is the name we have for a stroke to the tiny blood vessels in the back of the brain.

Art Muir, taken with an improvised pinhole camera. (C) Owen Muir.

Here is how high-end that posterior brain real estate is:

You lose the ability to swallow. Your voice becomes hoarse. You can have vertigo, nausea, and nystagmus (that is, difficulty with gaze to the side). Half of your face can lose the sensory experience of pain and temperature. Half of your body, on the same side of the stroke, has “ataxia”—the inability to coordinate smooth movements. You can have a pupil lose the ability to contract on the same side of your head as the stroke, called Horner Syndrome.

Horner’s can also happen transiently, for example, in this photo, where I had a stellate ganglion block (SGB), but you can tell my pupils are not the same size:

The pupil on the right side of the photo is larger than the one on the left.

There is quite a bit of “motor control” we can lose in the medulla, right below the pons in the brain stem. This is where my dad had one of his several strokes, but it was the bad one.

He was paralyzed on one side of his body, if not completely. When you can’t swallow, it’s a problem, because it makes it very easy to accidentally have liquid or food go down the windpipe—we call this “aspiration.” Food and beverages shouldn’t go into your lungs. This leads to infection and can kill you. Thus, it’s common for people, including my dad, to have a surgical procedure recommended when this occurs, to get a tube implanted. This way, they can pump nutrition into their stomach directly. This is called a PEG tube. This is a step taken after a nasogastric tube, which allows “feeds” of grim liquid to be pumped into your nose, spirited past your windpipe, directly into the esophagus. Thus, it can reach your stomach without the risks described above.

None of this is dignified. Much of it is something people outside your body can observe. The sensory experiences, however, are very much a “you” thing and are hard for others to understand.

This is a process for the human experiencing it. From the outside, it seems like an event .

This stroke, as described above, happened two days after I took the USMLE Step One. I had a brand new girlfriend of just two weeks 1 . I crashed my car on the way to the hospital.

My dad, however, won big . This may have been the best thing to ever happen to him.

You see, I was two weeks into a new girlfriend. He was two weeks into being…separated. My dad used to drink a lot. Like, entire bottles of wine a day. Hard liquor, too. Decades of alcohol use, done in secret, done in the basement. My mom , after a lifetime of this, over 30 years, had finally had it. My dad had already had one Left MCA stroke about a decade earlier, and was continuing to drink. This was not his doctor’s recommendation. It was not my recommendation. It was not his wife's recommendation.

Two weeks before the day that bloodclot hit, he had been asked to leave our family home, and was camped out, alone, in a cabin on a lake that a friend graciously agreed to rent him until he figured out whatever he was gonna figure out. Separation was being planned.

He was extremely lucky that the one hand that was functional after the stroke was the hand that was positioned near his phone when he went down. He was able to click a nine, and then a one, and then a final one. Dad was taken to the hospital instead of dying on the floor.

That wasn't the lucky part. The lucky part was that his wife decided now was not the time to leave her husband. For those not acquainted with my mom, she's a serious woman, but she's also extremely gracious, and he was absolutely the winner in that relationship. My mom is awesome, and he knew it. He almost lost everything.

Only losing half of your body? That's actually a pretty good deal, the second she walked back in the door and let him know she wasn't leaving him to suffer alone, for the end of his days.

So my dad, with graciousness and good humor, just kicked ass at his post-stroke rehab after discharge from the hospital. We received reports from the rehab center, where he was learning to swallow, speak, walk, and use one side of his body again. They reported that his perseverance in rehab was inspiring to other post-stroke patients.

My dad never drank again. Not once. He had eight more years ahead of him, and they were the happiest eight years of his life. He went to AA regularly, which he found helpful. He was eventually able to walk, talk, swallow, and get that peg tube removed, about six months later. He could eat again.

He would do relatively foolish things routinely—this would include going out to shovel snow as an 80+ year-old man with hemiataxia, which would inevitably lead to him slipping or falling on the ice and ending up in a snowbank, from which he could not necessarily extract himself. This was before there was just relentless cell phone coverage, so even if he had his phone with him, it wouldn't necessarily work to call us. He would cry out, somewhat feeble, because again he had some dysarthria from the stroke that led to the inability to produce loud yells. He would just kind of moan, in a snowbank, until somebody wondered where he was, and then decided, against all good sense, to check outside, and we find them in a snowbank. Smiling like a goddamn idiot.

But he wasn't an idiot. He was completely correct. His life was awesome. His family loved him. And he loved the hell out of his family. Arthur Joseph Muir, Jr. won the goddamn lottery with that stroke.

I remember talking to my cousin Chris Athens about Dad, and how he advised me that his snow-shoveling adventures were. Chris was a wise man and told me the following:

“Look, the same attitude that let your dad think it was a good idea to go out and shovel snow, that ‘nothing can stop me, I'm gonna live the heck out of my life” approach? That's the same reason he did so well in post-stroke rehab. You don't get the remarkable recovery without the reckless attitude. Only bold people can recover from something like that— that same boldness is gonna lead to decisions that leave you in a snowbank.”

Chris Athens was not wrong.

For now, I spent time talking about loss and grief. We talked about my childhood, my first friend—a woman who was Roald Dahl’s lover, at one time—and the death of my sister, Alison. What follows is that talk, with Vita’s voice front and center, so my readers can get to know the woman she is, and enjoy a moment with the voice in the ear of this newsletter’s authorial voice.

Owen : You lost a daughter, Alison , to addiction at 53, and you’re still here. You lost your husband. Your son was hospitalized, stalked, and nearly destroyed. And whatever you did with all of that grief is the thing the book hasn’t shown yet. The reader needs to see Vita not become Esther. That’s the scene. Not argued, not explained. Shown. What did your grief look like after Alison?

Vita: Oh, that was all mixed up with my grief over your father, because it was a two-week interval. Daddy died. Two weeks later, Alison died. And my first thought was: thank God he didn’t know.

Owen: Rewind. You knew Alison was dying for about a decade.

Vita: I hoped. I’m an optimist. I fought it, and I hoped, and I hoped, and I did absolutely everything I could. But you can’t do anything for an addict. They have to do it for themselves.

My Mom Vita, in her office.

Owen: Tell me what was wrong with her, like I don’t know who she is.

Vita: What was wrong with her from when ?

Owen: Exactly.

Vita: From birth. She wasn’t wanted. She was wanted by her father, but her mother didn’t want to have anything to do with her — didn’t really want anything to do with anybody but herself. Alison was the cast-off. K., her sister, was the one who got everything. And Alison had what we’d now call borderline personality disorder. They called it character disorder then, but I didn’t know that until much later. When I entered that family, K. was sixteen, and Alison was twelve.

Owen: So Alison got what most kids don’t — a brand-new mother who might actually care about her.

Vita: Yes. And she appreciated it enormously. K.’s room was full of French provincial furniture, fussy this and fussy that. Alison’s room was like a monk’s cell — a bed, and that was about all. So I immediately set to making a lovely space for her. She came to know me and trust me, mostly around cooking, but really because I was probably the only one who had ever given her any attention. I knew she needed it. I could do that, so I did.

She told me she had put a note under her pillow — a letter to God, asking God to send her a mother. And then I appeared. She was fully ready. And I was lucky not to be a self-involved person, lucky that I could see need and had the ability to meet it. So I did.

When her mother didn’t lock her out of the house — and she would literally lock her out, her own child had no key, so Alison would play in the woods and wait until five o’clock when she was finally let back in — on the days she could get inside, she would watch cooking programs aimed at kids and write down the recipes. So when I first came to visit and would cook, she would jump up on the counter and lean over everything I was doing, wanting to know how it worked, why I was doing that. I nurtured that like mad. If somebody in town was giving a cooking class, I signed Alison up.

Owen: It was no accident that my first friend was the owner of the local kitchen shop?

Vita: That’s a coincidence. Alison was interested in cooking. I spent time with people who could teach it. Can I tell you a little about Suzanne?

Owen: Please.

Vita: When you were little, your grandfather had given me an absolutely wonderful pram — it had plastic windows on either side and one at the back you could flip up. You would lie on your stomach and lift yourself up and survey everything around you. People would laugh because it was so enchanting to see this little boy just sucking the world in. You weren’t a whiner. You didn’t cry and carry on.

One of the reasons I picked Litchfield was that I wanted to live in a village. I knew what village life was like — I knew that if services were arranged around a green, there was something right about it. So I would take you for walks into town, and I would stop in and chat with Suzanne, who had just opened her kitchen shop. We became friends. Because we became friends, you and she became friends.

Owen: I was in the pram at the time of the friendship-making.

Vita: You were. And you would do that pose — you’d lie on your stomach and press yourself up and survey everything. People thought you were enchanting. And you won her heart immediately.

When you were two, you started having sleepovers at Suzanne’s. You would vacuum her staircase — she had a little hand vac. As you got older, she taught you to play chess.

Owen: Blue carpet. Robin’s egg blue, little white flowers.

Vita: You remember that. I’ve been thinking about Suzanne a great deal lately because I’ve been listening to a podcast —

Owen: About Roald Dahl.

Vita: Yes. How did you know?

Owen: I’ve been listening to it also.

Vita: Well. I met Suzanne when she opened the shop — actually, we showed up at the same time. She was closing on a house in Litchfield, and I was closing on a house in Litchfield, and we had the same real estate agent. So we met then. And I found out she had bought the shop because she was trying to put herself back together. She had had a terrible tragedy. She had a daughter who, at twenty, was desperately depressed — and they had no idea what to do about it then — so they sent her to the Payne Whitney clinic. And she killed herself.

Owen: The Payne Whitney clinic. In which I was later hospitalized.

Vita: Right. Interesting.

Owen: And my first guitar was her daughter’s first guitar.

Vita: I’d forgotten that. So she was in despair. But — in her former life, she had worked for United Artists, high-level, organizing events. I think that’s how she and Roald Dahl met. And Roald was in love with Suzanne. Genuinely in love with her. But the man who was something like a mentor to him — a kind of adoptive father figure — decided that wasn’t a good plan and steered him toward Patricia Neal instead.

Owen: And they looked very much alike. Suzanne and Patricia Neal.

Patricia Neal, not Suzanne.

Vita: You wouldn’t know it from Suzanne as an older woman, but if you saw pictures of her young — strikingly alike. So she was trying to mend. She ran that shop like a general. She got furious with me because my friend Jay wrecked one of her knives, hitting a coconut with it. Oh, I see, your houseguest serrated your knife. She was really quite cross.

Owen: Let me take you forward. You had a brand-new child — me — and a child who had been dropped into your life, Alison. And you took her in.

Vita: I couldn’t manage K. Your father was on the road; it was just me. I had one child who needed real attention and who I thought could have a life. And another who was just off the charts — narcissistic, utterly self-indulgent, would take the car without a license. So I said to your father: I can save one. I can’t save both. So we’re going to have to send K. to boarding school.

Owen: And Alison became a chef. She went to La Varenne. And then her vocation killed her, because she took a job with Les Wexner — Jeffrey Epstein’s sponsor.

Vita: She was working in New York as a cooking instructor, which is a grind. And it seemed like a tremendous opportunity — a position as personal chef to someone enormously wealthy. She thought it would be something. But she showed up at that mansion, and instead of cooking for a small family, she was expected to cook dinners for hundreds of people. Her back went. The degenerative disc disease was completely exacerbated. She was hospitalized.

His doctor ordered her to bed for nine weeks. Well, I don’t know if she lasted a week. The man who ran the estate got her up and sent her back to work. This house had twenty-seven kitchens, she told me, and they didn’t even give her someone to help clean up after she’d cooked for all of his visitors and their nannies and all of it.

So the doctor said she had to stay in bed — this is really serious — and they put her back to work anyway. She had the good sense to quit.

Whereupon she applied to the Culinary Institute. She took a test, and she got the job, and she went and had back surgery immediately. Then, without rest, without time to recover, she took up the position at the CIA — the youngest chef de cuisine they had ever hired. They usually only hired women as pastry chefs or associates. She had a real chef’s job. But she was in terrible shape.

Owen: She had to take morphine to stay on her feet.

Vita: She found a doctor — a Dr. Feelgood — who put patches on her arms. It was actually fentanyl. That put her out of enough pain to carry on. And then she became completely addicted. She started using a hair dryer to release more of it faster. And she just went off the deep end.

Owen: She never got clean?

Vita: We thought she did, but she didn’t. The only time she was semi-clean was in the nursing home — after whatever happened with the fire. She burned herself. We never knew exactly what happened.

Owen: Tell me about the night she died.

Vita: The phone rang at one, two in the morning. It was her husband — the man she’d met when she moved into that apartment because he lived down the hall. An alcoholic. The last thing she needed. He called to tell me she was lying on the bathroom floor, and she wasn’t breathing, and he thought she was dead. I said: Have you called an ambulance?

He hadn’t called an ambulance.

He found her on the bathroom floor, and she had passed out from an overdose. So instead of helping her, he covered her with a blanket and went and ate his dinner. And then at some point, he went back and found her when she had completely stopped breathing. He didn’t call anyone. He didn’t administer Narcan. He did nothing to save her life.

I called him the next morning and asked how he was doing. He had called the ambulance and had her removed by then. And he said: it’s very quiet around here now.

He wanted to be rid of her. He didn’t want the responsibility. He didn’t want anything to do with any of it.

Owen: And this was two weeks after Dad died. We buried them on the same day, in the same hole.

Vita: Yes, because the village cemetery was closed in the wintertime — they only opened it when the ground could be broken. So I had daddy’s ashes in a box, and R. — I keep trying to forget his name, but there it is — he was just god-awful, just completely god-awful. We held the ceremony ourselves. Do you remember? We all just said what we needed to say to her, or about her. And then we went to the lake house and had a nice time. I was praying the whole time he wouldn’t get completely blotto and ruin everything, because he had already started drinking before he arrived.

Owen: And then we sat down for lunch. You, me, Lindsay, and Alison’s murderer.

Vita: Well, that was me trying to get rid of him. Gently . I never acknowledged to him — not once — that I thought he was responsible for her death. I had to play as if he were the grieving widower.

She was responsible for her own death. But he could have prevented it. It would have happened anyway — eventually. But what kind of person sees someone they know, covers them with a blanket, eats their dinner, and waits?

Owen: You wanted him arrested.

Vita: I did. I called the DA — I knew him, because he was the one who had prosecuted Debbie, my employee who stole from me. So we had a prior relationship. I told him what had happened, and he said, “This occurred in Winstead. The chances of their doing anything are slim to none. They don’t even know how to handle something like this.” He said: “nothing will come of it except that you’ll expose yourself to a very unstable man, and that could be dangerous. I think you should drop it.”

So, I did.

Owen: You weren’t going to send him off hungry either way.

Vita: What was I going to do — say the husband couldn’t eat? I had to manage the situation. I certainly wasn’t going to make a scene in front of everyone.

Owen: The way you handled your grief — for your husband and your daughter at the same time — was to cook a meal.

Vita: Food. It’s the Italian way of showing love. I like to think I show it in other ways too. But it’s also, apparently, the Italian way of showing grief. And in this case, metabolized rage.

I wanted to be rid of him immediately. But I didn’t want to provoke him, and I didn’t want scenes for anyone. She was responsible for her death. But he could have prevented it.

Owen: That’s all I need for now. I love you. You just crushed that section of the book.

Vita: I did? All right. Have fun. Are you going to transcribe this?

Owen: Yes.

Vita: To be continued, then.
