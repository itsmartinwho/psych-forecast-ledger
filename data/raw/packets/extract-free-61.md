This is the 901st post I've done on The Frontier Psychiatrist newsletter, and it does seem fitting that it's about progress . Also, here is a photo of Batz Maru for not reason other than my daughter loves San Rio.

Today, I was alerted that my favorite health insurance company—and yes, I have a favorite, despite everything I've learned as a parent of twins—Cigna, made progress in its coverage policies. As a regular critic of health plan policies, I love when I have something good to talk about, instead. I am enthusiastic about pointing out when a health plan makes excellent decisions. I'll begin with a little background about what this treatment even is;

Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) uses an electromagnet to re-synchronize brain networks, and this process treats depression. It also treats OCD, and it is likely to be able to treat other conditions as well. I've written about this a lot before.

I even run a clinic that offers this treatment already and is in-network with CIGNA. here's a link if you happen to have this insurance, and you have an adolescent with depression:

Book an Appointment For Your Kid

I'll explain why I am excited. Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) has been an FDA-approved treatment since 2008. This past year, one device manufacturer, Neuronetics, obtained an FDA label for the use of rTMS for adolescents 15 and up to treat their major depressive disorder.

There's something different about this FDA label for adolescents; it's different from all previous FDA labeling on TMS. At this point, extensive FDA guidance documents are available on the topic. The history, with visuals, has been extensively documented .

The FDA label regulates marketing , not clinical practice, but insurance tends to follow the FDA and will rarely approve treatments in broader populations than the FDA was willing to sign off on.

Here is what the “Indication For Use” submitted to the FDA, and approved , said in 2008 when TMS was first approved by Neuronetics, for a device they called the “NeuroStar”:

NeuroStar TMS Therapy is indicated for the treatment of Major Depressive Disorder in adult patients who have failed to achieve satisfactory improvement from one prior antidepressant medication at or above the minimal effective dose and duration in the current episode.

And here is what the adolescent label says…for extra verisimilitude, I’m going to use a screenshot, so we can really feel how indicated for use we are talking about:

It's indicated as an adjunct for the treatment of major depressive disorder. To explain, it's an adjunct to the standard of care, which, by the way, includes both psychotherapy and oral medications, but not necessarily both.

I've described previously how psychotherapy is a first-line treatment for youth, and so the thing that's missing from that indication for use is the following sentence:

Who have failed to achieve satisfactory improvement from one prior antidepressant medication at or above the minimal effective dose

Which is in the original FDA label in adults.

This means TMS in adolescence is approved as a first-line treatment, not one you can only market for use after other failed treatments. The first day an adolescent shows up to a doctor, you can start them on standard-of-care therapy, which could be talk therapy, and add TMS to that as the biological intervention.

There's a really good reason to think it should be a first-line treatment. The reason is that all of the oral medications in the treatment of depression in adolescence have suicidal ideation as a side effect. TMS does not have suicidal ideation as a side effect. There is no black box warning on transcranial magnetic stimulation for adolescents. There is a black box warning on every drug we could prescribe adolescents for their depression.

To say this very plainly, transcranial magnetic stimulation is the only antidepressant modality in adolescence, other than psychotherapy, that doesn't run the risk of increasing their suicidal thoughts . It is intended to be administered along with the standard of care, which, as a child psychiatrist, I'm here to tell you is psychotherapy.

We've been limping along with oral medications that increase the risk of suicidal thinking for teenagers because we didn't have other good options for their depression.

Until now!

WE HAVE AN OPTION TO TREAT ADOLESCENT DEPRESSION THAT DOESN'T INCREASE THE RISK OF THINKING ABOUT SUICIDE MORE!

This is the actual black box warning we are dealing with when it comes to “all medicines to treat depression in kids:”

I wrote about my personal experience in childhood with this very adverse effect before:

So it's very, very, very, very, very, very, very exciting as a Child Psychiatrist, and as a parent, and as a person who used to be a suicidal child, to have an option that doesn't make that problem worse.

CIGNA has been at the forefront of excellent decisions about coverage for TMS nationally, and their inclusion of transcranial magnetic stimulation as a covered treatment down to age 15 in keeping with the FDA lower limit is one step in a much better direction. They also cover OCD treatment nationally!

I have one quibble—of course, I have plenty of quibbles. I'm like a quibble Factory. However, in this case, my quibble is not just about wanting to get paid to treat more people with more TMS; it's about reducing the risk of bad outcomes in adolescents in keeping with what the FDA has already signed off on.

The CIGNA/Evernorth coverage policy does not mirror the FDA clearance. It includes additional language that I don't think should be there, and I'm am here to encourage my colleagues at all health plans to update their coverage policy promptly, because not only is it not keeping with the evidence, nor with the FDA approval, but frankly, it opens them up to tremendous liability:

I’m going to zoom in, with bold text, on a sentence there:

“for an adolescent age 15–17: failure of two trials of antidepressant medications”

This is asking for trouble. They could've just followed the FDA label and covered it when the FDA says it is indicated for treatment in children. To ensure it's not handed out willy-nilly, they could've stated that it needs to be administered as an adjuvant to the standard of care, which includes psychotherapy . Instead, they require two prior trials of antidepressant medications that didn’t help.

These trials of antidepressant medications, by definition, come with that black box warning for increased suicidality. To quote the FDA :

• Antidepressants increase the risk of suicidal thinking and behavior (suicidality) in children and adolescents with MDD and other psychiatric disorders.

• Anyone considering the use of an antidepressant in a child or adolescent for any clinical use must balance the risk of increased suicidality with the clinical need.

Now, we can, in good faith, discuss all day long whether TMS should be sooner or later in treatment algorithms, and I have strongly advocated that it be sooner. I have advocated that it be sooner because it's more effective, more cost-effective , safer , and all sorts of other arguments . However, adults don't have an excessive risk of suicidal ideation and an associated FDA-black box warning to contend with.

In adolescence, we very much do . If you're an insurance company, do you really want to be in the position of demanding that the children take drugs that have a risk of suicidal thoughts before we offer them a treatment that doesn't have a risk of increased suicidality? Because what does that sound like? It is a lawsuit waiting to happen.

I will also comment: Does anyone on earth think Owen Muir, M.D., your author, will not be called an expert witness in such a legal matter? Or will this article documenting the risk-benefit profile of these two treatments not be included in the evidence ? Is there any chance anyone at your insurance company subscribes to this newsletter? Is this going to show up in discovery?

Of course, it will be.

If I'm a class action ERISA attorney, and I know this is out there because? I will forward this to everybody I know at every insurance company. Once the extremely predictable lawsuit happens, I can go to discovery, find this article in an inbox, find the proof that it was opened, and then prove that they knew this was a problem well before my lawsuit.

This covered policy is an excellent step in the right direction, but I also think it's a time bomb. Conveniently, it's easy to defuse:

All you have to do is make transcranial magnetic stimulation, along with standard of care treatment what you cover. We need to acknowledge that oral medications have risks in adolescents that aren't present in adults. Those risks are more serious and have a black box warning for suicidality, so maybe we should do an end-run around the coverage policy that forces children to take drugs that are more dangerous than treatments that are now approved and safer.

To make everyone’s life easier, I did a rewrite of the CIGNA/Evernorth coverage policy to avoid this series of angry and very sympathetic parent lawsuits and predictably withering coverage in the NY Times and WSJ that would inevitably follow:

Initial Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation (TMS) for Treatment of Major Depressive Disorder (TFP Revised Edition).

An initial regimen (i.e., 30-36 treatments) of transcranial magnetic stimulation administered in an outpatient office setting using a U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) -approved device is considered medically necessary for major depressive disorder when an individual meets ALL of the following criteria:

• age 15 years or older

• diagnosis of major depressive disorder (unipolar), moderate-to-severe, single or recurrent episode or acute relapse, without psychosis, as defined by the most recent edition of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders

• During the current episode of depression, ALL of the following criteria are met for those 18 and up:

• an adequate trial of an evidence-based psychotherapy known to be effective in the

treatment of major depressive disorder without significant improvement in depressive symptoms

• validated depression monitoring scales are administered at the beginning and the end of the initial and each subsequent course of TMS

Coverage Criteria by Age Group:

• For individuals aged 18 and older:

• Documented inadequate response to two or more antidepressant medication trials from different pharmacologic classes

• Inadequate response to an evidence-based psychotherapy trial.

• For individuals aged 15–18:

• No requirement for prior medication or psychotherapy failure

• TMS is covered as an adjunctive treatment in combination with either:

• An FDA-approved antidepressant medication; or

• An evidence-based psychotherapy known to be effective in depression is coadministered along with the initiation of rTMS treatment.

*NOTE: A failed antidepressant medication trial is defined as EITHER of the following:

• use of an antidepressant medication at adequate therapeutic doses for at least four weeks with no significant reduction in depressive symptoms

• use of an antidepressant medication with documented intolerance or medical contraindication

Thank you all for reading, and I hope this moves the needle. By the way, those who are managing this is problematic for CIGNA, imagine every other insurance company who doesn't have any coverage whatsoever for this treatment and their policy. For those health plans, I think they're even more exposed to liability, not only for a coverage policy that adds additional risk, but for ERISA fiduciary breach. Another major health plan already covers this, what's your excuse?

I want point out that these health coverage policies are really hard to get right. And we should celebrate, like I'm doing today, when a coverage policy gets incrementally better.

CIGNA has proven again and again that at least when it comes to transcranial magnetic stimulation, it's on the forefront of appropriate coverage policies. Today, they've made it even easier for families with children suffering from treatment resistant depression to access that life-saving care.

I’m going to think through an issue with my readers. I’m stuck with a problem to resolve! A little bit of backstory will help.

When I’m not writing this, I’m a practicing psychiatrist, and I treat patients with Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation (TMS) to treat brain disorders. TMS is a methodology, not a single treatment.

In brief, for new readers, a brain is an electrical system. Magnetic stimulation (using a pattern of electromagnetic pulses) can harmlessly pass through the scalp and skull, noninvasively into the brain, and create a pattern of neurons firing electrically in the brain. It’s like an external pacemaker for your feelings— or, maybe, a metronome for helping your emotions play in time with your world.

This treatment modality was initially developed as a treatment for depression by Dr. Mark George, among many others, and was FDA-approved in 2008.

However, the treatment has parameters. We know depression can be treated with “medicine,” but you’d want to know “which one,” “at which dose,” and “do I take that with food?” With TMS, we have different patterns of stimulation, various dosages, other targets in the brain, and other times between treatments. We also recently have evidence that we can co-administer TMS along with medicines and get better outcomes. It’s a lot of variables!

I’ll use a picture here:

patterns of stimulation.

Dr. Mark George started by using 10 hz stimulation to the left front of the brain, which in science terms is the left dorsal lateral prefrontal cortex (LDLPFC).

In that image above, I've illustrated other stimulation patterns— 1 hz, 10 Hz, and variations of something called theta burst. There is spacing between those patterns, there is now Quadra pulse, one could imagine an almost infinite variety of stimulation patterns that are individually tailored per person, and we are absolutely not there yet.

It would be awesome if there's just one pattern that was best for everybody, but keep in mind—We also have different disorders we treat! What started as just a treatment for depression using one pattern pointed at one part of the brain at one defined dosage with one type of stimulator became an array of stimulators and an array of patterns stimulating different brain circuits to relieve suffering across a range of disorders.

Just in the time I've been writing this newsletter, here's a quick sampling of articles I've written, addressing the use of this one modality to treat a variety of disorders:

But today, I'm addressing a different question: How do I decide when to use something new with a patient ?

Patients are not research subjects. In research, we have a different frame. When somebody comes in, they're not a patient but a study subject —or perhaps a study “participant.” Whenever we keep changing the language, it means we're nervous about it!

The nerves show when we don't quite know what to say. In research, we acknowledge that we don't know the answer to “what will be better for somebody,” we work together to answer that question between the investigator and the study subjects participating in an experiment. We don't know the answer. The goal of an experiment is to answer a question. The study subject's goal might be to benefit from the experimental treatment. But they have another goal if they're participating in research, which is to help the science move forward so that other people can suffer less. Everyone acknowledges we don't know what we're doing yet.

In clinical care, we face a similar range of uncertainty. However, the goal of the exercise is different. A healer and a patient work together to heal that patient. So, taking risks is not to answer scientific questions writ large; it's for the benefit of that patient. The only individual we are concerned about the well-being of is the patient. There are secondary concerns, but the primary concern is always that person. We don't “just wanna know.”

The tension is that new evidence comes along all the time. With transcranial magnetic stimulation, the speed at which new evidence is generated has only accelerated.

Publications on the topic of TMS per year…through 2019.

So, there's a new paper every day, literally. Multiple papers are published every single day. Heck, some days I publish more than one myself ! Here is what has happened to my citations—we can dig into why this system is nonsense later, but it’s clearly picked up.

Citations per year for your author, Owen Muir, M.D.

Some of them could change my practice. I will give you a straightforward example so we can make this concrete. Next, I will review the problem on the page with you, dear readers.

How many pulses should I give in an individual session?

The original FDA-labeled treatment used 3000 pulses. The history of that clinical trial is available here.

Brainsway used fewer pulses in their pivotal trials, 2000 or just south of it. But some scientists, like my colleague Jonathan Downar, began to ask the obvious question: What is the correct number of pulses? Or, as he put it in this provocatively titled paper in 2018 1 :

Number of pulses or number of sessions? An open-label study of trajectories of improvement for once-vs. twice-daily dorsomedial prefrontal rTMS in major depression

This study compared the same number of pulses given to two groups, but one group was given the same number of pulses in one session versus being split up into two sessions. It found that more sessions were better than fewer sessions in the same number of total pulses of stimulation:

Mixed-effects modeling revealed significantly faster improvement ( group-by-time interaction) for twice-daily versus once-daily DMPFC-rTMS. Across both groups, the pace of improvement showed a consistent relationship with number of cumulative sessions , but not with cumulative number of pulses .

Pretty compelling! It was data like that that pushed me towards work on accelerated TMS as a way to improve outcomes, culminating in data sets like those published in the abstract published in Brain Stimulation by my team:

Accelerated And Non-Accelerated dTMS Protocols Both Effective In Treatment Of MDD

We retrospectively evaluated 87 patients treated with once-daily TMS and 21 treated with accelerated protocols, with between 3-10 sessions a day. Both worked well to relieve depression, but one approach got the treatment over with in a week versus six weeks.

But one might wonder—how did I choose to change my practice? Well, the above publication is a retrospective review. So, in 21 cases leading up to that publication, between 2017-2019, we decided on a case-by-case basis to do something different than the established treatment we knew was safe and effective. These were, at the time, weighty decisions. More importantly, they were not randomly assigned.

I remember the first time very clearly. It was a matter of necessity. The patient's brain had a high measured “motor threshold.” It was so high that it would have necessitated a power out of the machine that it couldn’t generate. I could either figure out a different way to treat the patient, or I could tell her to get back on a plan to fly home with no treatment, hopes dashed. This seemed like a bad plan. Thus, I did some research and then called a colleague—the endlessly gracious Aron Tendler, M.D. He pointed me to a buried menu that allowed for “Theta Burst” stimulation, a pattern demonstrated to work at 40% lower power than the FDA-approved protocol.

This pattern had been explored since at least 2005 2 , so it was not brand new. However, it wasn’t standardized or cleared by the FDA at that time, in 2017.

Most importantly, if I were to offer it as an option to my patient, the dose would be low enough for the machine to generate an effective dose.

So, after obtaining informed consent from my patient, we chose to move forward. Her depression remitted, and I learned something new was possible. Dr. Jonathan Downar and I seem to be interested in similar scientific questions—questions that align with “how can we heal patients quicker, safer, faster, and easier”?

That “Theta Burst” stimulation (TBS) pattern—50Hz bursts in triplets at a 5hz “burst frequency”— can be deployed in a fraction of the time of the traditional 10 Hz pattern that Neuronetics got through a pivotal trial with the FDA:

(iTBS) is a newer form of rTMS that can be delivered in 3 min, versus 37·5 min for a standard 10 Hz treatment session.

Downar and colleagues set out to answer the question of “is TBS just as good as 10hz” in a well-designed trial, and given his flair for naming them, it’s abbreviated THREE-D 3 :

Effectiveness of theta burst versus high-frequency repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation in patients with depression (THREE-D): a randomised non-inferiority trial.

Rarely do I get as excited by “these two things aren’t different” in a science paper, but in summary, the two patterns were just as effective:

In patients with treatment-resistant depression, i TBS was non-inferior to 10 Hz rTMS for the treatment of depression. Both treatments had low numbers of dropouts and similar side-effects, safety, and tolerability profiles.

Dr. Downar, in previewing his ambitions to bring this modality to scale and the subsequent launch this year of Ampa Health , a medical technology company 4 , noted in the last sentence of his abstract:

By use of iTBS, the number of patients treated per day with current rTMS devices can be increased several times without compromising clinical effectiveness.

(End of Part I)

This seems promising…tomorrow’s issue is a podcast—it tell the more full story of how iTBS came to be, and where it is going…do I switch to iTBS as a First Line option?

For well over a year, almost two, I've been writing and publishing an article almost every single day. Some days are two, some days I publish one that that's old and became relevant again, and I built a remarkable readership with this approach. But I hit a brick wall, and not the one I was imagining. I imagined I wouldn't be able to write something every day, and then I'd be really pushing myself to get it done. It wouldn't be possible. At some point I would fall down and not be able to continue.

I'm sure if I kept going long enough this would happen. However; I had another problem come up. There's a limitation to what you can write every day. The kinds of articles you can address, when you're trying to do it daily, are limited by the amount of time of the day. What practically happens is I tell shorter stories. I write articles that I can get done in a single day, because I have to do another one tomorrow, and the day after that, and the day after that. I write quick hits.

It's not so much that the quality suffers, although that can be the case. But what you can never do, writing on a daily cadence all by yourself, is tackle something that's gonna take seven days to write, for just one article. One of the ways of addressing this is to break it up into pieces, like the serial soap opera of old.

But again, soap operas only tell certain stories. There are more ambitious articles I'd like to write. I've been not writing them, I've been avoiding writing them. I had one, you see, to write every day. And that meant more ambitious writing never happened. Or, at least, rarely. There was no room for long form, because I set myself a goal, and I pursued it, and I succeeded in that goal, but it turns out great writing isn't always quick writing. Ambitious stories aren't always short stories. I've become a master, inadvertently, of short form writing, and haven't built the ability to do longer form writing by focus focusing on a goal, that I was able to pursue, unable to conquer.

I was using the objectives and key results framework, and I picked a measurable key result:

Objective: build a great newsletter as measured by:

Publishing one article every single day.

Unfortunately, one of the things I learned was that I met that that result perfectly. And in the OKr framework, that's a lack of ambition. That goal is not audacious enough— the point of measurable key results in the world of OKrs? It's actually to hit 70% of your results.

I wanted my newsletter to grow by over 100 readers a month, which is happening, but I haven't got to explosive growth.

I wanted the newsletter to generate revenue, and it has, but it has a generate exponential growth in revenue.

The organic growth is nice, and I'm thrilled for all of my readers, but the feedback from them as if they can't keep up with the writing.

I set my goals wrong, which I could only learn by actually setting them and seeing what happened. It turns out that my objective, although still good, to write a great newsletter about healthcare, was not best measured by writing one article a day. I have a close to 100% completion rate on that metric, in the goal with OKr is to get to 70%. I have learned that one article that is not audacious enough. I have learned that I need to write more audacious articles, more complicated articles, and I need to reset my goals.

Thank you for reading, and they're going to be regular articles coming out of this feed, but they're not going be every day, and the reason they're not gonna be every day is because if I do it this way, they will be better. I may have something to say close to every day, and I really enjoy writing every day, but I'm not gonna click publish every day.

I'm going to write more audacious articles, I'm going to encourage more along form contributions from collaborators, and this is gonna be a better newsletter, a great newsletter, not just a daily newsletter.

Thank you, all of you, so much for reading The Frontier Psychiatrists newsletter.

Part of the commitment means I'm gonna make it better, and that means writing more audacious articles, and that means less frequent publication.

I hit a natural limit, and now, I will adapt.

“can we give this patient Clozapine again, as an effort to restore a much better quality of life, if it almost killed him the first time?”

Dr. Manu was familiar with these cases. He knew they were life-threatening adverse effects. Hillside Hospital was the place that brought clozapine to America, and our department chair, at the time, was John Kane, the scientist who ran that remarkable FDA approval trial, for still to this day the most effective medication for schizophrenia. My colleague and I wrote a series of reviews last year on the topic of psychedelic medicines. One of them was about MDMA 2 . Subsequently, a number of papers about MDMA were retracted from other major journals, including papers for referenced. Our letter to the editor explains why we made this choice 3 , which is not the same choice every citing author published by a journal made.

The data on MDMA has been tainted by unethical practices on the part of some, but not all, of the investigators. This corrupts the whole endeavor. It's wrong. Science can’t rest on a rotten foundation, and misconduct in research is wretched and rotten to the core. The way academic work is measured is by citations. This is true for both the academic work itself and truth the journals that publish it. It's also true for the academics in question. Like anything that can get you a job, or lose your job, or to find your career, there's some gaming system that can happen.

Let's start with how the system works. When you write an academic article, it has four parts. It has an introduction, methods, results, discussion. There's also an abstract and a quick conclusion. There are pretty strict limits on how many words you can have in that article, and the more prestigious the journal, the less words you're likely to get.

Depression is bad [Citation]. Tradition traditional treatments aren't very good [citation]. We wanted to understand if this new treatment would be helpful in depression, which prior studies suggested be the possible [citation]. However, this is the first time…

When you're writing a paper, everything you say in the introduction that's a claim needs a citation. You can't just say depression is bad—you need to reference someone's prior research demonstrating that depression is bad. That paper now has its citation count go up by one. The more citations on a paper, the more impactful it is judged to be. Successful academic researchers have their work cited by other academics. Cranking out 100 papers that nobody cites is less important than cranking out one paper that 100 people cite, by an order of magnitude or more.

The way we recognize science is important is that it impacts the science of others.

It also lets you know that there's a popularity contest going on, because when you're writing a review article, or preparing your own original research, you're likely to read other people's articles, and you're looking at their citations. You might even cite their citations. Things with lots of citations get cited again, so there's a snowballing effect.

Thus, if you want to be a successful academic, you need to write works that's likely be interesting to other people, and worth citing.

As astute individuals may have already recognized there's a way to gain the system a little bit. What if you write 100 papers, and then you cite all of your prior papers every time you write a new paper?

Then your first paper would be cited 100 times, your second paper would be cited 99 times, et cetera.

One system for tracking impact is called the H-index, which is a measure of how important you are as a researcher. It has a pretty simple formula: in H-index of two means you have two papers, each cited twice. An H-index of three means you have three papers, each been cited three times.

It's a game-able system. Enter the i10 index—a product of Google, it turns out. They have a simpler system. You get one point for every paper that has 10 citations or more.

It also excludes self-citations, practically, so it's less easy to game in one way, at least.

Impactful research is interesting to other people. They're going to read it, and they're going to have a reason to cite it. I also write this newsletter. Today, we're returning to a basic tenet of science: correlation is not causation. Two provocative papers to help us remember this maxim are about the use of cannabis in older individuals. Published February 5th, 2025, the first is called:

Cannabis Use Disorder Emergency Department Visits and Hospitalizations and 5-Year Mortality

Now, the most important thing about this article is that the authors definitely went forward in time, read my article published last week on great titles for science papers , and used those strategies. And death is bad…

This is juicy! Cannabis has gone from a dangerous, illegal drug to a commonly used substance with purported health benefits. It's natural!

Your author, a fuddy-duddy, never bought into the health benefits. I think cannabis is probably not good for your health. I have data supporting this suspicion from its impact on those who are at risk for schizophrenia at my disposal for years. 1

The short version of that data is that cannabis is a terrible idea and dramatically increases your risk of developing schizophrenia if you smoke cannabis as a young person. It’s also got dubious data as a treatment for PTSD. But just like the lottery, once it was made legal, more people would do it. Legal cannabis means that all the people who were kept away from cannabis by its criminal status might begin using cannabis now. Even old people. It makes the cannabis market—and thus the range of those who ingest it—bigger. I'm not a proponent of the illegality of recreational drugs—I'm more of an advocate for being honest in our intentions, and a lot of decriminalization enthusiasts have used the medicalization of recreational substances as a path to end the drug war. (Cough * MAPS * Cough).

One of the benefits of illegal drugs? Boring old people lack the relevant cool friends who can hook them up. Boring old people like me. There is just a cohort that never developed the social network to access illegal drugs easily. Thus, some of the harms of recreational drugs that are illegal have been seen in younger individuals historically, because they were the ones living fast and dying young. Cannabis doesn't kill you, at least not when you're young. Until I found these studies, I wasn't familiar with any data on the role of cannabis in elderly individuals.

In the above large-scale study, data were reviewed on individuals who had an emergency department or hospital visit for cannabis use disorder, and whether they were at an increased risk of death.

A subsequent paper, in preprint, also looked at the risk of dementia:

Risk of Dementia in Individuals With Emergency Department Visits or Hospitalizations Due to Cannabis

Those papers share the same authors from the same data set 2 , and I’ll review them together because I think it’s worth the effort.

Starting with dementia: this is a cohort study. That means the authors select a sample, a large group, and follow them over time to determine if an exposure (like cannabis) is correlated to a bad thing (a disease) that happens over time. Cohort studies are unable to prove causation. However, many things investigated in cohort studies would be unethical experimentally, with a randomized control trial that included the ability to draw a causal inference. If you're looking at outcomes that harm people, it's not ethical to randomize them to a possibly harmful thing, just for the purpose of seeing if it hurts people.

We can ethically proceed with randomized control trials in which harms might be discovered, because the intention of the trial is to determine if we can be helpful to an individual with an intervention. That's an ethical reason to proceed with a research study. However, when the only outcome is bad, you're stuck with non-randomized control trials to investigate the question. My friend Dr. Nolan Williams likes to use the following example: it would be nice to know how much safer motorcycle helmets make riding a motorcycle, you can't ask a bunch of research subjects to take off their helmet and ride around the Stanford campus at high speeds, and wait for some of them to crash and see what happens to their head.

When the only outcome is bad or risky, we can't use study designs that could prove that we caused the bad thing. It's a feature of ethical research, not a bug, that we are not subjecting human subjects to dangerous things to find out how harmful they are. That is Nazi %&$&—and despite the social prominence of Nazis, in their modern guise, it's worth remembering that literal third Reich Nazis did some truly horrible things in the name of science that have guided research ethics in the opposite direction ever since. Never do that, that's what we learned from Nazis in medical science.

Ethical considerations aside, for the moment, let's take a look at what the authors examined: The cohort was not “anyone who used cannabis.” It was:

Exposure Individuals with incident acute care due to cannabis use, defined using International Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, Tenth Revision coding.

The study was conducted in Canada:

…Ontario, between 2008 and 2021 (with follow-up until 2022) including all individuals aged 45 to 105 years living in Ontario who were eligible and did not have a diagnosis of dementia at cohort entry (2 620 083 individuals excluded).

And the total sample included:

6 086 794 individuals, of whom 16 275 (0.3%) had incident acute care due to cannabis use (mean age, 55.2 [SD, 8.3] years; 60.3% male).

Now let's think about that sample for a minute.

We must understand who's in the sample, because as a healer, I'm talking about this study to my patients who are actively using cannabis, unless I'm in the ER. They've just been admitted for cannabis use disorder, I'm trying to advise them on what they could or should do with their cannabis use or lack thereof in their life. It is crucial that we understand who's in the sample in question and what makes them similar or different from other people who may be smoking cannabis in the general population.

This is not all the individuals who use cannabis. These are people who use cannabis, in the period between 2008 and 2021, when it was still much more likely to be illegal. It was illegal for recreational use in Ontario from 2008 until October 17, 2018. During this period, possession, distribution, and cultivation of cannabis for non-medical purposes were criminal offenses under Canada's Controlled Drugs and Substances Act. All of Canada made cannabis legal on October 17, 2018, with the enactment of the Cannabis Act (Bill C-45).

There has been legal medical cannabis since 1999. However, given that at least some of the cohort were using cannabis between 2008 and 2018, at least a portion of these individuals were using illegal cannabis, not medically prescribed cannabis. It's not just that the cannabis may have been different, but that people who are choosing to do things that are illegal are a distinct cohort from people who do not make that choice.

They have different risk tolerance, different drug testing at work, different problems, driving them to use cannabis in the first place, etc.

Furthermore, even with that caveat that different cohorts undertake illegal and legal use of substances, there is something very different about people who have a primary admitting diagnosis to an emergency room or a hospital of cannabis use disorder. In NYC, for example, in a similar epoch, among older adults, the rate of cannabis use in individuals 35 and up was about 9% of the population.

Only 0.3% of this Ontario sample were admitted to the hospital for that presenting diagnosis. It’s only a tiny fraction of older cannabis users who were seen in a hospital for Cannabis Use Disorder. It’s still 16,275 people. Admitting diagnosis is rarely a person’s only problem, but it is a marker of the most serious problem.

Let's take a moment to consider the psychology of doctors in emergency departments. When it comes to the admitting diagnosis, we're making a choice. We get to choose which admitting diagnosis landed someone in the hospital. We—the health professionals— are making a choice, always , to include not only the primary presenting problem but to choose it above all other presenting problems that could have been. Let's imagine that you are smoking cannabis, and you fall and hit your head. The doctor is probably going to admit you under a head injury diagnostic code, because that's the most relevant for your subsequent care in that hospital episode. If you're suicidal, and you smoked a lot of cannabis, you're still gonna be admitted under a primary psychiatric code most of the time. People admitted for a primary diagnosis of cannabis use are rare. They are rare in the sample, and it's a rare choice for a doctor to make, mainly because cannabis use disorder won't kill you. Many of the things that get you admitted to a hospital are either very bad or could kill you. Thus, I am arguing that a Cannabis Use Disorder admission tells us different information than “the individual used cannabis.” It tells us cannabis caused such a significant problem that the doctors chose to “call it out” as an admitting diagnosis, as opposed to “falls” or “altered mental status,” or any other admission diagnosis. Essentially, CUD admissions may be “really bad” when it comes to cannabis, and are likely to be the admitting diagnosis only in severe cases of cannabis misuse.

This “ severe cannabis use problems only ” thesis is conjecture on my part, to be clear. I don’t know for sure, because we don’t get quantitative THC levels to know for sure. We are likely evaluating a sample that, to pull together the arguments above:

• Are older adults

(mean age, 55.2 [SD, 8.3] years; 60.3% male).

• And who were using cannabis at a time when cannabis use was both medical and illegal . These folks are more likely to be high-dose or problematic cannabis users, who have fewer questions about buying illegal cannabis than a sample collected today would represent in a post-legalization era.

And what did the authors find?

• Cannabis use leading to acute care admissions went up in this older cohort in keeping with increased legal access:

Annual rates of incident acute care due to cannabis use increased 5.0-fold in individuals aged 45 to 64 years (from 10.16 to 50.65 per 100 000) and 26.7-fold in individuals aged 65 years or older (from 0.65 to 16.99 per 100 000) between 2008 and 2021.

And with that increase in use, we saw other risks, including dementia.

• The risk was elevated, markedly:

Individuals with incident acute care due to cannabis use were at a 1.5-fold and 3.9-fold increased risk of dementia diagnosis within 5 years relative to individuals with all-cause acute care and the general population of the same age and sex, respectively

• The absolute rate of dementia was 5% in the next 5 years for those with a prior hospital admission for cannabis use disorder, compared to 1.3% for everyone else.

Controlling for all other factors, cannabis-related admission was still strongly correlated with dementia:

After adjustment for sociodemographics and chronic health conditions, individuals with acute care due to cannabis use remained at elevated risk relative to those with all-cause acute care (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR], 1.23; 95% CI, 1.09-1.39) and the general population (aHR, 1.72; 95% CI, 1.38-2.15).

To make matters even more frightening, we can turn to the other data set, the one on “5-year-mortality”—death.

This data set started with “everyone in Ontario”:

This population-based retrospective cohort study included all individuals aged 15 to 105 years living in Ontario, Canada, between 2006 and 2021 (n = 11 622 571 individuals).

Here, they were looking at everyone who was admitted for Cannabis Use Disorder, and not just older individuals, and matched them to controls who had no exposure:

The matched analysis included 527 972 individuals (mean [SD] age, 29.9 [13.6] years; 330 034 [62.5%] male) with a median (IQR) follow-up of 5 (3-9) years; 106 994 had incident CUD. Within 5 years of incident hospital-based CUD care, 3770 individuals (3.5%) died compared with 2550 (0.6%) of matched general population members. 3

So, population-wide,

• cannabis use that gets bad enough to land you in the hospital is an excellent predictor of increased risk of death in everyone:

And in older adults, it was a good predictor of the risk of dementia.

And now…what do we do with the data?

The individuals captured in the sample were people who used enough cannabis to have a real problem that landed them in the hospital or emergency room. Using enough cannabis to land you in the hospital predicts that you are more likely to get dementia (if you are older) and that you're more likely to die in the next five years.

We can't say that cannabis caused this problem. We can't say that some people who use enough cannabis to be a real problem at time point one end up having a worse problem— dementia or death—at time point two.

When I think about how to use data like this, when advising patients, the following comes to my mind:

If you don't smoke cannabis already, I wouldn't advise starting in older adults. The outcome of dementia is a bad one. Most people will do anything they can to avoid the risk of dementia, and although we don't know if cannabis itself causes dementia, or if people already at risk are likely to use way more cannabis as a “premonitory” phenomenon before the frank onset of dementia. We also know that if cannabis were helpful or health-promoting, the numbers would go the other way. It's hard to imagine that cannabis is entirely benign, also. It may be that this increased risk of death and dementia is only present in individuals who were using problematic doses of cannabis.

If you're already smoking cannabis and want to get out of the high-risk, wrecking Ball, at a bare minimum, I would recommend reducing your use. Don't smoke enough cannabis to Land in the hospital.

If you're already a high-risk cannabis smoker who has already had a hospitalization due to it, I would recommend much closer follow-up from both your mental health professional and your primary care doctor.

Cannabis isn't good for your health at very high doses. It may be that people were already at risk of death and dementia are the ones using those very high doses at a much higher rate. However, if you're asking me for advice, I'm likely to tell you to consider not smoking cannabis based on the data. If you're asking me if cannabis is useful for your psychiatric disorder, given a psychiatrist, doesn't prescribe cannabis, I'm review these outcomes for you, along with the rest of anything else we might do, and compare those risks. There is increased risk of death from heart attack if you have metabolic syndrome, and that risk could be increased by some psychiatric medications, for example.

Having a serious problem can become more of a serious problem later. We shouldn't dismiss individuals with cannabis use disorder or turn them away when they seek care—they're actually at a higher risk than the rest of the population. Individuals with cannabis use disorder, particularly older age, deserve closer scrutiny and more care to improve and prolong their lives.

We're gonna need to understand this risk factor in more detail in older adults, especially as cannabis products become increasingly legal and increasingly common.

If you don't smoke, I wouldn't start. If you smoke, I keep it to a minimum. And if you're an older adult, and you're using cannabis, follow up with your doctor regularly to make sure we keep an eye on anything that is another modifiable risk factor.

Thank you for reading. I wish I had better news, just a couple of days after 4/20, but I don't. The data doesn't support it.
