DK: I’m also boarded in Addictions and Clinical Informatics and, have been at Definium now for almost exactly five years, which is pretty wild. The time has flown by at an alarming rate. I didn’t come to it through any sort of apriori special interest or expertise in any drug class in particular—certainly not in psychedelics. I’d been watching from the sidelines. Having come out of psychiatric drug development at Pfizer, where we were working on very different classes of medicines. I left Pfizer shortly after they left the entirety of brain health. They left neurology and psychiatry. We were able to do some really interesting digital measurement work in other therapeutic areas, my passion to be there was dependent on working on diseases in psychiatry. I had been in digital health for a little while, and then, through a story that you know Owen, better than anybody, came to the attention of, of what was then called MindMed through those digital health efforts.

OM: Dan’s being a little coy here. I may have introduced Dan to the founder of Mind Med, and Dan’s experience in informatics and psychiatry led him to have a skillset that was a perfect fit. There was a subsequent acquisition of Dan’s prior company, Healthmode, by Mind Medicine, which is now Definium.

DK : In my current role, We’ve been able to accomplish things that I’m really proud of—we are working to bring our lead asset to the world, LSD. In specific, it’s our formulation, Lysergide Tartrate, an oral dissolving tablet.

Ultimately, the goal of these drug development efforts is to demonstrate to the satisfaction of regulators, treaters like like you and me, payers, and, ultimately, patients that we have a drug that has something meaningful to offer them. It needs to ultimately be proved both safe and effective.

OM: Both Dr. Karlin and I have found ourselves in roles as physicians, which include interacting with regulators at the FDA to get new treatments over the line. For Dan, he feels that his training is crucial to how he approaches drug development.

DK : The training that we get in psychiatry includes the same medical school everybody goes to— there’s a mandated degree of true medical training

Then, we get quite a bit of training specifically on the medical aspects of psychiatry, We learn medical mimics of psychiatric illness, drugs, drug interactions, drug metabolism. All of that is incredibly useful in drug development and in thinking about how to demonstrate that a drug is safe and and efficacious. Most of us would agree that the mind and the brain are entirely contained within at least the body.

We don’t think that there’s some part of the mind that exists outside of the body. Yet, when we think about our practice, before psychedelics, often we would talk about psychological interventions and psychopharmacological interventions. They really felt quite separate, though I would argue there’s always a psychological intervention happening. There is not always a psychopharmacological intervention happening! But every psychopharmacological intervention includes a psychological intervention and to my mind, includes a psychodynamic intervention. We’re always interacting with each other conscious brains and unconscious brains. We get to make a choice whether we acknowledge that. But whether we want to or not, we are certainly doing it. Any separation we chose to draw is a matter of convenience

OM: We moved onto the topic of obtaining informed consent for an experience you can’t imagine. I asked Dan how he thinks about the role of medical ethics in the process of informed consent for something as potentially life changing as lysergide.

DK: Our western orientation to medical ethics privileges autonomy over other ethical principles like doing good or not doing bad or or justice. Because we privilege autonomy so much, we do informed consent as a standard of practice. This is not without a trade off.

An academic paper on the topic of Informed Consent in Psychedelic Medicine is available in the American Journal of Therapeutics Here .

DK: There’s strong arguments for degrees and different ways of doing informed consent, but in part, if you give someone a pill and tell them you’re very confident, it’ll make them better. We know that it more likely to make them better. And if you give someone a pill and tell them all the things that it might do that harm them, those things are more likely to happen too.

O: There’s an argument that the extent to which we inform people of risks actually makes our medicine less effective in the real world. We are trading off the odds of the medicine helping more than it hurts by telling people how it might hurt — which isn’t an argument to not do it, but it is something we should be aware of. And this speaks to that overall principle: no pill is given for any purpose that doesn’t also come with some degree of psychological interaction.

DK: The interesting thing — and the biggest change brought about by the recognition that there are these artificially divided two sides, the neuroscientific and the psychological — is that when we deal with psychedelics, we are dealing with drugs that have an acute psychological change associated with them. There are these very distinctive, transient changes in sensorium and perception and affect and cognition that come along with the drug. And while there are some who argue that those are unnecessary side effects that should be designed out, I’ve taken a different stance, in consideration of the evidence that’s available, which to me suggests that those changes — and maybe not all of the changes, maybe just the cognitive or just the affective or just certain aspects of the perceptual — are in fact a necessary part of the mechanism by which these drugs exert change. And so now we’re talking about a collapsing of the distinction between the psychological and the psychopharmacologic, and saying they might not be disentangleable — they might be completely interdependent in the case of these drugs. Which allows us to talk about these things in a way that is more informed by actual research evidence and experiments that show efficacy, but also doesn’t let us retreat into that convenient but erroneous distinction between the two.

O: What is a psychedelic?

DK: The answer to that question has changed and evolved with societal use of the word. When we talk about what have now been termed classic psychedelics — but were once just psychedelics — those are probably best defined, and the definition becomes a little circular when you’re developing LSD, as medicines that have 5-HT2A agonism: a specific subtype of the serotonin receptor in the brain and elsewhere, that have a quote “LSD-like effect.” But what that ultimately means is that through their action on that receptor system, there are perceptual changes. These drugs have also been called hallucinogens, but they really aren’t hallucinogenic.

An interesting and somewhat unique aspect of them — and I don’t think we even really see this in organic illusions — what differentiates them from organic hallucination is that the quality of the experience is more one of disorganization of the system. The thing that seems to decrease in activity when these drugs are given in doses adequate to induce their characteristic effects is what’s called, on fMRI, the default mode network — really the sort of background, always-on activity of the brain. And what we’ve known for some time is that in the background, when you’re not actively trying to do something with your brain, what it’s doing is mostly filtering the world into some sort of sense of sensorium — which has visual components, tactile components, all the sensory stuff coming up from your body, cognitive components, emotional components — and what your brain is mostly doing is trying to turn that into some sort of order.

O: I’ll give you a metaphor — you tell me if I’m getting it right. When you’re making a film, you have a director of photography who controls what’s captured, and a director who tells people what to do. Then you have an editor at the end of the process who cuts up what the photography guy captured, cuts up what the audio foley guy captured — they’re putting things together and saying, “Cut this whole scene, it’s not going to make any sense.” So the default mode network’s job, question mark, is editing our experience such that we can make sense of it. And it has to happen so fast there’s no latency in our experience, despite the fact that there definitely is latency. This makes evolutionary sense — in order to survive, we need to be able to process visual, auditory, and tactile stimuli fast enough to react in time to, say, catch a ball thrown toward us.

DK: And this is exactly the sort of thing we have to contend with when trying to create immersive environments — they have to not fall out of sync with the processing happening in the background.

O: I want to hone in on that. When you say disorganization — in the organic sense, an illusion is a distortion of what we’re seeing. There’s something in the room; it’s a TV, but instead of being rectangular it’s oblong. A hallucination would be seeing something that isn’t there at all — a guy standing there who isn’t really there.

DK: Think about what the filter is doing — and this is what makes drawing hard for a lot of people. The TV is only visually rectangular from one really specific angle, directly in front of it, and even then if you were to draw what you’re actually seeing, it won’t come out looking quite right. So even if you’re looking at the TV from a different angle, your filtered experience of it is “rectangle,” because it likely is rectangular. The hallucination example is interesting because there’s a little distinction here, and this is another thing that makes psychedelics not hallucinogenic in the traditional meaning. Hallucinations are believed to be real by the person experiencing them — they can’t say, “That’s not something actually in the room.” Pseudo-hallucinations are susceptible to reality testing. I see someone in the corner; I know there’s not someone in the corner, but I still see them — versus: there is someone in the corner.

In an auditory hallucination in schizophrenia, I hear the CIA talking to me. In an auditory experience from a psychedelic compound, it feels like I’m hearing voices, but you retain the ability to know that’s not what’s really happening. You say, “Wow, that was weird, man,” as opposed to, “That’s just what’s happening.”

The most interesting one — which LSD can prominently produce — is synesthesia, where I hear the color blue. I know blue is not an auditory thing, but I tell you I’m hearing it. I know this is an unusual drug-induced experience.

O: I often talk about the synesthesia of distress that is OCD — things that wouldn’t normally be distressing, like objects being asymmetrical, can be experienced as just as painful as burning your hand. So this is a routine experience in humans: one thing substitutes for another. Similarly, things can sound blue in the right context or set of circumstances.

DK: OCD represents a different kind of failure — an inability to tune out the question, “Is my front door locked?” Everybody has that thought sometimes, but for someone without OCD it’s not distressing, not all-consuming. A failure to filter appropriately, I think, falls into a lot of distressing experiences that ultimately end up as components of various psychiatric disorders.

O: So what I’m hearing is: in classical psychedelics like LSD, we’re getting a hint at some mechanism of something, because what they do to the sensory experience during acute administration is produce disintegrated sensory change. They’re taking the default mode network — which is responsible for cropping, filtering, and editing in very rapid sequence — and taking it in some way offline, such that our sensory experience is different by virtue of a lack of editing. And we’re overwhelmingly aware, correct me if I’m wrong, that that weirdness of disintegrated sensory experience is not real.

DK: Yes, absolutely. That is the characteristic of it — the world experienced without, as we’ve been talking about, the perceptual filters. But I think there’s a more important mechanism of filtering that we learn through development. And this actually coincides really nicely with development from intrauterine life — because sound processing and things probably start there — all the way through to sometime in adulthood. Arguably development in some form goes on all the way till death. But these key parts that lead us to be who we are, happening through adolescence and early on, include this sort of visual, auditory, tactile filtering: figuring out where your body ends and the world begins, figuring out how to organize the world into meaningful visual units. We talk about the infant recognizing the primary caregiver’s face first, the sound of the primary caregiver’s voice — that’s an organization of those perceptual systems.

But then, from a very young age — though becoming meaningfully expressive around age one, when kids first say their first words — there’s language. Not only do we have to turn the perceptual world into order, we as people, possibly uniquely, then turn the world into language. We experience the world through language, we experience ourselves through language, we experience others through language. Arguably something like the self or the ego is just a story we tell ourselves about ourselves. And once we have words — our human token — we can do much more complex and perhaps computationally efficient editing. We can pop out a word in the middle of a conversation. Words are a great way to edit our experience.

O : You’ll notice this whenever you talk to somebody who doesn’t speak the same language. German has words we don’t have — there’s no English word for Schadenfreude , taking pleasure in the misfortune of others, so we borrow it because it’s a useful concept. In Hebrew they’ve got words and sounds we don’t have in English, and chutzpah among my Israeli colleagues and chutzpah among my New York friends is a different chutzpah . These are concepts that frame our understanding. Am I getting it right that language, as I’m presenting it, is in keeping with what you’re talking about?

DK: The neat thing is that while individual languages do have unique characteristics, and are deeply informed by and in turn inform the cultures in which they’re used, the quality of language-having seems universal. By virtue of having language — which far predates any of the languages we have today — having language at all is necessary to have almost everything we have today as people. Language-having is what makes us storytelling creatures. The amazing quality of language is that it both allows us to put things into one another’s heads and is how we contain things in our own heads. The thing that contains neuroses is the language structures. And by virtue of having those, we can have specific maladaptive narratives about our lives — narratives about our past that were useful then but aren’t useful now, stories that hold us together now but ultimately aren’t conducive to moving forward in life.

What we so often encounter in the psychotherapeutic process is: yes, this is distressing what you describe, and we can talk about what this distress does that hurts you — but in many cases, this distress is protecting you somehow. The question is often more importantly phrased: what does it do for you to have this? Because if we can figure out what it does for you, it’s easier to work through.

O: And so you’re a measurement guy. You clearly have a real background in psychotherapy, psychodynamics, the traditions that make psychiatry a unique subspecialty in medicine. And your friend calls you up in the middle of COVID — and you’d spent some time using your skills as a physician. You can tell me if this is okay to say, but you went to the ICU and did some work as a regular old doctor, keeping people alive in New York City, which at the time was ground zero for the pandemic.

DK: God, I don’t remember exactly what dates, but it must have been April. It was in the Bronx. I had been associated with the Montefiore system for a little teaching — I was credentialed, I covered a service every once in a while or an inpatient service if someone called out last minute, because I lived in the neighborhood and was running the digital health company. So if they needed me, I was happy to duck in and do what I could to help. Plus, I love the work, and the thing that working in industry takes away is the opportunity to see patients as much. So I really appreciated that department and the people who worked in it.

When things were clearly taking a turn — there was a day that New York sort of went from playfully arm-bumping instead of handshaking to a degree of panic — a general call was put out, including in the Department of Psychiatry: did anyone want to be helpful in the event they were needed? My background: I grew up on ambulances from when I was sixteen. When I was at Columbia, I ran their ambulance service on the Upper West Side of Manhattan, where I’m originally from. When 9/11 happened, because of my role there, even though I wasn’t in the city at the time — I was in Western Massachusetts — I was able to get to the city pretty quickly, and spent three days at the World Trade Center scene helping out.

With this pretty clearly impending disaster, particularly up in the Bronx, it was obvious that participating to the extent I was able would be helpful. My EMS training and longstanding interest in disaster medicine and overwhelmed systems made me think I could be useful. So I got a call one Saturday that a team was running out of docs, and I went into a hospital up there and found a terribly overwhelmed system. The team I joined was taking care of twelve patients, four of whom were ventilated, several more on aggressive respiratory interventions but not yet intubated. There was one pulse ox for the four ventilated patients. If you wanted a blood gas — which, for folks not in medicine, is how you monitor how well you’re doing breathing for someone — you could draw one and walk it down to the lab yourself. That was the level of being overwhelmed.

On the second day there, we ended up doing fourteen resuscitations over twelve hours — unexpected deaths. People who were sort of expected to die were intubated because that decision had been made, but these were people dying unexpectedly. It was as badly overwhelmed as I’ve seen any system.

O: Did any of them survive those resuscitations?

DK: Not to leave the hospital. You get a pulse back and keep moving. But it’s not like on TV. CPR doesn’t always work — in fact, most of the time it doesn’t. Especially when you’re dealing with an overwhelmed system. And so early in the experience of the pandemic, in many cases we weren’t even sure why we were seeing what we were seeing. There was the respiratory pathology, obviously, but there was also a lot of clotting going on that we just didn’t know was a feature of serious COVID — people getting pulmonary embolisms even after they seemed in the clear. And again, it was early. Everyone was doing their absolute best.

I spent two days there and left glad that I was able to help, but completely overwhelmed on behalf of the residents and staff physicians who had to keep going back. I got to leave because someone else got better enough to come in. I was glad I could do what little I could and, in many cases, just witness the heroic work that the folks there were doing.

One thing I could do — and I felt fortunate to be able to do it — was make calls to families whose loved ones were in these units and dying, and try to talk through that with them in a way that an incredibly busy internal medicine resident neither had the time nor the training to do. Trying to bring, I think like so much of what we do, some relief to a situation that is intrinsically difficult if not impossible. And again, this comes right back to where we were: trying to help people form a story around their experience that will allow them to move forward — with as much self-realization, self-actualization, sense of efficacy, sense of purpose as possible. So much of even that situation, once you’re done dealing with the acute physiological needs of a person, is about trying to help them tell themselves a story about the situation they’re in.

Part V: The Psychedelic Part and the End of The Interview

O: Now, we’ve known each other for years. I remember even maybe eight years ago having this conversation about the future of measurement — and so I’ve been an AI maximalist in some ways thanks to you saying, “If only we could measure this change.” And you had this strange thing happen: a measurement guy who happened to be a psychiatrist gets a call from someone who I knew — who you used to be adjacent to at camp, shall we say.

DK: I was never his counselor, but I was his counselor’s supervisor.

O: And you had a number of other superstars — Adam Farren was at your camp, he’s at Canvas now; Jacob Brody was a camper. Whatever that camp was, a lot of people went into healthcare technology.

DK: It’s funny to encounter people who weren’t there but overlap with everybody else. It was a YMCA camp — the YMCA aspect certainly didn’t mean there was a religious requirement, as you observed. You could have absolutely gone.

O: Only if I’d been invited. And so you were presented with this opportunity to consider joining a company that was working on taking LSD and turning it into a therapeutic. When you’re evaluating that — there’s a lot of business stuff that goes into it, but you were brought this drug, which you certainly knew about; it’s been part of the history of psychiatry since we’ve had any compounds around. How did you think about it?

DK: Like you, and like so many folks, I had been paying attention to the research in the space, though that research was largely oriented toward psilocybin for sociocultural reasons, really. LSD was tarred and feathered the worst in the prohibition era — in part, I think, because LSD was the drug that spurred the first psychedelic era. The discovery of the remarkably potent effects of LSD — it exerts its effects at microgram levels; we’re giving a hundred micrograms in our trials — that potency was very exciting to psychiatry, to medicine. There was this upswell of interest, papers by all sorts of folks on various potential uses in psychiatry and medicine. But of course there was also non-medical use, and that non-medical use was associated with certain countercultural aspects of society. It was also, of course, associated with the most dominant hegemonic forces in society — like our CIA. So both sides of that spectrum were tremendously interested in the drug and its potential power.

When I looked at the company and the drug under development, it didn’t take much for me to recognize that the bulk of the historical evidence — albeit evidence gathered under not the same conditions or with the same rigor as we conduct research today — and this accumulating evidence from the second wave of psychiatric interest in psychedelics, suggested that this drug worked differently than the psychiatric drugs that had dominated psychiatry for the past sixty years. It seemed to have remarkably transformative effects.

O: And LSD is definitely a psychedelic. When the definition of “classic psychedelic” includes “LSD-like effects,” one thinks that puts it squarely in the middle of the category. Similar to Haldol — it’s really potent, it’s definitely antipsychotic. The most potent thing defines the class.

DK: We try to define classes as best we can, and then the interesting stuff is always in the details, not what gets papered over with the language and the label.

O: The only one we don’t have to debate.

DK: Classic psychedelic? It is indeed. But what folks were seeing that I found most interesting is that when treating people — almost regardless of the presenting condition — with classic psychedelics, while there are changes that can absolutely be measured with symptom scales, these drugs were doing something different than basically all psychiatric drugs. When you think about what we use today in psychiatry, they’re suppressive: they suppress some sort of thing. And if that thing is mostly bad, great — if you feel mostly bad and you take a drug that makes you feel less bad as its dominant effect, that’s useful.

O: Even in our grandiose naming — we put “anti” in the name.

DK: Anti-something. Not “pro-happiness” — an antidepressant. And that’s true of antipsychotics. It’s also interestingly true of drugs that categorically seem to have a different name, like psychostimulant. We don’t use psychostimulants to stimulate — we use them to reduce the effect of extraneous stimuli and allow people to focus on the stimuli they wish to. That’s actually a suppressive effect. So for people with ADHD, stimulants are suppressive.

In a world of mostly suppressive drugs, the way patients treated with classic psychedelics talked about the effects of their treatment was actually expansive. They talked about having more interest — more interest in their own lives, more connection to themselves, more connection to others, more ability to connect to their own story of themselves. Which sounds to me a lot like how people talk after very successful psychotherapy, not how people talk after they’ve been on an SSRI for six to twelve weeks. Of everything about the category, this sense that maybe these drugs could be opening for people rather than closing — enabling for people’s lives rather than just suppressing badness — is what made this an obvious move for me.

O: And so you made the change. You went from being a measurement guy to being at the helm, as Chief Medical Officer, of a company developing pharmacologic agents for therapeutic use.

DK: Of course I still have to oversee measurement — we measure a lot of things and look very closely at those measurements as we go. Measurement is at the core of all science. If the point of science is to try to learn things that are true about the world, then measurement is arguably the only way to do that. But measurement isn’t the core goal of what we do. What we do is try to demonstrate — through measurement — that the drug itself does the things we’d like it to do without doing too many things we don’t want it to do, so that its efficacy exceeds its harms, and that both are describable: we can provide good evidence for the positive effects and provide real insight into the potential negative effects.

O: So the initial diagnosis you were targeting got a breakthrough designation from the FDA — and you can talk about what that means — and it was for generalized anxiety disorder. If you could tell us what GAD is, why you chose to go after that first, and what qualified the compound to get that breakthrough designation, that would be really helpful.

DK: GAD — generalized anxiety disorder — is a pervasive and non-episodic condition. We think about MDD as episodic; a major depressive episode is defined by having had a depressive episode. GAD is more constitutive, more constant. Folks with GAD, more often than not, have pervasive worries about multiple things, and those worries ultimately have a deleterious impact on their ability to function as they wish to.

The way I describe it to folks who don’t have it or don’t know someone who has it: it’s not that they worry about everyday things — we all worry about everyday things, and there’s plenty to worry about in the world today. We carry around a little slot machine in our pocket that pings us with worries at all hours. But with GAD, the worry seems to bubble up from inside in search of a thing to worry about, so that even non-worrying stimuli become a topic of worry. For folks with severe GAD, it impacts multiple domains of life — family life, work life, school. People have difficulty sleeping, have somatic symptoms. It’s pretty generalized, hence the name.

To be a bit more comprehensive: we have the DSM, these categories of diagnosis, and for regulatory, billing, and research purposes we do our best to delineate between psychiatric disorders. GAD and MDD are pretty close — depending on how you slice it, they have about eighty percent construct overlap.

O: It’s a little bit like that TV — look at it head on, it’s a rectangle; look at it from the side, it’s a trapezoid. But really it’s the same object.

DK: The temporal course is probably the cleanest way I’ve come to differentiate them. Some people have depressive episodes and are completely non-anxious between them — absolutely those people exist. And there are people who have GAD and have never had a major depressive episode, though the odds of having one go up the longer they’ve had GAD. For a lot of people, I think GAD is the background state, and periodically there are breakthrough major depressive episodes where the worries probably get worse, but there’s also a really prominent set of depressive symptoms — that failure to take pleasure in anything. These are arguments that anxiety is forward-looking and depression is more ruminative and backward-looking, but in the end they’re tremendously overlapping.

So the question often becomes: is someone in a major depressive episode, or are they in their euthymic state — their “normal feeling” state — but with a high degree of background anxiety?

O: Does the filter clear up entirely between episodes, or is there still some dysfunction around what you worry about?

DK: Is there more worrying than you would want in your life? I think that’s a really fair way to put it. What we decided with the MM-120 program was to develop it specifically for GAD. GAD has been really tricky to treat psychopharmacologically. The last new approval was 2007. Almost all the approvals in GAD have been add-on approvals to antidepressants — they got an MDD indication first and then worked for GAD. Most of our options to treat GAD are limited in their efficacy. SSRIs, which are the first-line long-term therapy for GAD as they are for MDD, often exacerbate anxiety early in care and don’t work particularly well against anxiety cluster symptoms. Benzodiazepines can be acutely very useful —and I always want to be cautious about being overly negative about the drugs we have today, because they are the drugs we have today. There’s no question that millions and millions of people benefit from them.

O: As the guy who wrote Essential Pharmacology , I’ll give you a pass on being negative about them.

DK: My take is: you’ve got the drugs, you’ve got the toolbox, you’ve got the patient sitting in front of you, and the best we can do is the best we can do at any given moment in the history of psychiatry. The same was true for Osler — we’re all trying. They were addressing rheumatism and dropsy as best they could.

O: Brain gout, right. So, the FDA created a pathway for breakthrough designation. What does breakthrough mean? I think this helps frame why MM-120 is different from other medicines not just in kind but in degree.

DK: The strength of our Phase 2b research — a dose range-finding study with five arms, including a 100-microgram dose arm, which is ultimately what we’ve moved forward with, as well as 25 and 50 micrograms — allowed us to go to the FDA for breakthrough designation. We were able to demonstrate a dose-response relationship: we got more efficacy up to 100 micrograms and no meaningful additional efficacy at 200. And the strength of that efficacy allowed us to apply.

Fundamentally, breakthrough designation is the FDA’s acknowledgment that evidence has been generated for a compound showing it may have distinct, potentially superior benefits over what is currently available for a disease with a high degree of morbidity and mortality. It has to be new, better, and it has to address a big, life-limiting problem. It doesn’t get you drug approval, but it’s meaningful recognition.

O: Just for a minute — what is so bad about anxiety that it is life-limiting? Say it out loud.

DK: It prevents people from living their lives to the fullest. What I’ve come to is this: mental health — and I’m not using that as a euphemism for mental illness, but what it truly means to be mentally healthy — if physical health is equated, however imperfectly, with not dying, then mental health is the ability to anticipate what will make you feel some degree of satisfaction in the future; to make a plan, move toward that thing, execute on that plan, arrive at that future you have planned, and evaluate whether that satisfaction was actually there for you — and then repeat that cycle.

O: Identity and self-direction intact enough to pursue your life. And when you get there, empathy and intimacy working well enough that you can enjoy it with yourself and others.

DK: And that you can make plans for what’s next. Life is a continual set of practices — one plan after another, hoping to guide yourself toward something you can anticipate in the future.

O: Some resilience built in there too.

DK: Because we can be wrong. You can guess wrong about what will make you happy in the future, make plans that don’t get you there, fail to execute. But it’s also about being able to continually reevaluate and make more plans.

And so, ironically, for something that might seem mild, GAD can be among the most life-limiting conditions because it tells you in every moment, “Don’t bother making a plan — it’ll go bad.” You literally don’t pursue your life because you’re afraid. You don’t pursue moment-to-moment opportunity; you don’t pursue longer-term opportunity because the anticipation is that it will go wrong. Anticipatory anxiety causes avoidance, and anything that’s avoided due to anxiety — to resolve the dissonance of not doing the thing you think you want to do — well, it must make you really anxious. So there’s a constant escalation: the things that are avoided become even more avoided. It leads to panic attacks, eventually depressive episodes, social isolation, interpersonal distance. So much of psychiatry is defined by this dissonance between how someone wants to be and what they’re able to be. GAD makes people unable to be who and how they want to be in the world.

O: And the data you’ve seen from your Phase 2b efforts — it wasn’t designed to show definitively whether LSD worked, but to tell you how you should evaluate that in Phase 3. What did you see there that convinced you it was worth moving forward?

DK: There were two big-picture findings — and there’s a JAMA paper we wrote that goes into the intimate details, and there are a lot of details when you have a five-arm study, a twelve-week follow period, and all the measures we did. But I think the most interesting and digestible findings are these:

One, we had a remarkable placebo effect. You don’t usually hear psychiatric drug developers speak favorably about a high placebo effect. But the fact that our placebo effect exceeded what you usually see in GAD trials gave us confidence that the methods we employed were good — that something we were doing was matching up to, and exceeding, the history of these other studies. And then what we saw at a hundred micrograms in particular — which is the dose we’ve moved forward with —worked well.

O: I’m actually going to pause you there — I’m so sorry, a patient is calling. Can we pick it up in about an hour?

DK: Of course. Just ping me by text.

Finding Our Way In Clinical Research

Brandon Li is the co-founder of Power , a platform that helps patients discover and connect with clinical research trials. Before Power, he built consumer marketplace platforms, most recently at a company acquired by Thumbtack. I sat down with him to talk about why clinical trials matter, who’s actually signing up for them, and how a couple of guys from the tech world ended up building something that might genuinely change how people access experimental medicine.

Owen: Start with the basics. Who are you, and what is Power?

Brandon: My name’s Brandon. I’m one of the founders of a platform called Power. We think of it as probably the best website on the internet for people who are interested in learning about medical research. We help people find and get access to leading medical research, learn about what’s out there, and see if there’s anything being tried that they want to get involved with. You can think of it as kind of a Zillow or Airbnb experience, but for research trials.

When we launched in 2021, we were the first team ever to use large language models on clinical trial information. We were scraping all the clinical trial information we could find online and rewriting it into plain English. So we had this beautiful interface with plain English descriptions of every clinical trial. The result was that patients signed up much more aggressively than we were expecting. Now we have thousands of people signing up every week, and we’re working with the folks actually conducting the research to connect patients directly with researchers and their teams.

Owen: If you’re interested in clinical research, there’s probably a reason — and it usually isn’t sunshine and rainbows. How did this start for you?

Brandon: Both my co-founder and I come from a consumer tech background — consumer marketplaces, specifically. We’ve been building platforms that help people navigate real-world transactions historically filled with friction. Most recently, we were both at a company acquired by Thumbtack — general contractors, electricians, that kind of thing. Which is to say, we don’t really come from a research background.

But we both had family members and friends who had to confront the research landscape themselves. Personally, I had a very good friend who was diagnosed with a brain tumor at a pretty young age. She managed to save her own life by finding her way into a Phase 3 study that had a positive readout. She did everything people have to do today: she got into ClinicalTrials.gov, read the medical literature, joined every Reddit group and Facebook group you could imagine, and eventually caught wind of something interesting.

When she went to connect with that team, she was faced with a ClinicalTrials.gov page where the contact information was basically the front desk of the hospital. She was cold-calling, trying to navigate her way through to the department doing the research, to reach the PI’s team, and eventually get herself booked for a visit. Watching that journey is what inspired my co-founder Baskin and me to ask: can we take some of the learnings from general contracting and plumbing platforms and bring them to the research world?

As we started poking in, we realized that from the researcher perspective, folks are really excited to meet real people who are enthusiastic about their work. There was demand from both sides.

Owen: I started out as a patient and research subject before I was a researcher. In my twenties, I was enrolled in a trial — Ramin Parsey was the PI, J. John Mann gave me a lumbar puncture, and Maria Oquendo, our recent past president of the American Psychiatric Association, was my study doctor. It was the experience of being a patient that set me on the twenty-odd-year path to becoming an investigator. But let’s dial it back. Explain it to me like I’m an idiot: what is a clinical trial, and why might anyone want to be in one?

Brandon: A clinical trial is an experiment. It’s the process by which people doing life science or medical research determine whether their work has merit and can actually help the people they’ve set out to help. I say “experiment” intentionally, because it’s typically designed to get a yes-or-no answer: was this better than what we’re comparing it against?

Share

Owen: Right. We don’t know the answer yet. And when people say “I don’t want to be a human guinea pig” — we actually have pretty high barriers around what we’re allowed to do to guinea pigs, too. But you’re signing up for something where neither the doctor nor the research subject knows what’s best. We’re agreeing to work together to answer that question. So in the face of that tremendous uncertainty, who ends up raising their hand?

Brandon: It’s people who don’t believe they have really good options on the table today. People who may have tried a bunch of different treatments and decided that none of them are to their liking. One of the areas where we see a lot of excitement on our platform is depression and anxiety — not because there aren’t approved treatments, but because the things that are approved don’t often work and sometimes have pretty gnarly side effects.

You should see the stories people write when they sign up. They tell us why they’re interested in research, and oftentimes it’s some combination of: “I’ve been fighting severe depression for 10, 15 years. I’ve tried one, two, three, four different drugs” — and they name them all — “and I’m just not happy with where I am. I’d love to be part of this and see if there’s something better for me.”

Owen: So people are coming to Power looking for two things at once: hope for themselves and hope that their experience will inform others’ care. There is some altruism baked into this. For general audiences — Phase 1 trials test safety in healthy volunteers. Phase 2 trials give an experimental treatment to patients with a disease, usually to figure out dosing. Phase 3 trials randomize people to the new intervention versus a placebo or standard of care to answer the question of causality. Phase 4 follows up once a drug is out in the wild. The process doesn’t stop. As someone doing this all day across disease states, what motivations do you hear most?

Brandon: I’d actually love to hear from your perspective as an investigator — what motivations do you hear?

Owen wearing his “withpower” t-shirt.

Owen: In psychiatry — I mostly run trials around depression and schizophrenia — altruism is huge. People want to help others. They want to get something for themselves, and it would be nice if it was free, which by the way it isn’t always. They’re looking for hope and looking to help. There’s a class of person who’s willing to take a chance on getting better and willing to subject themselves to extra scrutiny and risk because they care about getting better and want access to something different. But that’s not the whole story. You can access many of these things without being in a study. LSD, for example — Mind Medicine’s MM120 is basically LSD, and you can go buy LSD. The worst way to get LSD is through a clinical trial . It’s really wanting to be part of an answer to a larger set of problems.

Brandon: Right. I sometimes read about the concern that patients are lining up around the block for psychedelic studies just to get high. But the burden of time and proof to participate in a research study is extreme. Anyone doing that because they want to get high has probably taken these drugs before and would be explicitly excluded from participating. It would be very difficult to game the system that way.

Owen: You’ve seen thousands of people — maybe more — looking for something. What have you learned from that?

Brandon: One of the most interesting things is how much agency these individuals exhibit and how well-educated they end up being about the landscape of options. There’s maybe a subtle myth in research around the lack of education of patients about their situations. There’s this weirdly parental mindset. But we read these stories and meet with these people, and they can tell you exactly why they’re interested in a specific trial. They can point you to literature they’ve read recently, or walk you through a medication history that leads them to believe now is the right time. They’re doing pretty sophisticated self-advocacy.

That’s in line with what we want to be building — a platform that makes it easier for the most engaged people, who are going to bang the doors down anyway, but also makes it easier for people on the cusp who’ve gotten frustrated because of how arcane the systems are for discovering trials and connecting with the teams running them.

Owen: How important is access to clinical trials for everybody?

Brandon: I don’t think it’s ethical to push everybody into the research apparatus. I think it is ethical for everybody to have the ability to understand it and self-select into it, should they choose. We kicked this off defining research as an experiment with unclear outcomes and risks we may not understand. So I probably don’t think it’s right that everybody should be doing research right out of the gates.

But I believe people deserve to know more about what’s available and what’s currently being researched for their situations, especially if there’s no clear good answer for their condition today. When doctors are presenting options in a clinical setting — “you can have X or Y, and maybe Z” — it’s not standard medical practice to also offer a clinical trial. I think it should be. Especially as individuals become more treatment-refractory and we know less about what should come next, a clinical trial should be a choice people are allowed to make.

Owen: I’d go further. We need more clinical trials — maybe less risky, less early, more structured ways of getting there. But if I don’t know what to do as a doctor, randomization is a pretty ethical thing to do when there’s genuine equipoise. The problem is I can’t use random choice in clinical care, but I can within the framework of a clinical trial. By the way, there’s a massive study on coin flipping recently — a hundred thousand flips in the Netherlands — that demonstrated heads and tails are not equivalent. Some flippers reliably landed on the side they started with about 55% of the time. Even the thing we use as our example of randomness isn’t truly random. You can’t know that until you do a large-scale experiment.

Brandon: So there’s a real skill issue at play?

Owen: There’s both a skill issue and a fundamental bias toward the side you start with. Which wouldn’t matter in most contexts, but in some it’s tremendously important. There are people who move massive data centers right next to the stock exchange to be a fraction of a second faster, because tiny advantages become wildly profitable at scale. But back to clinical trials —

Brandon: We’re all hoping we can bend fate, essentially.

Owen: That’s right. In clinical trials, people don’t want the placebo. But ironically, placebo care in trials is pretty great care. If you’re in a study at all, you’re going to do better, which is why we randomize to placebo in the first place. How do you talk people through the placebo question?

Brandon: I’d actually love to hear how you handle that as a clinician.

Owen: A lot of my research doesn’t involve placebo — I do medical device research, within-subjects crossover designs, diagnostics. But when I have to explain it, it goes something like this: we don’t know what’s better, and to figure that out, we have to contend with the speed limit, which is hope. Hope is one of the best treatments we have. What we’re trying to determine is whether this new thing is better than one of the most potent tools in medicine — the hope that things will get better. That turns out to be a really high bar. One option is placebo, which is hope plus nothing else. The other is the new thing, which may work better or may work worse. By consenting, you’re consenting to the chance that hope may be healing — which you were doing anyway, just without it being explicit. I’m often working with populations who haven’t responded to treatment, so there’s less of a chance that hope alone will be the thing that works.

Early TMS Pioneers on a Boat Ride. © Owen Muir, 2025

Brandon: Is there an intentional decision behind your not doing as many placebo-controlled trials?

Owen: It’s completely by accident. The work I do started as clinical care, became post-marketing research, and smoothly transitioned into open-label clinical trials and designing my own studies. I had John Kane as a research mentor — the person who brought clozapine, the most effective medicine for schizophrenia ever, to the US. He also dealt with a very risky drug with complex clinical trial design challenges. So I had crossover designs and mirror-image studies in my mother’s milk. My first paper was on clozapine re-challenge after life-threatening adverse events. My second was on long-acting injectable antipsychotics in youth. Not every study is best answered by randomizing to placebo. Sometimes you can look within the same person, which increases your statistical power enormously because your control is perfect. I was interested in conditions where the gold-standard randomized placebo-controlled trial wasn’t the best answer, and where functional unblinding would be profoundly disruptive.

Brandon: For you as a practitioner, what’s the appeal of research alongside clinical care? Not all clinicians do that.

Owen: It’s ethics. I started as a research subject. I have bipolar disorder . By the time I was sixteen, it was clear nothing was helping. We still don’t have great treatments for bipolar disorder — the stuff that works for me isn’t FDA approved. My mom did what every parent does: she called a friend. The friend was Denny Zeitlin, who is both a jazz pianist — my mom runs a jazz festival — and a professor of psychiatry at UCSF. His advice was: get your kid into a clinical trial , because they’re not going to drop a study patient. And if he gets lucky, he’ll be referred to someone good afterward. That’s the bonus plan.

Lo and behold, I was enrolled in a study at Columbia University at the New York State Psychiatric Institute. I see the same psychiatrist I was referred to after that trial to this day. It saved my life. What do you want from me? But I’m also forced to do research because the traditional standard of care treatments in psychiatry aren’t good enough for most of the people I see. I see patients who are highly treatment-resistant, and I’m obsessed with the problem of how to get novel treatments paid for — which means evidence that will convince anybody, which means clinical research.

Brandon: That’s a powerful story. This idea that just being on the path in clinical research might give you access to differentiated care — it’s talked about a little, but I don’t think it’s talked about enough. You immediately get access to a quality of provider that would be really difficult to see under normal circumstances.

Owen: I think it’s a total cheat code. Here’s what you want as a patient: you want someone who’s going to care. You want the best, and you want them to care so much that you can suss out whether they care more than average. If you’re seeing someone who does clinical research — it’s not for the money. You can make a lot more money doing things that are not clinical research. They’re doing it because they’re driven in some way. They chose this with their time, and it’s not just because it’s a job.

Brandon: And in all likelihood, they have specific domain knowledge for your situation that your average certified provider probably doesn’t, because this person is obsessed with that specific domain.

Owen: Right. And by the time you’re looking at a clinical trial, your problem is significant enough that you’re past the first line. There’s something about you that’s different, and there’s something about the person helping you that’s different. So is your call that more people should be considering clinical trials than currently do?

Brandon: More people should be considering it, and more people should be offering it — even clinicians who don’t do research themselves. I think we all have a vested interest in a flourishing clinical trials world where trials are part of what we offer every patient. And every clinical trial is an opportunity for someone who’s not in a trial to have a more equitable chance at a great outcome.

It really does weigh on me. We’ve talked a lot about this internally — how our tool can help more clinicians at least consider research as an option for their patients.

Owen: I think the search experience matters here. How does Power think about making clinical trials discoverable — not just for patients, but for the clinicians trying to help them?

Brandon: Today, the search experience is very condition-driven — condition and location. That makes sense, but it unnecessarily abstracts away the individuals doing the research. Sometimes patients are most interested in: who is the doctor at this institution who is the best at this specific thing, and what are they working on?

I think the individual researchers are just as important a part of the discovery and evaluation experience. Shouldn’t every PI or doctor have a profile page on Power — kind of a LinkedIn page for their research expertise? That’s a basis by which someone might want to connect.

Owen: I think that’s right, though it’s a little different from other marketplaces. If you’re looking for a researcher, you’re probably not making the purchase more than once. If it works, you go into a clinical trial, get a great treatment, and you don’t need another one. The referral sources have repeat business, but the research subjects — I hope — don’t have to keep coming back.

Brandon: What we actually see on our platform is that patients often have a first conversation and then realize it isn’t the right fit, and they go on a few “first dates” to figure out where they want to go.

Owen: I was a big fan of the data behind OkCupid back in the day, and dating is actually a better analogy. I met my wife at a child psychiatry conference, but she joined OkCupid right after we met, and we had the highest match I’d ever seen — 99 percent. The algorithm did a better job of retrospectively predicting how great my match was than it ever did prospectively. But the point stands: you should probably get screened for a couple of studies before you commit. It’s a relationship. You might be with these people for a year or more.

My entire family was enrolled in an NIMH longitudinal trial after my first study. Every living relative said yes. I hope it does something useful in aggregate — I’m never going to know. Brandon: Certainly.

Owen: So I clearly think about this a lot, because it was so important for me and has been so important for my patients. I have tremendous respect for research subjects — not just PIs. Henrietta Lacks, however she was treated, which was not well, is responsible for more good in the world after her death than almost anyone I can think of. The burden on all of us to think about how important we could be because of something — because of chance — that’s what the power of this is. It’s a good name for a website. So — how is this a business?

Brandon: It’s a business in the same way most pharma services are. There are parts of the clinical trial operation we can be uniquely positioned to support — patient recruitment, site selection, study design. If we have enough data on where interested people are, what geographies they’re in, and what they’re looking for, there’s a tremendous amount of information being generated through the platform that, if we’re thoughtful about it, we can use to inform research and help everyone do better research over time.

Owen: So you’re a licensing play. Does it cost anything for patients?

Brandon: It’s entirely free for everyone looking for research. If along the way we discover ways to help the folks doing the research — in ways that are advantageous to their study designs — that’s how we design our offerings.

Owen: And this is venture-backed?

Brandon: Yes. We’ve raised $19 million to date. Started about three years ago. We’ve been tremendously blessed with this triple bottom line of impact for individuals and for society.

Brandon: One of the things my co-founder Baskin and I asked ourselves when we decided to embark on this was: twenty, thirty years from now, if this goes really well, will we have been happy that this is what we dedicated our professional lives to? We reassessed other ideas, and not many passed that bar.

Brandon: And look, it’s probably because we don’t get to see patients like you do every day. We just make software and websites. To stumble upon something we can do with that skill set that is this impactful is really meaningful to us.

OM: When we think about electronic health records and people with psychosis — what gets in the way for patients and families?

BR: The big issue is interoperability. If someone with schizophrenia shows up in the emergency room, their outpatient psychiatrist might not know about it unless the patient tells them at their next visit. There are walled gardens between hospitals and community psychiatrists who aren’t part of a large hospital system, and who don’t have Epic or Cerner. Those community docs have no window into what’s going on for that patient in a crisis.

OM: What about families? How do they find out what’s happening with their loved one?

BR: They largely…don’t. HIPAA gets in the way. If you have a minor, you can technically access their MyChart through an app. But the moment they turn 18, you lose that. That’s one of the biggest pain points for parents of young people going away to college — which is often exactly when psychosis first appears. Parents are in the dark, and when they try to get information they run into walls of permission and privacy rules. A lot of people would actually like their families to be informed but don’t know what to sign or do to make that happen.

OM: Even if they knew everything they could sign — are there design problems on top of that?

BR: Absolutely. If you’ve ever tried to log into a portal like MyChart on your phone, it’s built for the patient as a solo user. There’s no easy way to share a feed of information with a family member. You’d have to request records from medical records, and then maybe it comes in the mail — or they fax it. Most families don’t have fax machines.

OM: I’m having a horrible image of a family that just keeps a bunch of spare fax machines around.

BR: There are virtual electronic fax machines now, but nobody should be dealing with that. The other option is an encrypted email — but then you need a password, and you forget it, and there are a billion hoops. It should be as easy as scrolling an Instagram feed. There is no Instagram for health records.

OM: Years ago I actually timed myself making a (fake) discharge summary as a TikTok video versus writing an actual discharge summary. Three minutes for the video. The written discharge summaries I get from hospitals these days check medico-legal boxes but tell me essentially nothing about what actually happened during that hospital stay. Whereas a thirty-second video — “hey, I took care of your son, he participated in groups, he’s looking forward to going back to college in the fall” — would do far more.

BR: That format is so much better than the medical-record-speak that gets copy-pasted and carried forward full of terminology no family member can parse.

OM: So if you were waving a wand — what would good design actually look like?

BR: You’d want the patient, when they’re in a more lucid space, to choose who they want involved — mom, dad, a sister, a partner, whoever. Give them real autonomy over that. And then you’d want a shared private app — not Instagram or TikTok for the world, but something like that for the people who matter. A feed where the care team and family can all contribute and stay on the same page.

Why They Built It — Two Families, One Company

OM: Tell me how you and your co-founder Mimi Liu ended up starting Doro Mind together.

Brady and Mimi from the Doro Mind website.

BR: Mimi and I worked together for about fifteen years before this company. We met at Athena Health — one of the first companies to put practice management software on the internet rather than on a server in a closet in your doctor’s office. We were the founding engineering team for their electronic health record, and we built it from two customers to around ten thousand physicians on the platform.

The theme running through our whole careers together has been: find something that’s working well at a small scale, and figure out how to make it larger using technology and operations. We’d done that over and over again.

But here’s the thing — we both have younger brothers who had their first psychotic episodes during their freshman years of college. And it took us a surprisingly long time to put those two facts together.

OM: What was the experience like for your families?

BR: Even with access to good resources — we’re not billionaires, but not unfamiliar with medicine—it was still utter chaos. There was a lot of not talking to each other. American healthcare is fundamentally a single-player system. It thinks about the world as one patient, one provider, one interaction. So there was my mom trying to get information through a complicated system of signals — without telling her anything confidential, could you just cough once if he’s basically okay? There wasn’t a real plan. A lot of confusion about what could and couldn’t be shared. And then someone would emerge from the hospital and it was just: here is your son, he is out of the hospital. Can he take care of himself? Probably not. Good luck.

OM: Mimi’s situation was different in some ways?

BR: In some ways more resourced, in some ways just as hard. She went to MIT, her husband is a surgeon who trained at Harvard Medical School and Columbia. She was deeply embedded in academic medical institutions. And when her brother had his episode, she said: move in with me, we’ll work on it together. He lived with them while she was right in the middle of some of the world’s leading research on psychosis. And it was still a mess.

We found out about the Lieber Clinic at Columbia — one of the best psychosis programs in the country — while we were founding Doro Mind. Not during her brother’s actual care.

OM: So even being inside the system didn’t mean the system worked for you.

BR: Right. The expertise existed. The knowledge existed. But the system didn’t exist to connect any of it in a way that worked for a family. We kept coming back to this framing: American healthcare is single-player, and we need to make it multiplayer.

OM: When did you two cross from “this is a problem” to “we should start a company”?

BR: We went to NAMI Con — the National Alliance for Mental Illness conference, in Minneapolis — and just talked to as many families as we could. NAMI was originally founded by mothers of children with psychosis who were tired of traditional psychoanalysis blaming them for their kids’ illness. These were people who had been fighting for their families for years.

We heard story after story that mirrored what our families had been through. Parents who had drifted for years without structure or support. People discharged from hospitals to — I don’t know how to say this more gently — essentially a bus stop. “We left him there. We don’t know what bus stop.” That was enough. We weren’t trying to solve a problem that had already been fixed.

OM: I wrote an article a while back — something like “free ideas for mental health startups” — and I gather that was part of the mix.

BR: It was part of the reading we were doing, talking to everyone we could find who was deep in the space. We were looking for some validation from people who actually knew the system — that there was room to do better. Your newsletter was part of that. It wasn’t just outsiders saying we can fix this. People inside the system saw the opportunity too.

The Clinical Model — Clozapine, Families, and the EASE Protocol

OM: For listeners who aren’t familiar — can you walk through what psychosis actually is?

BR: I’ll preface this by saying I’m not a clinician, so keep me honest. We talk about psychosis spectrum disorders as having three categories of symptoms. Positive symptoms are things that happen in the illness that wouldn’t happen at baseline — auditory hallucinations, somatic hallucinations, fixed false beliefs. The classic stuff: believing the CIA has instructions for you, or that God does, or that the devil does.

Negative symptoms are things that would normally be present but aren’t — anhedonia, flat affect, poverty of language, sedation. People don’t always expect psychosis to look that way, but it often does.

And then cognitive and executive symptoms — reasoning becomes hard, structuring a project becomes hard, taking a shower becomes hard. All of that can fall apart pretty aggressively.

OM: One thing worth adding: people get very hung up on worrying that they’re “crazy.” Almost thematically, if you’re worried you’re crazy, you’re not that kind of crazy — because you can see it. The problem with a delusion isn’t that the belief is strange to the person having it. It’s that what they believe is frightening . And one of my favorite things about psychiatry is how often my patients are actually telling me the truth and other people just don’t believe them.

BR: Our Associate Clinical Director talks about the tension between lack of insight on one side and autonomy on the other as the thorniest issue in psychiatry. If someone deeply believes they’re not ill, how do you balance respecting their autonomy with getting them the care they need? There aren’t easy answers.

OM: So walk me through the clinical approach Doro Mind uses.

BR: We’re very clozapine-forward. Our medical director, Dr. Robert Laitman, is a nephrologist by training whose son was diagnosed with schizophrenia. He found that none of the antipsychotics his son was taking were working, read deeply into the literature, and concluded that clozapine was worth trying — and then couldn’t find anyone willing to prescribe and administer it. This was the late nineties. So he and his wife pivoted their careers to understanding clozapine dosing, first for their son and then for a growing practice.

When we met Rob, he was seeing recovery outcomes in the high seventies in his practice. The literature suggests recovery from psychosis might be around fifteen percent nationally.

OM: That’s a staggering difference. And clozapine has been around forever — it’s off patent, which is probably part of why it’s underused.

BR: Exactly. The medication protocol Rob uses is called the EASE protocol — Early intervention, Assertive management, Slow titration, and family engagement. What’s distinctive about it is that it doesn’t just use clozapine to reduce positive symptoms and call it done. A lot of the work is tolerability: clozapine can cause drowsiness, metabolic changes, cardiac effects. Dr. Laitman does a lot of work on heart rate and metabolism — making sure the dosage is calibrated so the person is both symptom-controlled and able to be awake and functional during the day.

OM: You’re managing the metabolic side too — metformin, GLP-1s?

BR: Metformin first, GLP-1s second if metformin isn’t getting there. And a lot of attention to cardiac effects.

OM: One thing I’d add from a clinical standpoint: the goal isn’t always complete elimination of symptoms. Dr. Laitman’s framing of this resonates with me — he’s interested in reducing suffering, not just suppressing psychosis on a rating scale. Some of his patients still experience auditory hallucinations but report that the hallucinations no longer disturb them. At that point, chasing further symptom reduction may not be the right call.

BR: That’s exactly right. We have plenty of people working forty hours a week at jobs they enjoy, in relationships, who still have some hallucinations. That, for us, counts as recovery. Whereas someone with no positive symptoms who is living in a basement and not doing anything meaningful — that wouldn’t count as recovery by our definition.

OM: How do you define recovery formally?

BR: Twenty hours a week doing an activity the individual finds meaningful. School, work, caregiving — but with the individual’s own sense of meaning doing the work. It’s trying to make it falsifiable while keeping it person-centered.

OM: In personality disorder research they define it similarly: meaningful work or school, plus one relationship outside the family. And crucially — none of that is about symptoms. It’s about lived outcomes.

Dr. Robert Laitman’s EASE protocol is described in published literature. Doro Mind’s clinical team includes psychiatric nurse practitioners operating under a primary-care model for people with serious mental illness.

Making Psychosis Care Multiplayer — The Business and the Mission

OM: What does Doro Mind actually look like as a service? What are you building?

BR: At the core, we’re trying to solve the transition problem. Transitions in mental healthcare are dangerous — and not just the obvious ones. Everyone understands that having a first psychotic episode and ending up in a psychiatric hospital is a crisis. What’s less understood is that step-downs are also dangerous. You’ve achieved a certain stability inside the hospital, with a certain structure and set of people around you. And then you discharge — even to a residential program, even to family — and that transition is often where things go wrong.

We also see it at the other end: first episode programs are typically limited to two years. And if you look at outcomes, people tend to be doing significantly worse at the two-year-plus-six-months mark than they were at program completion. The program ends and the support evaporates.

So we’ve tried to build a system that can scale up and down with someone through what is, honestly, usually a non-linear recovery process. At our lowest tier of intensity, we’re really good informed primary care. At higher tiers we look closer to a first episode program or ACT program. At the highest intensity, we look more like an IOP.

OM: And you don’t discharge people just because they’re doing well?

BR: Right. If someone stabilizes and achieves real recovery, we don’t say “congratulations, you’ve graduated.” We move them to a lower tier of intensity and stay with them. And if they get admitted to a hospital, we don’t stop being their team.

OM: Walk me through what that looks like in practice.

BR: I was at a gala with our medical director when one of our nurse practitioners reached out — a member had gone to urgent care and his mom was worried. It would have been very easy to say: he’s in the hands of the medical system, it’s late, I’m at dinner. What actually happened was everyone stepped away and we huddled. We knew he was a clozapine patient. We knew that if he had pneumonia — which we suspected — his clozapine dose would need to be significantly reduced, and the urgent care team probably wouldn’t know that. So we spent the back half of that dinner calling people, working our way through the chain until we confirmed: yes, pneumonia, yes the doctor knows to adjust the clozapine, yes the mom knows, yes we have someone checking in tomorrow. Then we went back to dinner.

That’s what it means to actually close the loop.

OM: What’s the financial model — and why value-based care?

BR: Psychosis is extraordinarily expensive. The economic burden of schizophrenia alone exceeds the combined economic burden of all cancers in the United States. It starts early, it lasts a lifetime, and hospitalizations are the cost driver. The UK’s CUTLASS trial basically showed that the cost of medications is almost irrelevant — it’s hospitalization that blows up the budget.

So the logic is: you should be willing to pay for a lot of proactive, intensive outpatient care if it prevents a single hospitalization. That’s value-based care applied to serious mental illness. Instead of paying per service, you look at what it costs to achieve the actual outcome — keeping someone stable, employed, connected to their family — and structure payment around that.

We’ve been working with Medicaid managed care organizations in particular, making the case that intensive, clozapine-informed outpatient care for their members with psychosis is exactly this kind of play.

OM: Where do you operate right now?

BR: Doro Care — our hybrid model where we act as the patient’s primary care team for psychosis — is currently in the tri-state area and Massachusetts. Doro Compass, which is fully telehealth and designed for situations where we’re not the prescriber but can support a willing prescriber who hasn’t dealt much with clozapine before, operates in all fifty states.

OM: What’s the longer-term vision?

BR: Our mission is to make recovery the norm for people affected by serious mental illness. Right now there are around three million people in America living with psychotic illness. We have pockets of really excellent care getting outsized results. The problem isn’t that we don’t know how to help people — it’s that we’ve built systems that prevent us from doing what we know we should do, at scale. The stigma around serious mental illness makes it worse: there’s a layer of blame-the-sick-person baked into how the culture and even the healthcare system approaches it.

We’re not claiming we’ve invented a new treatment. We’re saying: there are positive outliers — places and practices where people are recovering at rates far above the national average. Let’s understand what they’re doing and build systems to move the average there. If we can get from fifteen percent recovery to something closer to what Dr. Layman sees in his practice, without waiting for a new drug breakthrough, that’s an enormous amount of human suffering we can prevent.

OM: I wrote that article because I wanted someone to do it. I knew it wasn’t going to be me. I’m glad it landed where it did.

BR: It helped confirm that people inside the system saw the same gap we did. That mattered.
