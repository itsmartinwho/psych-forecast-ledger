In this chapter, Dorothy, who has new onset OCD and Psoriasis, along with the Founder of a Rheum/Psych startup, called inFlames , stuffed with seed capital but without any product-market fit, meet The PE investor. They are on their way to meet the specialist!

This is a silly project. But it is a fine way to relax.

When Dorothy awoke, the sun was shining through the trees, and Toto had long been out chasing birds around him and squirrels. She sat up and looked around her. The Founder, still standing impatiently in his corner, banging out emails on his phone to generate more traction, waiting for her.

"We must go and search for water," she told him.

"Why do you want water?" he asked.

"To wash my face. It has to be clean after the dust of the road, and I have to drink, so the dry bread will not stick in my throat."

"It must be inconvenient not to have that problem solved for you by a disruptive health technology innovator," said the Founder thoughtfully, "for you must sleep, eat and drink, all without the difficulties in those processes having been disrupted with platform solutions. However, you have a day job that pays a salary, and it is worth a lot of bother to be able just to be paid with cash money without second guessing how it’s going to impact your burn rate or equity stake."

They left the cottage and walked through the trees until they found a little spring of clear water, where Dorothy drank and bathed and ate her breakfast.

Dorothy was feeling a little bit of soreness, and her knees and the bottoms of her feet hurt. This is a lot more walking than she was used to. She was wondering if she might have plantar fasciitis or something. She was definitely fatigued. This really sucked. She wondered if she was going to be able to get orthotics that would fit inside her shoes. She heard those were good for arch support; set a quick Internet search revealed that they were generally reimbursed as durable medical equipment and required a visit to a podiatrist …like she didn't have enough doctors to see already!

They heard a whack. Whack. Whack. Just ahead.

What the hell?

One of the big trees, around which the Great Forest coworking space had been constructed, had been partly chopped through, and standing beside it, with an uplifted axe in his hands, was a man wearing an Oxford shirt and a Patagonia fleece vest with a quarter zipper. He looked pretty fit. It was like he's gotten to take a lot of time off. You know, for himself. He still looked very stiff . He was holding a giant axe, awkwardly, like he didn't know how to relax.

Dorothy looked at him in amazement, and so did the Founder, but Toto bit the man on the leg.

He didn’t move at all, but he did make a little noise.

"Did you make a noise?" asked Dorothy.

"Yes," answered the fit man, "I did. I've been grinding it out on this deal for more than a year, and no one has ever heard me before or come to help me. Loose ends like this can kill a deal.”

"What can I do for you?" she inquired softly, for she was moved by the sad voice in which the man spoke.

"Get me a Flat White and let me caffeinate," he answered.

"I’m burned out so bad; I can barely fire anyone else. I’ve cut the fat on this balance sheet to the bone, but I’m just kinda wrecked and need a double espresso shot to get back on my A-game, or maybe an Oat Matcha Latte.”

Dorothy, feeling a little taken for granted but not wanting to upset this new person, ran to the nearest Starbucks and came back with a flat white.

"This is sweet," replied the Private Equity Investor. He looked at her and then looked down at the espresso drink.

“Did they put in this?”

“I think they might've,” answered Dorothy.

“Oh fuck, I don’t do artificial sweeteners. You can get me another coffee. It doesn't have to be an espresso drink; they'll screw that up again. Even a cold brew will do.”

Dorothy went to get a cold brew, but they were also out of that, so she got a Grande Red Eye, Hoping it would have enough caffeine to wake the Private Equity Investor out of his stupor.

The Private Equity Investor gave a sigh of satisfaction and lowered his axe, which he leaned against the tree.

"This is adds value," he said.

"I have been holding that axe in the air ever since I was brought into this deal, But there was so much blocking and tackling to do that I really didn't get a break. How many administrators do you need in one medical practice?” He continued.

“Wait a minute,” Asked the Founder, “I thought you were cutting down a tree?”

“Jesus, no. NO! That sounds like manual labor. This is for reducing headcount. Somebody else must've been working on the tree. I think we outsource that to a Portfolio company that rolled up a bunch of the area's ground care and tree health companies. The only thing I use my axe for is reducing headcount….Thank God you brought me a coffee. If you hadn't, I might've been just fine to sleep right here. Seriously bro, thank you.”

"We are on our way to the Crimson City to see the Specialist," she answered, "and we stopped at what looks like your PE firm's office to pass the night."

"Why do you wish to see The Specialist?" he asked.

"I want him to make the definitive diagnosis of my autoimmune condition so that he can get prior authorization on the medication that I need. But otherwise, it's way too expensive, and the Founder wants him to put market insights into his Strategy," she replied.

The Private Equity Investor appeared to think deeply for a moment. Then he said:

"Do you suppose The Specialist could give me a heart?"

"I think he is dual-boarded in rheumatology and psychiatry, but he must've done internal medicine before Rheumatology Fellowship, so that he would know something about cardiology," Dorothy answered. "It would be as easy as to give the Founder some key market insights for his play."

"True," the Private Equity Investor returned.

"So, here's the thing. I was actually not asking about a cardiology problem. Honestly, I'm feeling pretty burnt out. I basically fire people for a living, and they make a lot of money on the management fees by pretending that's adding value. But, to be honest with you… I don't even know what honesty is anymore. I say “to be honest with you” all the time. I don't even know what it means. You said he has a General Psychiatry board certification. Do you think he knows therapy or something?”

“Oh, so you're being metaphorical when you said give you a heart? Not like give you market insights about cardiology for some rollup of cardiology practices?” Said the Founder.

“Don't get me wrong, I'm not going to turn my nose up at a good cardiology roll-up play, but yeah, I'm thinking a lot about what I do with my life and not feeling very good about it. I just need some fucking direction or something. Maybe I need to go to Jackson Hole. That usually helps.” Replied the Private Equity Investor.

"Come along," said the Founder heartily, and Dorothy added that she would be pleased to have his company 1 .

So the Private Equity Investor shouldered his axe, slipped his Android work phone into his Patagonia vest pocket, grabbed his personal iPhone, and tapped it a few times. A blue Tesla Model 3 rolled up right outside, and he said, “ok, expensed it. Get in; this will be much easier than doing this whole trip on foot.”

They walked out of the Great Forest coworking space and saw no driver in the car! “I’m in the pilot for the Full Self Driving Next Gen RideShare Mode.” Dorothy and the founder exchanged looks.

“Bro, this is freaking sweet,” Exclaimed the founder.

“My dad was a seed investor in one of the companies that owned technology that wasn't as problematic as the Tesla self-driving patents. So we just fired everybody and sold the company to Tesla, and then they just used that patent as the basis of their fully self-driving ride-sharing product.”

“But is it safe?” asked Dorothy tentatively.

“We made 20x on the acquisition; it's perfectly safe; we had close to par value shares, so there is very little tax liability 2 .”

“Super Safe,” Agreed the Founder.

“Ruff,” barked Toto.

Dorothy felt pretty misunderstood by this. Tax liability isn't the kind of safety she had meant. She was wondering if it would be safe for her health!

But it seems like if she brought that up, there might be some real problems, and she was the only woman here, so it was a little bit awkward. And that little bit of awkwardness meant that she'd rather not bring it up.

The Private Equity Investor had asked Dorothy to get him another coffee before they left.

"For," he said, "if I should get caught in the rain and tired, I don’t want to be in caffeine withdrawal. No sweetener.”

It seems as if, despite it being pretty obnoxious to ask someone to keep doing it for you instead of doing it himself, this was perfectly normal behavior for the Private Equity Investor. So Dorothy just rolled with it…no matter how problematically sexist it was, for the Private Equity Investor to keep assuming that the woman was only there to get him coffee with or without his sweetener preference . I mean, he probably didn't even notice he was doing it.

It was a bit of good luck to have their new comrade join the party, for soon after they had begun their journey again, they came to a place where the traffic grew so heavy over the road that the travelers could not pass. But the Private Equity Investor set to work with his App, and soon they had successfully rerouted not just their car, but others on the road seemed to move out of their way.

“I love this feature,” The Private Equity Investor said.

Dorothy was thinking so earnestly as they walked along that she did not notice when the Founder stumbled into the trap of trying to pitch that private equity investor to sign up for his platform solution.

“With InFlames, if you're feeling depressed and have an inflammatory problem, we can handle both.”

“I mean, I might be depressed, I don't know, or anxious, probably anxious. I should see somebody privately. I'm not going to use an app,” said the Private Equity Investor.

“What about the convenience?” queried the Founder.

“Are you doing customer discovery with me?” asked the Private Equity Investor.

"I don't know enough," replied the Founder cheerfully. He continued:

“My balance sheet is stuffed with seed money, you know, and that is why I am going to The Specialist to ask him for some tips on what is going on in the market so we can execute the right go-to-market motion. We need more traction to justify our valuation, especially in this down market, and I don't want to have a down round, so it's all about getting that product market fit."

"Oh, I see," said the Private Equity Investor.

"But, after all, we're investing after traction and the ability to execute some blocking and tackling so we can flip to UHC, CVS, Carelon, or another major player in 2 to 4 years. That's the whole game.”

"Have you traction or market insights?" inquired the Founder.

"No, my head is quite empty," answered the PE Investor. "But once I had a perfect sense of problems in the market, you know, for traction, and a heart also; so, having tried them both, I should much rather have a heart and do some good in the world for patients."

"And why is that?" asked the Founder.

"I will tell you my story, and then you will know."

Some days you wake up and feel validated . Today is one of those days. We all know that the FDA did not approve MDMA-AT. Until Yesterday, we did not know why. The FDA does an amazing job of making it’s reasoning clear, and many life sciences companies don't share that reasoning with the world. Some choose to. The FDA, as part of it’s laudable push toward greater transparency, is moving to release more of these “complete response letters.” (CRL)

In its PDF form, it’s available here.

A quick definition: “MDMA-AT” is the “MDMA assisted therapy,” as manualized here, with an adherence manual published here , and with commentary from The Frontier Psychiatrists here. It’s a brand-new, made-up therapy specifically for the purpose of use in the branded experience of combining MDMA with the official MAPS therapy approach. This combo of an otherwise generic drug plus branded therapy was the submission to the FDA.

To avoid getting into too much trouble with my friends at the newly rebranded company —and yes, I have some of those—I’m going to do my best to stick to the facts.

This is a total lie. I’ve got opinions, and you will hear them! This is me, “ moving the goalposts ” on my readers.

Other writers, like Josh at the invaluable Psychedelic Alpha , outline some of the broader concerns with the MDMA-AT program the FDA highlighted:

• Failure to collect ‘positive’ adverse events (“FDA inspections also identified several unreported adverse events for at least two sites”, the agency added, “which increased our concerns about the reliability of safety data.”)

• Lack of durability data (“Overall, the data contained in your application fails to establish how MDMA should be used to treat this chronic disease”, the agency wrote.)

• High rates of prior MDMA use among participants, and high rates of prescreening failures (“Together these two factors suggest the possibility of selection bias”, FDA said.)

As someone who is a general cheerleader for the public servants at the FDA who work tirelessly, and in often challenging conditions, to ensure approval is a meaningful standard when it comes to safety and efficacy, remind Lykos that, to be blunt, “we told you to follow instructions” and MAPS/Lykos didn’t (Quoting the CDL):

FDA had advised in our, communication that "For all Phase 1, 2 and 3 studies, AEs associated with potential abuse or overdose must be documented," and we referred you to our guidance for industry, Assessment of Abuse Potential of Drugs (2017) for recommendations regarding how to appropriately document adverse events associated with abuse potential even if they are considered desirable (e.g., euphoria-related experiences, mood changes). During the sponsor inspection, FDA identified that the definition of an adverse event (AE) in the MAPP2 training information and the safety manual were not consistent with the MAPP2 protocol .

To make this very blunt, when the FDA says the “FDA had advised…” it means they told the sponsor what they wanted. The sponsor did otherwise. The MAPP2 training information is the MDMA-AT manual, I’m guessing. It’s not ok to use different definitions of Adverse Effects (AEs) in your trial what the FDA asks for. To expect your drug-therapy Frankenstein to get approved is quixotic at best.

In keeping with the unbecoming and hostile attitude toward FDA we've come to expectfrom MAPS (the non-profit)— put out a statement about the CRL:

But let’s be clear: the CRL reveals a devastating truth — the FDA moved the goalposts. In 2017, FDA selected the Phase 3 protocol design after a formal Special Protocol Assessment process, then, after the trials were complete and the application accepted, FDA shifted its standards regarding the approach to the challenge of conducting double-blind studies. In addition, FDA demanded more information after patients and researchers had already poured years of their lives into this process.

The cost of this bureaucratic backpedaling is not abstract. Thirteen million Americans live with PTSD today. Every year of delay means more will die by suicide and more remain trapped in cycles of trauma and despair. They deserve better.

The science is clear , the urgency is undeniable, and the human suffering is immense.

Can someone please get a memo to the PR team at MAPS? I’ll make it concise:

“Please stop insulting the work of the human regulators whose approval you need to have your drug approved for medical use.”

Because the FDA said the science wasn’t clear. To wit:

you have not demonstrated that the effect observed with midomafetamine is durable beyond the 18-week end-of-study assessments (8 weeks after the last dose of midomafetamine) in MAPP1 and MAPP2.

and:

There are multiple design issues with MPLONG which render the data inadequate to establish the durability of effect from MAPP1 and MAPP2.

and:

Although the MAPP1 and MAPP2 protocols allowed for enrollment of participants with prior experience taking midomafetamine, approximately 40% of the enrolled participants reported prior use of midomafetamine, which is much higher than the background use of midomafetamine in the proposed indicated population with PTSD, and higher than what would be expected based on available data

To highlight a few examples of the lack of scientific clarity in the data presented by Lykos.

And thus, bullying the scientists and career public servants who have made this call for every single other therapeutic in modern medicine isn’t winning you any friends at the agency. And now, to my real point for the day:

MAPS promises to continue “training therapists” in its dubious approach. FDA, however, makes it clear that this “training therapists” mission is not in the best interest of those therapists who have to pay a pile of money for…something:

The MAPS-based regimen may not be generalizable to all types of psychotherapy practices. We recommend using an evidence-based standard of care psychotherapy in lieu of the MAPS-developed psychotherapy in any future studies.

The MAPS mission of getting a therapy cult over the line as an FDA-approved therapeutic is dead .

I’ve written about both the paucity of evidence for branded psychedelic-assisted therapy before :

The summary of all of that: even if you believe the now retracted data from a number of the MAPS clinical trials, one of the things they never did was study the MDMA absent therapy . The MAPS trials studied, in theory, MDMA plus “AT” and “AT” alone, but never established that “AT” alone was an effective therapy for PTSD. The studies lacked an arm for “assay sensitivity”—this is a third trial arm designed to demonstrate that an already established therapy, in the recruited population, outperformed the placebo in the studied condition. Typically, this design is employed to address situations where both the active agent and the placebo exhibit similar improvement, and the study sponsor wants to suss out if that was because the trial failed (and thus it’s worth doing another one ) or if the intervention failed compared to placebo and also compared to treatment as usual.

And now, in the depths of my smugness, FDA backs up my opinion in one tidy sentence in the CRL:

Consider a factorial study design that includes an arm with no psychotherapy.

In summary, any future psychedelic-assisted therapy trials intended for approval from FDA won’t include kooky inner healing intelligen ce or recreating psychotherapy from scratch.

It is time to get the legitimate, evidence-based psychotherapy researchers and psychedelic researchers together, in the same rooms, and plan a future where evidence-based psychotherapy works hand in hand with potent medicines to healing human suffering.

As one such psychotherapy expert, this is a bit self-serving, but at least I’ve actually edited a psychotherapy manual while not on drugs. (Amazon affiliate link!)

It’s also worth considering if psychotherapy is even necessary. Just yesterday, the MindMed phase 2b MM120 trial was published in JAMA , and the results were—in the absence of any psychotherapy—impressive.

Onward, to a more accessible future, with more rigorous science. We’ve been here before, as a field. With, perhaps, slightly more wisdom:

The best kind of patient for [the purpose of satisfying their therapist] is one who from great suffering and danger of life or sanity responds quickly to a treatment that interests his doctor and thereafter remains completely well; but those who recover only slowly or incompletely are less satisfying.

—Tom Main, The Ailment, 1957

The world can be really scary. In case you haven't noticed, things might not be all right . For its worth, this includes up to “everything.” The problem with reassurance, especially when it comes to providing it to children, is that if “everything” does not end up all right, you are not only not the kind of help you were seeking to be, but you're a liar .

Newsflash: children hate liars. Let’s spend a moment on reassurance. Reassurance is the gummy peaches of human interaction. What I mean by that, of course, is that gummy peaches are delicious. They are sugary, they are sweet, and you don't want to eat them all day. You don't want to eat them every day. You want to have gummy peaches only occasionally . No responsible parent would give children less gummy peaches instead of nutritious food, because it would give your children diabetes, obesity, and all manner of ills.

One gummy won't kill you. Infrequent gummy peaches are not the end of the world. But any dreadfully sweet candy is just not an every day treat. You should save them, make them infrequent, and you should do the same thing with reassurance.

Children are frequently anxious. Adults are also frequently anxious. One of the ways we deal with that anxiety is often by looking to others and trying to assess whether we should be scared. The sweet, sugary, addictive version of “addressing anxiety”—that is not particularly healthy—is reflexively telling a child that it's gonna be fine. In the moment, this feels good. The length of that moment, in which the good feelings last, can be highly variable. It would be delightful if reassurance just lasted forever. Feel anxious, you ask them if it'll be OK, they tell you it it'll be OK, and then you're done. You're not anxious anymore!

If ONLY.

This is not how anxiety works. That feeling of anxiety, nervousness, it's really potent, it exists to keep us alive, to keep us on edge, so that we can mobilize our biological systems to fight or fight and get the hell out of there before we die. That system, mediated by our sympathetic nervous system, needs to work on a hair trigger all the time. Evolutionarily, the one time that system failed in any of our ancestors, that person was weeded out of the gene pool. Anxiety gets a little dose of reassurance, feels good, thanks to the trusted relationship. We have with the reassuring individual, but all of us who survived this day have had our fear system appropriately tuned to be worried again soon, if the threatening conditions persist. Which, in this day and age, they can be guaranteed to do. Most of the things were worried about, aren't gonna actually eat as alive like tigers, and yet we feel worried, like they might.

Reassurance— “You're fine!” It doesn't last—it’s the sugar high of addressing anxiety. Just like a sugar high, when it crashes, you couldn't find yourself wanting more. Reassurance feels good. Ask again.

Now you're on the loop. Another really common and terrible strategy is to help your child avoid what's making them anxious. Maybe they're worried about going to school? Maybe their stomach hurts because they're so worried about going to school. Maybe you should just let them stay home from school. Perhaps there's an adult, and they're worried about work, and now they're taking a day off because of their anxiety. Didn't you know they have chronic anxiety? Have you considered that what you said might be triggering? They're gonna need to avoid going to work or school because of their anxiety.

The strategy is also deeply problematic. When anxious people avoid something, they learn a valuable lesson: avoiding feels really good. My friend Dr. Jonathan Downar explains this idea quite nicely in a podcast here. This is what my co-authors and I did in an FDA approval trial, granted today.

This study was remarkable—in more ways than one. Most importantly, this study demonstrated that the accelerated version is wildly effective at ending depression in those for whom prior medicines and therapies hadn’t helped:

HDRS-21 depression scores, after statistical adjustment, were reduced by 19.02 and 19.79 points in the accelerated and standard Deep TMS groups, respectively; and response and remission rates were 87.8% and 78.0%, respectively, for the accelerated group

This is a GREAT result for a Treatment-Resistant Depression study. The other remarkable thing was the size of the trial—keep in mind, the SAINT pivotal trial only included 29 subjects total—15 in the active group, and 14 in the sham group. The study was stopped early by the IRB because it was deemed unethical (at the “midpoint” analysis) to continue to give sham treatment to human subjects when the active treatment was so remarkably effective.

This new trial, funded by study sponsor Brainsway, was much larger because it randomly assigned subjects to one active treatment or another active treatment. This means the risk of the trial getting shut down early was countered.

I was thrilled to be an author on this trial, collaborating with many dear friends, such as first author Colleen Hanlon, Ph.D , senior author Aron Tendler, M.D. , with a special shout-out to Molly Davis, M.D. , who enrolled a ton of subjects too!

We now have multiple studies making it blazingly clear that no one should have to keep suffering forever with depression, stuck in a rut, thinking that if medicine doesn’t work, there is no hope. In 80% of people, across multiple data sets, relief is waiting for you, using more than one variant of this modality.

More treatments exist, and are coming too. One addresses anhedonia and doesn’t hurt at all—brand name PRISM-MDD . Another, ProLivRx, can be done entirely at home . Let’s not forget psychedelics!

Oh, and how do SAINT and Accelerated dTMS compare? This is a cheat—these two studies don’t compare overlapping samples. The SNT FDA trial included only individuals with severely treatment resistant depression (The average was 13 failed medication trials and 20 years of depression!) The Accelerated Brainsway FDA trial was designed to mirror the original FDA clearance trial, and thus the inclusion and exclusion criteria mirror those—which were up to 5 years of depression, and more than 1 medication trial, but not more than 4 trials of medications. No Brainsway study patients would have been appropriate for the SNT trial, and vice versa. So, when people want to compare them, based on these two FDA trials? You can’t. These were conducted in totally different groups of patients, with the only thing in common being the diagnosis. It’s like asking to compare my experience in a hot tub versus the Atlantic—both involve being up to my neck in water, and the similarities end there.

A reader recently chimed in on my prior article about gun deaths and brought up the crucial point that many of those who die by bullets shot from guns are deaths by suicide. A debate in the comments ensued, and it was both spirited and respectful! I was thrilled to see that. I love a respectful debate, and our heroes should be those willing to engage in a conversation, not those who would abruptly end it.

It’s not a bullseye, it’s a treatment target!

This morning, it led me to dig into the data on a further point raised by my reader—given how “easy” suicide is to imagine completing, is lethal means restriction a rational and realistic strategy to reduce suicide deaths?

My hunch was that suicide is, almost axiomatically, irrational. Thus, people don’t sit down, “do the math” after deciding in utmost dispassion that it’s time to die, and choose the means with the highest likelihood of ending it all from a drop-down menu, as if they were choosing shipping options on a new purchase. So I had a question to answer:

Does the restriction of firearms prevent suicide?

Hunches are not data. So, I went to dig up the data. Here is what I found, published just last year!

Means Restriction for Suicide Prevention: An Umbrella Review

This is a review of systematic reviews.

12 systematic reviews were included in this umbrella review. Activities undertaken around the world were implemented for the prevention of suicide by firearms, jumping from heights and in front of a moving object, and suicide by hazardous agents. A variety of factors associated with the success and/or failure of mean restriction activities were identified, including the prevalence of method and presence or lack of a substitution effect. Most reviews found means restriction activities successful in the prevention of suicide.

Then, I realized it’s behind a paywall to a journal I don’t have access to. Gosh darn it! Feel free to send me the paper if you have access to an academic library with a subscription to that journal!

Science authors, a brief aside…remember, most people will only read your abstract. It should be the highest yield writing with the most data of your entire life.

For example, instead of the dreadfully general: “A variety of factors associated with the success and/or failure of mean restriction activities were identified,” authors could have chosen to say “which factors” were related to success and, conversely, failure. The details matter; please don’t leave readers guessing. To be frank, no one cares about your paper —until everyone cares about your abstract’s enticing data and wants to know more! Rewinding to 2020, we have a meta-analysis published on the topic that had unequivocal results:

Effectiveness of suicide prevention interventions: A systematic review and meta-analysis

In General Hospital Psychiatry, they reviewed

The meta-analysis was performed in 15 studies with 29,071 participants.

And…suicide prevention efforts as studied were effective:

A significant effect was found for suicide prevention interventions on completed suicides ( d  = −0.535 , 95% CI −0.898; −0.171, p  = .004) and on suicide attempts ( d  = −0.449 , 95% CI −0.618; −0.280, p  < .001). 1

That “d” there is a Cohen’s d, a.k.a. effect size , that makes suicide just about half a standard deviation less likely, and only slightly less effective for reducing the (much more common) suicide attempts.

It’s been hypothesized that multi-level interventions are likely to be helpful—that is to say, programs that approach the problem across settings, such as

(1) providing gatekeepers such as teachers and with priests training others to aid recognition of persons potentially at risk; (2) combining it with a publicity campaign [21,22] and with (3) instructions to the press on how to publish information on suicides. In addition, on the primary care level, general practitioners could be trained on how to address suicidal thoughts and behaviour in patients.

What does work? We will notice a trend—it’s mostly not psychiatric care, explicitly. None of these things is “ psychiatric hospitalization. ” In youth? School-based prevention programs were demonstrated to reduce attempts (there were no completed suicides in the trial):

The Saving and Empowering Young Lives in Europe (SEYLE) study is a multicentre, cluster-randomised controlled trial. The SEYLE sample consisted of 11 110 adolescent pupils, median age 15 years (IQR 14–15), recruited from 168 schools in ten European Union countries. We randomly assigned the schools to one of three interventions or a control group. The interventions were: (1) Question, Persuade, and Refer (QPR), a gatekeeper training module targeting teachers and other school personnel, (2) the Youth Aware of Mental Health Programme (YAM) targeting pupils, and (3) screening by professionals (ProfScreen) with referral of at-risk pupils.

Which, to be clear, didn't work at 3 months but did at 12 months:

No significant differences between intervention groups and the control group were recorded at the 3 month follow-up. At the 12 month follow-up, YAM was associated with a significant reduction of incident suicide attempts (odds ratios [ 0·45, 95% CI 0·24–0·85; p=0·014) and severe suicidal ideation (0·50, 0·27–0·92; p=0·025), compared with the control group.

Again, the risk was reduced by about half.

However, in the most up-to-date review, evaluating means restriction, a.k.a. keeping people away from deadly things like guns, Shank et. al. (2025) determined that the quality of the data was bad . It's not clear what restriction of guns does, if anything, to reduce suicide, in the existing (and very “high altitude”) studies on a population level:

Results Researchers screened 8522 studies and 36 articles met eligibility for inclusion. Most studies evaluated the impact of population-level firearm interventions on suicide deaths and found that stricter regulations were associated with a small reduction, if any, in total and/or firearm-specific suicide deaths. The ecological level of analysis precluded individual-level causal inference. Findings within interventions targeting methods other than firearms were limited, mixed and/or inconclusive. Notably, no high-quality randomised controlled trials (RCTs) were identified that met our eligibility criteria. 2

What are the problems with evaluating means restriction for suicide prevention? One is that it is most useful at preventing suicides that we have no ability to predict otherwise, which is among the most complex domains to study the effects of. If we could just take the guns away, or any other lethal means, from people at extremely high risk of suicide, perfectly, we would live in a different world. Large-scale means that restriction is a “all of us” decision to make, as-of-yet undetected high-risk individuals safer. It's also probably a decision to keep all of us safer, to keep those interested in the dreadful combination of murder and suicide away from guns, and maybe even reduce mass shootings. All of this, of course, makes it difficult to study, as Yip et. al. point out in the Lancet:

Means restriction entails a community or societal action that (ideally) does not depend on an individual’s intention or volition. Applied to the population as a whole, it t ypically affects people whose suicide risk is otherwise undetected and who do not seek therapeutic assistance to prevent their crisis or for life-saving interventions when necessary . Removal or restriction of access to a lethal method changes the context of a potential suicide by precluding potentially fatal actions or forcing the use of a less lethal method. Because means restriction is broadly applied, detection of its individual- level effect is often impossible ; it is best measured by aggregate findings of method-specific community rates of suicide and related self-harm injuries. 3

And perhaps the best answer to the question of whether “suicidal people will just switch if their preferred means are taken away” is that it’s highly context and culture-dependent. We have cases ( like paracetamol in the UK ) where interventions like blister packs made death by that means drop 4 without substitution 5 .

Similarly, suicide in Asia has a very different profile from suicide in the West:

The occurrence of substitution varies between regions and is associated with individual characteristics such as age and sex. The effectiveness of means restriction differs between the sexes; women seem to be more responsive than are men, and method substitution is more common in men than in women (appendix). Where means restriction has been implemented in Asia—typically of pesticide and charcoal—substitution has been reported rarely . The cause of this apparent difference is unknown; characteristics of the populations affected or the restricted methods might play a part. (also 3, below)

And so, after reviewing the evidence, to my readers who chimed in and insisted that restricting access to guns is not proven to reduce suicide, I am left, at this point, to agree. We don't have great evidence, on an individual level, that restricting access to firearms reduces death by suicide. It's also true that evidence would be extremely challenging to gather. It's also likely true that the cultural differences in gun ownership in the US versus gun ownership anywhere else would make the results less generalizable. Suicide prevention is hyper local. It's also challenging to study, because it's most important in them individuals who are the most difficult to identify in advance. I stand firm that the data suggest that mass shootings only happen with guns, and that restricting access to guns would reduce mass shootings, because obviously. Guns are efficient tools for mass murder, and they're highly lethal tools for suicide, but we don't have great data to suggest that it's possible to use means restriction at a population level to reduce suicide by firearm. This is a sobering realization, but it's what the data shows thus far. Thanks, readers, for nudging me to look into this further!

I can point out that we have effective treatments for people who are treatment-seeking, such as ketamine , which are rapidly effective for the reduction of suicidality. I suspect that accelerated transcranial magnetic stimulation will also be effective, and we have some data to suggest that is the case.
