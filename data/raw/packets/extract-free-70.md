Here on The Frontier Psychiatrists newsletter, we often cover exciting science. A study is done, and they find a difference! It’s easy to get excited about these “positive results.” I have often argued, however, that negative outcomes (there is no difference between groups) are just as important for science.

For today’s lazy weekend column, a round-up of my favorite negative trials of the week.

Hello from Lake Sunapee, where I write this missive.

We will start with a scientific definition— the null hypothesis . This is the thing we expect if two populations are not different. Or, as Wikipedia would have it:

The null hypothesis can also be described as the hypothesis in which no relationship exists between two sets of data or variables being analyzed. If the null hypothesis is true, any experimentally observed effect is due to chance alone, hence the term "null.”

Science experiments are looking to disprove the null hypothesis to some degree of certainty—this is what a P value is about. The formal definition often proffered defines a p-value as:

The probability of obtaining test results at least as extreme as the result actually observed, under the assumption that the null hypothesis is correct.

In this week’s round-up, we will discover things that are not different, no matter how much we might expect or want them to be.

First up, an ambitious trial:

Antidepressant Efficacy of Ketamine Plus Naltrexone for Major Depression Comorbid with Alcohol Use Disorder: A Randomized Controlled Trial

It would save us all time if they put “a negative randomized controlled trial” in the title. They did not. Fun fact— “they” in the previous sentence often refers to the journal editors, not the authors of the paper, who are the ones who make the final call on a title in their journal.

To the Methods Section!

Individuals with depression and Alcohol Use Disorder were randomized:

Randomized (1:1:1) to receive

1) intravenous (IV) ketamine (0.5 mg/kg) once a week for 4 weeks (a total of 4 infusions) plus intramuscular (IM) naltrexone (380 mg),

2) IV ketamine plus IM saline, or

3) IV midazolam (0.045 mg/kg) plus IM saline. 1

We have every reason to believe this trial will yield something—firstly, ketamine has been shown to be superior to IV midazolam, the “active placebo,” in other studies.

The inclusion of both IV midazolam + IM saline and IV ketamine + IM saline in this trial serves not only as a placebo control but also allows for “assay sensitivity.”

A 3-arm trial design that includes an experimental treatment, an active reference treatment, and a placebo is useful for assessing the noninferiority of an experimental treatment. The inclusion of a placebo arm enables the assessment of assay sensitivity and internal validation, in addition to the testing of the noninferiority of the experimental treatment compared with the reference treatment…

In 3-arm noninferiority trials, various statistical test procedures have been considered to evaluate the following 3 hypotheses: (i) superiority of the experimental treatment over the placebo, (ii) superiority of the reference treatment over the placebo, and (iii) noninferiority of the experimental treatment compared with the reference treatment. 2

I know, readers, I know. It’s boring when it’s written like a dry science article. This is why I try to write this column in a manner amusing ! Otherwise, you’d want to claw your eyes out. And, frankly, so would I. Turns of phrase to delight, with wit, they are the milkshake that brings all my readers to this particular yard, over and over again.

In simpler terms, we already know, from prior trials, that Ketamine is superior to Midazolam. Thus, if the Ketamine + IM saline group doesn’t outperform Midazolam + IM saline for depression? We learned that the trial failed, not the experimental treatment. Thus, when the authors found the following:

All groups improved significantly (> 80% depression remission). No group differences were observed in MADRS changes during treatment (primary outcome), although antidepressant effects persisted longer in ketamine groups compared to midazolam. There were no significant group differences in alcohol-related outcomes.

They didn’t actually learn a whole lot about the role of nalrexone plus ketamine. One of the errors in this write-up is that, to salvage reader interest, it goes on to analyze secondary endpoints, looking for something interesting to say. However, this is generally a no-no. When you miss your primary endpoint, unless you powered your study to determine a difference in secondary endpoints, you should stop.

However, as a learning point, in this trial, we discovered that the sample recruited for this study was unable to demonstrate a difference between two things that we know are different. We didn’t learn much else. It wasn’t the experimental group—it was the study design and recruitment.

90% of drug trials fail , by the way.

Inflammation! That may be important in mental health, say most people. Interventions are now going through clinical trials to demonstrate this effect. Not all of them are proving useful, however—just this week, a trial evaluated adding the cholesterol-lowering medicine simvastatin to lexapro in patients with depression.

Simvastatin as Add-On Treatment to Escitalopram in Patients With Major Depression and Obesity

In this randomized clinical trial including 161 participants with comorbid MDD and obesity, no antidepressive effects were found for simvastatin, 40 mg per day, in the primary end point (change from baseline in Montgomery-Åsberg Depression Rating Scale scores) or any key secondary end point. However, simvastatin reduced low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, total cholesterol, and C-reactive protein levels. 3

Now, one of the issues with this study is that the design made it hard to see a difference. The trial compared lexapro alone, an antidepressant that does something, plus a placebo, to lexapro plus simvastatin.

Any study of “thing that works plus something else” vs. “thing that works plus placebo” will be less likely to show an outcome that rejects the null hypothesis because math .

If you are running a traditional “one thing vs. placebo” trial, powered to show a 50% difference between groups, you need N people to demonstrate that 50% difference. If you are demonstrating that adding PQR to the standard of care, you are likely only posting the 50% difference between groups to 60%, which means you need many more subjects to show this 10% difference.

The authors of this paper do their sample size math out in the open for us. Based on prior research, they estimated that:

we calculated that a sample size of 64 patients per group would achieve greater than 80% power to detect a difference in mean changes in MADRS scores from baseline to week 12 using a 2-sample t test at a 2-sided significance level of 5%. To account for an anticipated dropout rate of approximately 20%, we planned to recruit 80 patients per group, resulting in a total of 160 participants.

This is the very clear “showing your work” that more authors should aspire to!

The problem, for my dear readers, is that subtle differences or errors in the authors' calculations during preparation can make it easy to miss a true effect if it’s not a significant one. Do your sample size math wrong, enroll too few people, and you publish a negative trial where a “real” difference might exist. There are also issues with increased variance, which means broader confidence intervals. 4

That issue, of course, is what the first study in today’s article gets right—they include an “ assay sensitivity ” arm to detect if the treatment didn’t work (experimental agent doesn’t beat placebo, but established treatment does) versus if the trial failed (both experimental and established treatments don’t beat inactive placebo at the same time).

There was no signal that simvastatin works for depression. It still works well for hyperlipidemia. I’d also add that oral antidepressants contribute to obesity and, subsequently, plausibly inflammation in depressed patients on their own, and antipsychotics even more so.

Clinical trials—they are hard to get right. Negative trials are just as crucial to the progress of science as positive trials.

This newsletter, authored by Owen Scott Muir, M.D., DFAACAP, might as well be the “mental health crisis star ledger for youth.” From covering the relentless increase in gun deaths in kids— now the leading cause of death in America! —to the slow death of sexual activity among younger adults , the parade of grim news is pretty relentless.

Today, some excellent news. No, it’s not about fewer suicide attempts among youth. That keeps going up. Especially among girls, as I wrote, along with my dear friend

It’s about what happens after a young kid—those aged 10 to 14—attempts suicide. Generally, we think of this as awful . It is. What it is not, a new study tells us, is a death sentence, or even a predictor of bad outcomes over the long term in the vast majority of such kids.

This is the topic of a new paper in JAMA Psychiatry:

Psychiatric Prognosis Following Index Suicide Attempts in Early Adolescents

Way to bury the lead, JAMA psych. Let me fix that for you:

The Psychiatric Prognosis Following Index Suicide Attempts in Early Adolescents is Better Than We Feared

Ok, to break it down: an index attempt is a first attempt. We could have just said “first,” but that isn’t as sciency. The authors sought to answer the crucial question:

What are the long-term social and psychiatric outcomes of individuals making first suicide attempts that come to medical attention between 10 and 14 years of age? 1

For a moment, as a parent of 9-year-old twins…that is NEXT YEAR. No, no, no, every bone in my body screams if I even get close to picturing this nightmare in my mind.

I’m going to use this video to calm down:

No parent ever wants to think about the answer to that question in advance. However, when it happens, which is now dreadfully more common, we should know the answer. Being able to share well-founded hope with families is one of the most important things any healer has to offer, especially in the darkness of those moments.

Tyler Black, M.D. FRCP , a child psychiatric suicide expert in Canada, has an awesome podcast interview where he addresses this question , on the show Psychiatry Boot Camp , 2 He describes the typical scene when a kiddo hits an emergency evaluation:

“If you come in to accomplish your checklist and you're reading things off of a paper or you're, you know, like, so are you suicidal?

So did you hurt yourself? Okay, so then what did you do? And you're not really responding in the conversation.

You're not going to get genuine responses. So usually I spend maybe about five, 10 minutes just chatting with the patient. How are you doing?

Tell me a bit about yourself. What are the things that you enjoy? This must be really stressful to be here… is [there] anything you need?

You know, just basic human stuff to bring down the defenses a little bit. People after a suicide attempt typically feel better than during the suicide attempt.”

This episode is worth listening to for almost everyone, but it should be mandatory for child psychiatrists and peds ER docs:

“So, you know, whatever happened before, here you are now talking to me, a psychiatrist, and you know, I'm a doctor, I'm probably here to help you or to save your life, or to, you know, some other idea I have in my head of making your life better. How do you feel talking to me right now? And when you phrase things in this way, and you're very open about yourself and very casual about it, and you know, I'll often make a quote unquote joke, but I'll say something, you know, like, if the person's name is Mark, I'll say, Hey, Mark, just to let you know, I'm on Team Mark living here.

Like, my job is to help you live. But I want you to live a life that's, you know, enjoyable to you. You know, whatever happened, whatever's going on for you, I want you to have a good life, a life that you enjoy, where you achieve everything you want.”

I am similarly on team life. How likely is that? Am I a quixotic dreamer? An absurd optimist? Or, maybe, do most kid s turn out ok ?

This research attempts to answer that question. I promise, you will love the answer. The authors followed up on:

164 individuals, in the follow-up period of up to 36 years after the first attempt,

NO ONE DIED by suicide.

Feel free to take a deep breath.

Now, this is obviously not all kids; it’s just one sample. However, it is perfectly reasonable to say, to parents and kids, in the fallout after a suicide attempt, that the most likely outcome, by a mile, is that the kids will be ok.

There is some nuance needed here. Not dead by suicide and “ok” are not synonyms, and some portion still have serious problems. Again, however, it’s vastly less than you might fear.

This sample is as middle America as it gets. It’s from a data set representing:

a 22-year period (1986-2007) in Olmsted County, Minnesota.

Of 164 individuals aged 10 to 14 years who made index attempts (128 [78.0%] female; mean [SD] age at index attempt, 13.7 [1.1] years), 3 (1.8%) died on the index attempt.

Three dead is three too many. However, it’s not most—it’s a small percentage. Most attempts are non-fatal in this age group, and even small percentages are in keeping with other devastating illnesses of childhood. The fatality rate of suicide attempts is on the order of unvaccinated cases of whooping cough, cholera, or measles. It’s not good, but most kids will live.

The other good news here is that 80% of the cases had good long-term outcomes—they didn't end up with repeated attempts, hospitalization, or chronic psychiatric illness. That is also a huge relief.

20%, however, had long-term morbidity—that is to say, serious psychiatric problems that continued to haunt the patient and their family. The predictors, here, included male gender and a psychiatric diagnosis before the suicide attempt.

We can never take suicide attempts lightly. We should also not take measles or pertussis lightly…but…

We deserve to have accurate information about what to expect. In the case of suicide attempts in young kids, we know a little more today about how remarkably resilient kids are. On average, it’s going to be ok.

Chapter 1: The Cyclone Insurance Policy

Dorothy lived on the Kansas Prairie. She was born there and grew up there. Dorothy is 19 years old; she just graduated from high school. Her first job was working for one of the companies that moved to the Kansas City area. It attracted many tech companies on account of its investment in high-speed broadband. She was a simple country girl, and due to a tragic, Adverse Childhood Experience 1 , she was raised by her Auntie Em and her uncle, Henry. Henry was stern, always solemn, and had a history of dark moods.

Her family was farmers. More precisely, they worked in the accounting department for Archer Daniels Midland (ADM). They lived in a modest house with four walls, a floor, and a front door painted green. It had blazingly-fast broadband. They led a simple life. Uncle Henry had purchased the appropriate insurance policies to ensure they were indemnified against cyclones. They had whole life insurance, an umbrella policy, car insurance, homeowners insurance, and, through ADM, health insurance 2 . It was a “great policy,” according to the insurance broker who ran the open enrollment workshop for ADM. “It's UnitedHealthShare; they are the best. You don't have to worry about anything!”

They lived on the outskirts of Kansas City, and in years past, not a tree or building broke the view from their home. Their straightforward home, updated in the 1980s from its original farmhouse to bring it up to code, was painted a simple white. Now, of course, their home was surrounded by development. They could see prefabs and businesses in every direction. The family couldn't help but notice many small medical practices had been acquired by a large hospital-based health system. Three of every four enterprises were medical: urgent care centers, pharmacies, and Moment Clinics. Even the old Theranos Lab inside the Walgreens was now a Fibula Health OrthoStop. Kansas must have a lot of people who care about their health!

As far as the eye can see, the slate-gray Kansas sky met the tops of buildings. Those buildings were all gray, tan, or other earthy colors. Drab and conservative was the color scheme of Kansas City. The architecture was squatish and spartan. It was often hard for the eye to tell where the Private Equity Leased Buildings for healthcare companies ended and the sky began. Getting help, no matter what your problem, was easy. Dorothy had no reason to worry. After all, the family had great insurance. Uncle Henry had listed her as a dependent, so she would be on the ADM health plan until she had a “qualifying event” or turned 26 years old 3 . It did seem a bit much to put almost half the household income towards insurance premiums.

Dorothy had a pet, Toto, the family dog. Toto was a purebred dog and the pride of the family. Unfortunately, Toto had the congenital hip dysplasia 4 common in purebred dogs. As a result, Toto frequently needed care at the Vet. Like most of the small businesses in Kansas City’s suburbs, Toto’s Vet was acquired as part of a private equity roll-up play in the “pet space.” They also purchased supplemental pet insurance for Toto and his complex health needs.

Dorothy was new to the workforce. Dorothy had big dreams. She worked at a Software as a Service (SaaS) startup. Her role was as a customer support representative–but the high-quality fiber optic network made her home also suitable for work. Some day, she would get out of the broadband-enabled suburbs of Kansas City, with its oppressive gray skies and endless focus on risk mitigation through indemnification. Dorothy dreamed she would someday complete her studies to obtain a Master's in Public Health. Harvard University was far from the prairie, with its stern folk and midwestern values. In Boston, well… that was the most prestigious Public Health degree in the land. These were dreams, and she held them close to her heart as she completed her chores in her midwestern home and updated her customer support calls in the company Slack.

But she was having trouble, and that trouble began to disrupt more than half the days of the week. Sometimes she would have difficulty concentrating. A lack of appetite was also prominent, more than half the days of the week. Those gray Kansas skies seem to be in her mind as well. She had a bad case of streptococcal infection last month. And, of course, she took an antibiotic. But three weeks later, she developed red spots all over her body. 5 They itch. And they were a little bit scaly. Dorothy began to worry. Over and over again, she would worry 6 . Was there going to be a tornado? Would the winds blow so hard there would be damage? Would she be forced to file claims on their myriad insurance policies? Would the carrier underwrite the catastrophe? Was she going to make a mistake on some of the paperwork? She checked their insurance policy documents, first just once or twice, to ensure they were there. Then she started reading them closely. Soon, she was all day going back and forth between scratching those little red spots and checking to ensure the insurance policies were in place. The language on them indicated they were indemnified against any cyclones that could come their way. So why was she so worried?

Auntie Em was a responsible adult, and she noticed Dorothy was suffering:

“My girl, my beautiful Dorothy, why don't you see the doctor? You've got those spots, and they weren't there before. Of course, they do better when you're out in the sun, even though it blisters the paint on our home and makes it as gray as everything else in the Kansas Prairie.”

Dorothy spent the next week searching the internet. She used matching platform after matching platform. First, she would book an appointment with a dermatologist on FlockDoc. Then, thinking better of it, she would try to find an in-network psychiatrist on Malma . And she then used Listened. And then Headstrong . Then MindMatch . Each time she used a matching platform to find an appointment, and each time the match felt…wrong. Something wasn’t right. It was hard to say what.

The number of in-network options looked large at first 7 , but most weren’t accepting new patients 8 . Many of the doctors weren’t accepting the insurance plans as listed. Some had long waitlists 9 . She couldn’t find the right match, and none of the algorithms were helping.

Eventually, she booked with Dr. Skinman, which seemed like a…right name for a dermatologist.

A fee-for-service healthcare system already doesn't work well. Some people are going to get very sick. These people will require a lot of health care. Insurance is usually how we manage the risk that is rare and expensive. The issue, of course, is that when you create a system where people have an incentive to act riskier because they won't have to pay for it, you get what we call a moral hazard. Let's imagine a world with readers’ insurance. In that world, readers’ insurance will function as health insurance does now.
