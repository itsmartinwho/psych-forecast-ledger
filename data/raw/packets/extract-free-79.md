Dr. Ahmed Tahseen is a psychiatrist, faculty at Yale , and life-sciences medical director, who was medical advisor #1 at Radial . He runs bipolar disorder and depression clinical trials. He sat down with Editor-in-Chief of TFPs, Owen Muir, to talk about the path that brought him to psychiatry, why bipolar mania trials keep failing in the United States, and what the best preventative treatment for bipolar disorder might actually be.

Owen Muir: Welcome to the Frontier Psychiatrists. Would you like to introduce yourself?

Ahmed Tahseen: I’m Ahmed Tahseen, psychiatrist by training, and I work as medical director in industry. I run bipolar disorder and depression trials.

OM: We’ve met before, so I know you didn’t start out as a psychiatrist. You went to medical school to do something else. Tell me about that.

AT: I went to medical school to be an oncologist. But I grew dissatisfied — actually, by dissatisfied, I mean angry — with the feeling of not being able to spend time with my patients. I went to medical school because I wanted to hang out with the people I was taking care of, and I realized there was too much distance.

OM: Because in oncology you’re not spending time with patients?

AT: My family had a personal experience with cancer, and that really informed it. Our oncologist was fantastic, but he probably went out of his way to spend time with us. What ends up happening with oncologists is they have to approach people as cases. They start thinking algorithmically — managing treatments, managing side effects. It’s a bit dehumanizing from the oncologist’s perspective. Obviously I didn’t become one, so maybe I’m not totally right, but that’s the way it started to feel.

OM: Your early experience was that your desire was to spend time with people, and you were watching people who didn’t do that in that field. Was this still in medical school?

AT: While I was in medical school, yeah.

OM: So you had to choose a residency. What did you end up applying to?

AT: This is counterintuitive, but I realized I really liked dermatology. Part of the reason is that a dermatologist spends almost all their time with their patients. Other specialties don’t get to do that. The whole day, the whole time, you’re spending time with people dealing with whatever’s going on in their skin in a way that only you understand and only you can help them with.

OM: Why is it different than oncology? What’s so different about the skin as an organ system?

AT: To understand someone who presents with cancer of the liver, you have to spend a lot of time at the computer — their chart, their intake, making phone calls, imaging. You go see them, but actually seeing them — and I don’t mean to be crass — is sort of a waste of time, in the sense that the oncologist has to balance all these different things. Treatment plans, prognosis, communication, follow-up care, diagnostics. What’s the marker of your tumor? How are we going to treat it based on the markers? What side effects do I watch for? Checking in on how you’re doing is important, but now it’s taking away time from all the other people you have to see. It becomes unfortunately reduced. I’m sure oncologists don’t wish it was this way.

OM: It seems like given the progress in oncology on biomarkers — every tumor has its own genetic profile, and they know so much about what they could or should do based on a lab test — the history and exam become less important. Because of the progress in the field, does the bedside encounter matter less to the clinical decision?

AT: You’re getting at exactly what it is. Nowhere is the theater of medicine more highlighted than in this realm.

The Theater of Medicine

AT: Should we talk about the theater of medicine 1 ?

OM: I think we should!

AT: The theater of medicine is the idea that your doctor comes in and puts a stethoscope on you, puts a hand on you, because the act of doing it and the sight of doing it is expressing care. It’s not necessarily that the stethoscope is going to pick up something the X-ray won’t, or the CT scan you might order. But it is an expression of care. That’s what it becomes — this theater of medicine.

OM: And so dermatology is different. Since no other doctor is any good at anything involving skin — I did four years of med school, four years of residency, two years of fellowship, and only by having psoriasis personally did I get any good at any skin thing whatsoever. When I go to evaluate a lesion, it’s pure pattern recognition.

AT: Yes.

OM: One dermatologist in med school told me: you don’t even take a history first. You do the exam first. You look at the skin, which is right there to see, and then you get the history of what led to the rash — after you’ve already seen it and formed some opinion based on your pretest probability. Time with the patient, an examination of the skin that takes you moving around them, being in the room with them. Before you look at the chart — who cares what the chart says? Nobody else has any idea how to do a derm exam. The computer is less useful. The exam is your expertise. Am I getting this right?

AT: I can’t say it any better. The thing about dermatology is that what’s going on is observable. And what’s observable describes the story that matters, which we then need to pursue. That can’t be relayed as well in other fields of specialty. Historically, they were just writing things down. “I saw a maculopapular rash” — and dermatologists are famously precise at describing things on the skin. Is it papular or macular? You never want to say both. That’s how you know you’re not a dermatologist.

OM: So the inability of other people to describe a lesion creates the medical specialty of dermatology.

AT: Yeah. You go in, you examine the patient, you see the observable phenomenon, and your clinical training prepares you for that assessment.

There’s this whole book — sort of our Bible — Fitzpatrick’s Color Atlas and Synopsis of Clinical Dermatology. It’s really thick. But how much of it do you see in clinical practice? Almost none of it. The reason that matters is because you need someone who will catch the one to three percent of the time that you’ll see these hundreds of different presentations. That’s where pattern recognition becomes important.

The reason dermatologists spend so much time with their patients is because they’re spending time with their skin . And the skin is the pattern you’re recognizing. Every person has areas of lesion versus not, so in each person you’re getting so much for your clinical expertise — especially if someone shows up with something rare. You want to know what it looks like for next time. Like a flagellate rash attributable to a weird mushroom someone ate. You’re not going to know that based on anything the person tells you — why would they mention they ate a weird mushroom? But you go see the rash and then you ask, “Hey, did you eat a weird mushroom?” And you’ve got the diagnosis.

From Dermatology to Psychiatry

OM: And eventually it turned out you didn’t want to be a dermatologist with your time. What happened?

AT: I had to really think about what was the thing that called me to medicine. We don’t always get a chance to sit with what brought us there — a lot of our training makes us keep running forward. COVID happened during my year in dermatology, and I had a moment to think about it, feel about it. Why did I want to hang out with people who had cancer? It was that I was seeing people suffering in isolation. I was seeing people suffering with the loneliness of a major medical illness. I realized it was the pain of the disease — the pain of the condition — that I cared about. So I needed to go to where they know about this sort of pain.

OM: Pain that you can’t see on the skin, at least not always.

AT: Sometimes you can — there’s a lot of relation between dermatological presentations and psychiatric pain. But I went to the place where they were talking about: How do people suffer? Why do they suffer? What can we do about that?

I was also discovering at the same time that you can involve yourself psychiatrically in cases of pretty severe cancer and improve five-year survivals more than being better at lymph node dissection or diagnosis. It makes you wonder about the power of the spirit, of will, of togetherness in suffering and loneliness.

OM: In psychiatry, as trained and defined at the time you did it, that was the specialty that spent the most time with human suffering and had the most engagement with the broader structures of a patient’s life that could alleviate suffering meaningfully.

AT: Yes. And psychiatrists are reflections of all illnesses, because anyone getting majorly sick is dealing in some way with the things we study and care about. Nothing that impacts your body doesn’t impact your soul.

Bipolar Mania and the Shame We Impose

OM: Following that thread, you ended up working in industry and on clinical trials related to the illness that I have — bipolar disorder — and on the side of the spectrum that most people don’t think about, which is bipolar mania. How did you get there?

AT: I have to go back to residency. Going from preparing to be a dermatologist to psychiatry, I went straight to one of our most acute units. What alarmed me most was that it felt like — and the data supports this — we weren’t doing anything for people who came in with suicidal ideation or after a suicide attempt. They weren’t leaving our hospitals any better. We were just making sure they were saying the right things.

This isn’t one hospital. This is healthcare systems at large. That made me frustrated, because these people then think, “I got the best care possible and I’m still hurting. There’s no hope.” That’s part of the reason we have even higher suicide rates after people leave the hospital. We’re not getting people better.

This hurt me, and you’ve already gotten a picture that when something hurts me, I go towards it. I got frustrated and thought: what are some mechanisms we can use to address the things that lead to suicidal risk? Not just diagnoses like bipolar disorder, but other constructs — irritation plus anxiety, thwarted belongingness, the sense of being disconnected from people. I stumbled upon the mechanisms that psychedelics influence and how there was a connection there. I started working on clinical trials with psychedelics and mood disorders, one thing led to another, and I got the chance to do this in industry.

Our first trial with our mechanism was bipolar disorder, which I cared about because it’s another illness where it often feels like we’re not doing much and we don’t have the treatments. This is also a population where clinicians can just deny you or not treat you when you need help, because this is a really difficult population in the hearts of clinicians. I didn’t like that, the same way I didn’t like how people coming in with suicidal ideation weren’t getting help.

OM: I’ve been spending time in bipolar influencer TikTok, and it’s grimly funny as someone who’s got it. Some of them are like, “if you don’t have bipolar, swipe away.” But there isn’t another illness where we expect the people suffering to apologize for their symptoms afterward. The role of shame in this disorder that we just accept — there are plenty of “get well” cards, “sorry you were sick.” But there’s no card that says, “Sorry you had sex with your best friend and then her best friend in the same night, and they both blamed you for it, and now you’re expected to apologize for the acute psychotic episode that led all your friends to abandon you.” You have to say sorry for it being your fault, even though we’re telling you it’s not your fault. Does this relate to why bipolar disorder carries one of the highest suicide risks?

AT: Is it the shame we play into? Even with things that are effective, like interpersonal social rhythms therapy — what if you just didn’t do it well enough because you were sick? Sleep disruption is both a symptom and a causative factor. “Oh, you should have gone to bed on time.” “Why did you go off your meds?” Because they didn’t work.

This speaks to something really real about humans. Depression — and I mean to be inflammatory with this statement, but I don’t mean to take away from the seriousness of depression — depression is a safe diagnosis compared to bipolar mania. If someone sees you depressed, they will feel sympathy for you. If you have bipolar mania, someone might feel a little sympathy, but there’s also a likelihood they’ll think, “stop doing that.” They’re not going to do that to someone with depression.

But change the depression scenario a little. If someone is depressed and is a financial drain on your resources, or an occupational presence in your home, you’ll start to feel feelings like “stop being depressed.” This reveals something real about humans: once something starts to affect us and we start to hurt about it, the way we deal with it is by creating a locus of control in that person. We say they could do something different because we’re hurting. We’re no longer allowing the possibility that they don’t want to do this.

OM: “Why can’t you just...”

AT: Even when we know there’s a good answer to that. When we hurt, we’re so bad at seeing that someone else is hurting.

Why Bipolar Mania Trials Fail in the United States

OM: You mentioned running clinical trials in this challenging part of a challenging illness. What makes someone a great candidate for a bipolar mania clinical trial? Who’s perfect for that study from your perspective?

AT: This is tricky. Before the Russia-Ukraine conflict, a lot of sponsors and pharma companies focused on running clinical trials in Russia, Ukraine, and other Eastern European countries. Everyone has a different idea of why, but this is a true statement: there is no industry-sponsored trial of bipolar mania, powered enough to achieve what you need for drug approval, that has been successful with a majority of its enrollment based in the US. At least in the past couple of decades.

OM: And for the audience, one reason we’re differentiating modern and historic trials is that placebo response rates change over time 2 . A trial done ten years ago and a trial done twenty years ago have different underlying math about what you need to show.

AT: Right. And maybe that math is changing differently in Eastern Europe compared to the US. When you start talking about placebo response, that’s an end-stage result. It doesn’t necessarily mean people in the US have a high placebo response and people in Russia don’t. There are a lot of different reasons.

Some people say investigators in those countries know their subjects better. Here, the subjects entering bipolar mania trials are often not people the investigator knows well. It could also be that in those countries, you can get someone into a trial who is more manic. Part of that is a more paternalistic culture — which isn’t necessarily a bad thing, because what we’re saying is that someone is very sick. Over there, when someone is very sick, you can get them in a trial. Here, we apply a judgment: since this illness affects consciousness, we say they can no longer participate because it affects their ability to consent.

OM: They’re too impaired to consent — which also means they’re very sick.

AT: And this goes back to the earlier point about why we make people with mania apologize. We’re applying a variably subjective evaluation of consciousness instead of just saying someone was sick. And we’re not running these trials on acute agitation — we’re running them on the disease state. In acute agitation, you can get past the consent issue because the situation may be life-threatening. But if they’re not life-threatening, or your intervention doesn’t work fast enough, or there’s no injectable form — we’re missing a lot of really sick people.

Maybe there’s something about the culture elsewhere that allows for different acuity levels. Higher numbers regress to the mean no matter what. If you have sicker people enrolled in your study, you have more delta to work with. If we’re screening people out, we’re not conducting studies in the US that demonstrate our interventions are effective. We’ve had to adopt transnational study designs in this disease state because — why would a drug company run a study that’s more likely to fail? They wouldn’t. But now we’re forced to get better at it because of a war that halted large-scale studies in the countries that had great enrollment populations for bipolar mania.

The YMRS Problem

OM: Bipolar disorder has become a socialized diagnosis through digital media. A lot of people identify with it who may or may not meet clinical thresholds.

AT: This is the Young Mania Rating Scale, or YMRS, that we use to say someone has severe enough bipolar disorder for a clinical trial. Usually people say you need at least 20 points to qualify.

If you look at some of these items — elevated mood, euphoric, inappropriate laughter, singing — you can give someone four points. And the difference between “mildly possibly increased” and “possibly increased on questioning” is not well standardized rater to rater.

OM: What’s the interrater reliability of the YMRS?

AT: I actually don’t know off the top of my head, but it’s low.

Editorial Input from Owen after the interview: It’s relatively high as a absolute number, which doesn’t mean it’s good enough in clinical trials that are trying to enroll people who may or may not have the underling illness:

Kappa = 0.85 to 0.93 for total scores. It is a widely validated, 11-item clinician-administered tool used to assess manic symptom severity, with individual item reliability ranging from 0.67 to 0.95.

Some items, like irritability, are designed to carry up to eight points versus four on other items. All you have to do is make the interviewer feel like you are interrupting them, that they got cut short. A lot of this is a subjective sense. If the interviewer feels, “they cut me off quite a bit” or “they were kind of irritable” — it’s not an objective measure. It’s based on the interviewer.

In a culture where being cut off is more normative, you’d just rate it lower. You didn’t feel cut off. These are subjective measures by the interviewer. It’s a clinician-rated scale, not an objective third-party-watching-the-interview scale. And once I say “Owen keeps interrupting me,” I can also give eight points on speech — because he’s uninterruptible. Same coin, different sides. That’s already 16 points from just two items.

There are these different things that can easily inflate the score. And there’s a lot of personality overlap, ADHD overlap that can scale you up. Those are very hard to parse out as well.

The Perfect Subject and the Unicorn Problem

OM: If you’re on the sponsor side designing study criteria and you want the trial to work, what does the ideal patient look like?

AT: This gets into why other countries may do better than us — because we overall have, excuse my language, shit healthcare. Other systems might be able to document if you had a prior diagnosis, a prior episode, prior treatment. It’s very difficult for us to do that here. In fact, we usually can’t.

Someone on their first episode of bipolar mania is not someone we really want in the trial. We want someone who has had several episodes, or at least one before, because it increases our confidence that they actually have bipolar disorder. Who’s defining it and who’s saying that is very difficult, especially in the US. When someone tells me, “I had bipolar mania a hundred times in the past couple years,” you start to question: by whose standard?

OM: You want someone who has had treatment experience before, because it increases your confidence in the diagnosis?

I’ll give you an example — I was looking at the bipolar depression trials in fMRI-guided accelerated TMS. They’ve run three of those now, and two of them I think are bad a reason I’ll throw at you. They used the Antidepressant Treatment History Form to stratify refractory bipolar depression. That form only includes antidepressant medicines with no known efficacy in bipolar depression — medications that are prescribed in 70% of people treated for bipolar disorder, even though it’s recommended against by several different bodies. Not only does it make me less confident you have bipolar disorder — because otherwise someone probably would have given you lithium or Depakote — but the treatment was never going to work. So how does it matter for your refractoriness? Things that didn’t work, didn’t work. That tells me very little.

AT: Exactly. On the flip side, the question you’re alluding to is: did you receive a treatment we’d reasonably expect to influence what you’re experiencing? And then, did you get better on it or not? We don’t have great ways to answer any of this. If you got better with Zoloft from your mania, I actually have less faith that you have bipolar disorder.

OM: So the person with bipolar mania who, in their first episode, was naked in the streets screaming about UFOs, and then it abruptly stopped when they got lithium — they have classic bipolar disorder. And they weren’t on cannabis, they weren’t drinking.

AT: They’re a unicorn. Yes. And this is rare — 70% of people are not recognized, or they’re treated wrong, or it was mild the first time.

OM: But essentially, what you’re looking for is : is it actually bipolar disorder? That’s the most important thing before you enroll them in a trial for a disorder they might or might not have. Because enrolling people who don’t have bipolar in a bipolar mania trial will skew the results?

AT: Correct. The perfect subject for a trial — which is difficult to quantify — is someone who has a bonafide presentation of mania, observed, leading to an intervention. And the intervention is a treatment we know someone would respond to if they have mania. Lithium is the best example, which is also very rarely prescribed. We need to know they responded to it. And we need to know what their baseline is, and that they’re above their baseline when they enter the trial again.

OM: So you’re looking for this very rare subgroup, in part because the US has such a terrible health system for people with bipolar. People who were evaluated, correctly diagnosed, and treated successfully — which also probably reduces the chance they’re excited about a clinical trial. “Just give me the lithium that worked again, doc.”

AT: Right. And people do quite frequently have breakthrough episodes. If you respond to lithium, that’s great, and usually you’ll be fine for a long time if you’re one of the roughly one-third who are lithium super responders. But a lot of people will have breakthrough episodes.

Time-to-Event Trials and the Outcomes That Matter

OM: This is why I think time-to-event trials in bipolar disorder are great. My dream outcome measure is time-to-girlfriend.

AT: Because it’s something patients would actually care about.

OM: It would work in schizophrenia, too! Can you imagine advertising a drug: “With new Goplexitol , time to romantic relationship was improved by 3.1 months.” As comedic as that sounds, it’s also actually important. You have to pull together a lot of things to have a romantic relationship. Time to meaningful relationship — that’s really why we care about negative symptoms in schizophrenia, why we care about bipolar mania. Time to job. Time to feeling okay . And you get a compound endpoint with the statistical power of time-to-event as opposed to categorical endpoints, which makes my math nerd heart sing.

AT: Exactly. Patients would love to have a full, rich life!

Why Patients Go Off Their Meds — And Why We Shouldn’t Blame Them

OM: Can we study anything as monotherapy for bipolar mania?

AT: Yes, and part of the reason is that the average person with relapsing mania comes off their medications, or they’re not receiving treatment, or they’re receiving poor treatment. Unlike their “epileptic” colleagues, people with bipolar disorder are more likely to discontinue treatment.

OM: In epilepsy, your insight isn’t necessarily as impaired.

AT: Going off your drugs is common in bipolar disorder — in fact, it’s almost the rule. On average, someone with bipolar mania who’s treated will likely come off their medication in a month or two.

And part of that is not actually the fault of someone with bipolar mania. We have utterly intolerable medications.

OM: It may not even be bad judgment.

AT: This is something everyone should understand: if you are on an antipsychotic correctly and for the duration of your life, are you going to die from the antipsychotic or from your illness? The average antipsychotic shortens life by about three years. It’s often the antipsychotics’ adverse effects that kills you.

OM: Had I been less adherent, I might not have tardive dyskinesia. But I was very adherent, for good or ill.

AT: I’m sorry. That’s a hard thing to know.

OM: But what we have is patients with bipolar disorder making choices that to some degree are rational. Going off your meds in an episodic condition is what we do with recurrent bacterial infections — you do a course of treatment. We’ve chosen to define bipolar disorder differently, and for medications like lithium, that’s probably the right call. But it may be that we got it wrong with these other acute treatments for mania. Maybe they’d be better understood as courses of treatment rather than daily, indefinite symptomatic management.

AT: And maintenance trials — the way they’re run is that you look at the difference in time to the next episode between your medication and placebo. And the patient still has that episode on the medication. For Lamotrigine, it was 90 versus 170 days. You can’t say, “I know for sure this will prevent your episode” or that it will prevent it to a meaningful degree. There’s inter-episode morbidity that’s pretty bad, and you’re dealing with adverse events on top of it. It’s very difficult to say, “You need to be doing this.”

We like to blame patients with bipolar disorder for going off their meds, but in our clinical trial data — which is a subset of people who are on average less sick, because they could consent to a trial — they’re getting sick again anyway. That’s how we know the difference between treatment and placebo: you get sick slightly faster on placebo. But you still get sick in the future even if you took the medication. And yet we say, “Did you go off your meds, bro?”

We also have this idea that if you have another “naked” illness — you go out there without medication and have another episode — it’s going to be more difficult to treat. We don’t really have great proof of that, but that’s another judgment we impose.

The Best Preventative Treatment

AT: This is why I’m so interested in cohort support and peer support. In a way, for people with bipolar disorder, having friends — especially friends who understand — is the protection against long-term morbidity when they inevitably have another episode. Having friends around who can catch it early and keep you well — this is for everything, not just bipolar disorder. It’s especially bipolar disorder, but it’s everything. Bipolar disorder is a good teacher in this regard.

If I could prescribe the best preventative treatment to anyone, it would be a friend who cares and whom they trust.

OM: I love that. Let’s leave it there.

Dr. Ahmed Tahseen is a psychiatrist and medical director who leads bipolar disorder and depression clinical trials. Dr. Owen Muir is a psychiatrist, co-founder and CMO of Radial , and the Editor-in-Chief of The Frontier Psychiatrists.

I have to admit, spending any time with the news is difficult. A lot of dreadfully stressful things are happening in the world right now, and this article is quite mindful and not talking about those events. Most people can decompress with celebrity gossip. Not this guy. As my readers might recall, I am a physician, and I have bipolar disorder.

I have not written a book on this topic. I consider the best of the bunch to be penned by Mark Vonnegut, M.D., the son of Kurt. It’s called “ Just Like Someone Without Mental Illness Only More So .”

When I do make the mistake of reading celebrity gossip, I inevitably hear stories about people going off the rails. That is me being polite. I see psychopathology, particularly my kind of psychopathology, bipolar disorder, behind a lot of celebrity news.

Kanye West has struggled with this problem. Think what you will of his forays into racism, nudity, or music, it's hard to walk away thinking he's an easy person to get along with. One of the Hallmark symptoms of mania, the flashy albeit markedly less sympathetic pole of bipolar disorder, is irritability.

It looks pristine, but a hole in the roof was also present, leaking water into the kitchen. I resolved to treat it as “no big deal.” © Owen Muir, 2026.

I'm lucky in one way, which is that I'm a male human in America. It's socially normative for us to get angry. This is undercut, for those of us with publicly acknowledged bipolar disorder, by the fact that everyone thinks you're an unstable lunatic. You can't get normal angry about things one could reasonably be angry about without someone worrying, either publicly or privately, that you might be sick again.

Part of the most frustrating part, for those of us in the stage of the illness with more preserved insight, is that sometimes they're right. Anger, rage, irritability, these are all hallmarks of mania and even a mixed episode—that's when you can be depressed and a little bit manic— or a lot manic— at the same time. High energy unpleasantness.

I've written before about things people with bipolar disorder can do to manage their illness. Today's column is about what managing that illness successfully can look like when it pays off. It's one sentence my daughter said to me, right at bedtime, as she was getting into bed with mom and dad and getting ready to listen to her second favorite podcast, Acquired .

That's quite a cliffhanger! What did Quinn say? You're going to have to wait, readers. If I were a real sadist, I'd put a paywall right here.

A moment on irritability from my perspective, as both a psychiatrist and a patient with bipolar disorder.

Irritability is a lot like playing life in a rigged casino. If you imagine that roulette was a game where you could only stay calm if your bet paid off, then bipolar mania would be the process of gradually, slowly, placing higher and higher risk bets only. The parent rate on black or red—who am I kidding, obviously red!—is south of one-to-one, enough for the house to win. But you won't immediately bankrupt yourself if you put all your money on red. Similar things can be said for even or odd. As mania gets more severe, not only do the other symptoms exacerbate—the grandiosity ramps up, the insight trails off, your judgment gets worse, your impulsivity increases, and your desire to do things like literally gamble increases. Pleasurable activities with a high risk for painful consequences become the norm. But in my imagined casino of life, the bets are placed on staying calm , and every time you lose, the irritability can take over. The odds on the single-number bet in roulette are 35 to 1. Now imagine every time you lose a bet, you blow up at your partner. Or somebody you need to succeed in life. Or, once you become a parent, your own child.

Kids remember things. My daughter remembers things remarkably well. My son is not worse—he's just a little less flashy about it. Traveling around Japan with my two 9-year-old children last year, it was Trent who helped us make our way on the subways of Japan. “It's the next stop, pay attention dad!”

As a child and adult Psychiatrist, many of my patients tell me stories about their families. One of the things that stands out, in memory, is ragefulness. I can't recall one time in the entire history of my clinical practice where somebody told me a story of a rage-filled parent that was positive. It's always a negative memory, and it can lead to therapy being necessary or adjunctive, part of your treatment plan for the rest of your life. Being a rageful parent is a terrible thing, for your children, and for you.

It's the kind of failure that none of us wants to be. For parents with bipolar disorder, it's not always an option to keep their rage in check. There are times when they're playing anger roulette. The only way to win roulette, statistically, is to walk away from the table. In bipolar disorder, walking away from the table means a lot of very difficult choices.

It means sleeping regularly. It means waking up at the same time every day. It means taking medicine that you need to take when you need to take it. It means taking care of your general medical health, because that is also more likely to affect how close you are to the edge of anger. Anything that makes you more likely to be more irritable is a bad plan. It might mean a ketogenic diet. It probably means exercise. It means no cocaine, it means no alcohol, and it means no cannabis. It probably means no recreational drugs at all.

However, there are plenty of times where, assuming you do both a good job at the above lifestyle choices and get lucky with the course of your illness, you do have a choice about how to respond.

Today, I advocate to all my readers, both those with and without bipolar disorder, to consider treating nothing as a big deal that's worth getting angry about if you can possibly avoid it.

I'll give you an example from yesterday. There was an incident with the car. It was the car that I drive, but it wasn't me driving it. I am not going to embarrass Carlene by showing pictures of the car. There was a parking lot incident. She was not pleased, and it was not particularly exciting to me. The car will need subsequent evaluation. Nobody was hurt. Somebody was parked illegally. These things happen.

As someone with bipolar disorder who is not currently struggling with symptoms, I had a choice. I could walk away from anger. So I did.

The downside of walking away from an anger response to anything is close to zero. Particularly when you're in a long-term relationship, the cost of anger is high, and the payout is extremely low. In any long-enough-term relationship, you will also be the person in the hot seat in the future, and you probably already have been in the past. However, the odds of getting to play are different when you have bipolar disorder. They make him a time when you can't freely choose how calm you will stay.

So, I say to you, euthymic people of the world—choose calm, choose kindness. It's particularly important when, over the long arc of time, not every bet your place will be of your own free will. Most of us will need grace from others. We will certainly be in a position to pray to the Lord— if you are a person of faith. The Lord might dish out Grace willy nilly, but our sons and daughters are more Old Testament than New.

Being calm—when the other option is, reasonably, anger? That is a deposit into the bank of Grace, from which you can later, in hard times, withdraw. Be kind now. Be kind in every moment. Don't be angry. Every time you choose not to be angry, when somebody would understand how angry you are, that's something they might remember too. If you do it enough, people will remember you as a kind and gracious person who doesn't get angry.

One of the nice things about having children is that they didn't know you before they were born. It's like a clean slate! That you could get away with being in your 20s, and maybe even 30s, you get the slate wiped clean when you have a child. But thereafter, you stopped writing in chalk and started etching in stone.

Anger is memorable. It's broken plates, it's a bad reputation, it's resentful children. It's a failure , you could worry, as a parent.

If you're playing the odds, with an uncertain hand, in any moment, when you can choose calmness—that's the right bet! Never be unkind when you could be otherwise.

This is not advice I routinely have been able to follow—and my apologies to my colleague, stuck on the opposite side of me on a peer-to-peer review. I know, it can be quite difficult when someone's all riled up.

There are plenty of things in the world that can get us angry. That should get us angry, and I'm not getting into what those are.

Here are some steps to staying calm in moments when the heat starts to rise:

• Breathe deeply . I know this sounds hokey, but you have these stretch receptors near your lungs that can feel how much you're breathing and how deeply. Your body assumes that if you're breathing deeply, things can't be that bad. It might even make sense to practice breathing deeply when nothing is going on one way or the other, so that you're really used to it and can pull that tool out when you need it.

• Imagine all the worst responses you could have to your situation . Dialectical behavioral therapy, as communicated to me by my wife’s friend, Blaise Aguirre, M.D., has a solution. He has a hilarious acronym for this, called SCREW. This is an anti-DBT skill. It helps you remember all the ways you can make a situation change—and this works best if you actually lean in on detailing how you're gonna do both the E and the W!

• S olve the problem

• C hange your relationship to the problem

• R adically accept the problem

• E mbrace Misery

• W orsen the Problem

• Inject humor into every problem you face , as you are able. That's the point of embracing misery, and the worse the problem responses. They exist in the acronym to encourage you to think about what embracing misery would look like —the more outrageous this is, the less likely you already do it. For example, with my car situation yesterday, I could've spent the rest of the day looking at pictures of my car, rocking back and forth under a blanket, eating endless ice cream, or the like. To truly worsen the problem, I could've crashed my wife's car into a wall, also. That would show her! I could scream at my wife in front of our kids, telling her she needs to be more careful, in an angry and vengeful tone. However, this would make the problem worse. If I wanted to get even stupider about it, I could lay booby traps in the driveway so that the tow truck returning the car before it can go to the shop, we'll have its tires blown up— That would definitely make the problem worse. I'd probably be facing jail time! Etc. You can really go to town with this image, exercise, and by the time you do, you're probably decide that solving the problem or radically accepting the problem are better ways to live.

• Get a little over-the-top with your kindness, so you build up a good track record! This doesn't have to be expensive; it can be as simple as making dinner every night for a week. Or offering to take your kid to their sport, so your partner doesn't have to. My wife really likes Diet Coke, and she really likes it when I bring it to her in the morning. I have arthritis, and this is not necessarily easy for me. I limp down to get a Diet Coke anyway. Because God, she loves it. She knows I do it every day. Being consistent with your kindness, and being one notch more kind than you might have been at baseline, is a great way to build up a track record.

• Practice calmness when the stakes are low. There are times when other people will get upset, and it's pretty reliable. Babies cry when they're not fed. Your team at work gets frustrated when something doesn't go just right, and in many of these situations, it might not be a big deal to you. That's a great excuse to practice being calm. The point of this practice is to give you a routine that you can fall back on when you're actually upset with yourself. Showing up and being kind when things are difficult for other people is awesome practice, and, again, it's money in the bank of Grace for you when your steps falter.

• Embrace kindness when the stakes are high . My example for this is a man I knew, Gerry Pietroforte . Gerry and I are both Italians, and we're not necessarily known for our culture of calmness. His whole job, and I'm not kidding, was staying calm. I've never seen so many genuine mourners at someone's funeral in my life. A lawyer by training, some of his work included helping pilot companies that were going to sh*t into their crash landings—he did this, famously, at Lehman Brothers. He was the guy you brought in to help make sure that the flaming wreckage of a disaster was less of a flaming wreck for everybody. His tool for doing this was relentless kindness. He was a big deal in business. However, his obituary reads:

• He was an avid "Superfan" of his sons' numerous teams from pee wee sports through their college athletic endeavors in baseball and lacrosse. When he was not on the sidelines cheering a team on, Jerry loved to play golf and bicycle, particularly in far flung destinations.

• Use potent skills to stay calm when it gets hard. My favorite biological hack to calm yourself down is called “ice diving.” It's the process of hijacking the mammalian dive reflex!

You can use this skill out and about with a cold can of soda—hold it against your face, right below the eye (this is division V2 of cranial nerve V), lean forward, and hold your breath for 45 or so seconds, and then repeat a few times—you don’t need to use literal ice water every time.

With all that said, attentive readers will note I have been making them wait for what Quinn said to me, just the other night, that was my big win as a parent. She’s almost 10.

“Daddy never gets angry,”

She asserted to Mommy. It's a lot of work to stay well and to walk away from the roulette wheel of rage, but with a payoff like this, who could resist the lure of always betting on calm?

Michelle: So how would you work in a broken system more successfully?

Owen: Curiosity. These moments of genuine curiosity, trust, and authenticity. Understanding. Trying to understand. And recognizing when you didn’t—that’s the real one.

Michelle: Recognizing when you didn’t get it?

Owen: Yes! Or, telling a joke that someone actually finds funny. Because you’ve imagined what they’d laugh at and you’re not going to offend them. “Oh, you did a good job of getting me,”—and that thing you said wasn’t expected, but it was palatable to me.

Michelle: Humor.

Owen: Humor is inherently mentalized if it’s funny. Off-color humor is missing the mark. It’s not mentalizing the person. And the response is offense, because you misunderstood me.

And the flip side: I said something sarcastic to you earlier, and you knew it was sarcasm.

Michelle: Because I know you’re a good person, and I know that must have been sarcastic.

Owen: So you’ve mentalized that joke. I actually meant the opposite of what I said. And it’s mentalizing that lets us work out that something offhanded—one sentence which is the opposite of the truth—is in fact meant to underline the point. You felt me on that, not as an insult, but with respect. Because you trust me.

And this is why reincorporation—callbacks—work in comedy. “Oh, that’s the thing you set up twenty minutes ago!” We both understand why the callback is funny, because we shared that experience. That’s mentalizing.

The Chuckles Safety Plan

Owen: Look, I know we’re gonna need a safety plan here. So—what’s gonna work? My safety plans are ridiculous. I want you to take Chuckles when things get really bad.

Michelle: Like the candy?

Owen: The candy. Only when it’s serious.

Michelle: That sounds like a stupid safety plan.

Owen: It sounds like a stupid safety plan, but you’re laughing.

Michelle: Because it’s not pretending to be a serious safety plan. You’re presenting it with a wink and a nod. And yet you’re suggesting it—basically making a mockery of all other safety plans while you do it.

Owen: The thing that keeps you safe is mentalizing. How easy is it to kill yourself if you’re curious about what you’re doing?

And the suggestion to take a Chuckles as a treatment for acute suicidality is—

Michelle: Ridiculous.

Owen: But who made it?

Michelle: Someone who cares about you.

Owen: And suddenly, instead of thinking about killing yourself, you’re thinking about how funny the person who cares about you is. That they thought this would be helpful. And then you’re thinking about being cared for. Suddenly, someone held your mind in mind. And you felt understood.

The data backs it up. Mentalizing is basically a way they found to teach therapists how to be people.

The Language Has to Inspire Trust

Owen: The language matters because the language has to inspire trust in us to go with it. When I explain mentalization-based treatment, has anyone in the room heard this term before? No? Great. That means I can define it any way I want.

Michelle: It sounds smart, right?

Owen: Too many things we don’t know sound smart. And for some people, it sounds too smart. British accent equals— “Oh, I shouldn’t believe this, it’s different from my accent, and it’s probably something smart people do.” The exact same accent might have a totally opposite effect on you, depending on where you come from.

I’ve done MBT trainings where I do the introductory material, and the younger psychiatrists are like, “Wow, that’s great,”—and the older ones are like, “Eh.” Then a more senior colleague presents the same material, and the older psychiatrists are like, “This is great!” And the younger ones are lost.

I had assumed my introduction was way better. But it was only more relatable to some of the audience.

Michelle: So you want both.

Owen: Right. Having two different people teach it, even if it’s the same thing twice, is going to be different enough that it speaks to different people and reinforces the thing. This is why, in any given setting, you should have someone who’s culturally relevant to the audience and someone who’s not.

What Limits Us Is Simple

Michelle: How do you do it? How do you actually help people?

Owen: It’s about being curious. Making people feel comfortable enough to actually—

What limits us in psychiatry is really simple. The data we have is largely based on what people feel comfortable enough to actually tell us. And if we’ve built a system full of pretend mode—safety plans nobody believes in, handcuffs for panic attacks, hierarchies that ignore the people spending thousands of hours with patients—we are eroding the trust that is the only tool we have to get the real information we need.

Michelle: Most useful thing I was ever told was:

“if you need nursing care, you’re in a hospital. If you only need a doctor, you go to a doctor’s office. If you’re in a hospital, it’s because you need nurses around the clock.”

Most doctors don’t think that.

Owen: You don’t have to tell anybody that the nurses run the show. Except for the doctors. They don’t seem to get that.

Michelle: A good doctor, a good nurse—they’re both as important. They need each other. But not some power-hungry know-it-all—

Owen: Because knowing it all isn’t that helpful. Not in psychiatry. Not in medicine. I know a lot of things. I can tell most of my patients what to do from the get-go. But knowing what to do and getting someone to trust you enough to do it—those are two completely different problems.

Michelle: I guess that’s what it all is. It’s about being a good human.

Owen: Being a good human. Plus, when weird things happen, you should be able to recognize the difference.

All mentalization-based treatment is: some very, very thoughtful, smart people figured out that trust is really important, and that curiosity helps us get there faster and less dangerously than any other approach. And they figured out a way to turn that into a treatment.

And so I think it’s two steps. It’s being curious. And it’s getting back to being curious once we realize we’ve mucked it up.

This post is a guest post by my dear, dear friend David Carreon, M.D. He’s start a substack of his own: . Subscribe already! This post of his is a meditation on joy…

Joy is one of the most sublime of human experiences. In Galatians, Paul lists it among the “fruit of the Spirit,” alongside love, patience, and self-control, as a mark of Spirit-formed life. Not an achievement but an outgrowth, the way an apple is the outgrowth of a tree that is doing what apple trees do. And yet joy is precisely the thing that appears to be dying on the vine.

Various flavors of doomerism fill the air: despair over the coming AI, the corruption in high places that never seems to result in prosecution, the economy (stupid). Antidepressants are now a normal part of the medical landscape in the United States. About one in seven adults reported using a prescription antidepressant in a recent survey period, a figure that would have been almost unimaginable when these drugs first came to market. Joy is not in abundance.

But the shortage is stranger than mere sadness. It is not that we have forgotten how to mourn. It is that we have forgotten how to stop mourning. There was a time, in healthier ages, when the calendar did the work for you: clear seasons of grief and clear seasons of celebration, a rhythm as old as Ecclesiastes (or the Byrds). A time to weep, and a time to laugh. The genius of the liturgical year, and of every good civic calendar, was that it gave you permission to do one thing at a time. You could be fully present to sorrow on Good Friday because Easter was coming. You could feast without guilt because the fast had been real.

We seem to have lost that rhythm. A gray fog, not quite depression, not quite despair, but a perpetual overcast, has settled over our celebrations. Those celebrating their own mothers do so with a visible asterisk on Mother’s Day, because not everyone has a mother. Those wanting to celebrate a promotion, or a vacation, or a healthy newborn baby are silently pressured to not be too happy about it because others who hear them might not have those things. Which is true. Of course it is true. But in a civilization that has lost the rhythm of mourning and feasting, the mourners do not get better comfort. They merely get the cold satisfaction of knowing that everyone else is a little less happy too.

But not at the Olympics. At the Olympics, the fog broke.

• • •

Two images from the Milano Cortina Olympics have been circling my mind ever since I saw them, and they will not leave.

The first is Alysa Liu, spinning free on the ice with a broad smile on her face, in bliss, as she does the thing that she is, by definition, the best in the world at. Olympic gold in women’s figure skating, ending a drought in the American program that had lasted long enough for people to forget what it looked like when it rained.

The second is Jack Hughes, who lost teeth to a high stick in the gold medal game against Canada and then came back to score the sudden-death overtime winner that delivered Team USA the gold. Bloodied and grinning. Or trying to grin, with what remained.

The one a picture of feminine grace: beauty, elegance, ecstasy. The other masculine and beaten and victorious. Both are pictures of triumph. Both are pictures of people who have learned what joy is. And I have been brooding, yes, truly brooding, over why these images pierced the fog when so little else does.

• • •

It struck me as I reflected on the pair: their jobs are secure.

I have been anxious about our future. What role do ordinary humans have in a world where AI is advancing rapidly, with no signs that we are hitting a ceiling and some signs that we are beginning an acceleration? I’ve written before about whether the coming intelligence will take the shape of a monolith or a mosaic, and I still believe the mosaic is the better hope. But the deeper question has been gnawing at me: What do we contribute that cannot be optimized away?

And I think the answer was on the ice.

We love watching other humans operate at the peak of their performance. We do not love watching accountants at the peak of theirs. We do not tune in to watch bankers. We appreciate the human stories behind programming or law, but we rarely watch people engaged in the technical act of their work. Sports are different because sports are whole-person events. The figure skater has to know where she is in space, her body tracking the angle of the spin, her eyes registering the ice and the blur of the crowd, her memory holding the choreography she has rehearsed ten thousand times. And then there is the thing that may have pushed Liu over the edge into ultimate victory: the sheer fullness of joy that was visibly coursing through her. Not just the perfection of the technical execution, not just the creativity, but the pure unadulterated joy she experienced on the ice, deep emotion pouring out through her movements so that even a viewer who knows nothing about figure skating could see it.

For hockey, add to all of that the presence of other minds: you have to model what the defender will do, plan three moves ahead, feint one way and cut another, all while absorbing punishing contact from players whose full-time job is to stop you. Everything happening at once. The brain and the body it is connected to, pushed to the absolute limit.

This is what we are built for. Not the narrow, efficient execution of a single optimized task (machines do that better already) but the whole thing. The feeling and the planning and the courage and the memory and the spatial awareness and the improvisation and the pain and the joy, fused together in a body that is hurtling across the ice at thirty miles per hour. No machine will replicate that. And even if one did, we would not care. We watch sports because we are watching one of our own do something we can barely imagine doing, and in their triumph, we participate. Their joy is our joy, received in diminished form but received all the same.

• • •

In one of my favorite films, Pixar’s Inside Out (perhaps unsurprisingly for a psychiatrist), there is a scene where the protagonist, Riley, recalls skating on a frozen Minnesota lake with her family. No competition. No stakes. Just a girl moving across the ice in freedom, the interior emotion Joy dancing alongside her in a way the animators rendered so beautifully it makes my heart ache. In the sequel, Inside Out 2, Anxiety seizes the controls during hockey tryouts, the fear of losing, the terror that her future is slipping away, and Riley becomes a clenched, desperate version of herself. The resolution comes when Joy returns, divine (literally?) sunlight pierces the darkness, and Riley stops performing and starts playing again, doing it for the pure love of the thing.

That trajectory, from compulsion to freedom, from anxiety to joy, is not only Riley’s story. It is Alysa Liu’s. Her father has estimated spending half a million to a million dollars on her training. Countless falls. Countless hours before dawn. And then she stepped away, burned out, the love of it gone. When she returned, it was different. She returned for the thing itself, not for the medal. And then the medal came anyway.

What if that is a picture of something larger than skating? Humans work, but what if we didn’t have to toil?

After Adam followed his wife into disobedience, Scripture says the ground itself was cursed: “Cursed is the ground for thy sake; in sorrow shalt thou eat of it all the days of thy life; Thorns also and thistles shall it bring forth to thee; and thou shalt eat the herb of the field; In the sweat of thy face shalt thou eat bread, till thou return unto the ground” (Genesis 3:17-19, KJV). Before the garden, their job was to tend the garden. They had work to do. But something about the punishment changed it, the same effort producing less, or producing something wrong, so that despite our best intentions the labor never quite yields what we meant it to yield. The ice is there, but now it is cold, and the falls hurt, and the thorns come up where we planted grain.

And yet. And yet. Two athletes rose above the thorns last month. Perhaps the technology, the skate, the stick, the arena, is not the enemy of the human but the instrument of the human’s freedom, the way an ice skate is not an obstacle to the dancer but the very thing that lets her fly.

• • •

As the film Chariots of Fire imagines Eric Liddell saying: “God made me fast, and when I run, I feel His pleasure.” This captures something about our species. There is a moment in competition, in running, in skating, in the full-body exertion of a human being doing the thing they were made to do, when transcendence breaks through. We hold a lighter or a cell phone flashlight up at a concert. We clap our hands. We pray on our knees or faces. We worship with arms raised. We are embodied creatures. Whatever happens in the future, whether or not we can upload our consciousness into the cloud, our species is and has always been embodied. The joy we feel is not some ethereal, disembodied abstraction. It runs through nerves and muscles and the wild electrochemistry of a brain that is, for one impossible moment, doing everything at once.

I know this is true because it happened to me.

I was a young medical student, bicycling back from class on a fall evening. Nothing remarkable about the night, nothing I had sought or planned. And unbidden, I began to sing Joy to the World in my head. As I sang, something shifted. The only way I can describe it is that the veil dropped, and I saw things as they really were. When the line came, and heaven and nature sing, nature truly did sing. The moon sang. The red brick rooftops sang. The trees and the sky sang. Tears of joy streamed down my face, and I was grateful. Grateful to God, who had created such wonderful things and placed me, like Adam in the garden, to enjoy them.

I did not earn that moment. I was not operating at the peak of anything. I was just a tired student on a bicycle. But joy came anyway, the way grace often does: unasked for, unsought, arriving on an ordinary evening like an unexpected visitor. Some of the most joyful moments in my life have been in worship. But that night was the most joyful of all. And it was given, not achieved.

As we build these machines, and we will build them, and they will be extraordinary, let us remind ourselves, and perhaps the machines too, that joy is a superpower. Not because it is useful, though it may be. Because it is valuable in itself. Let us use our ten fingers to play beautiful music and sing with our human voices, songs of old and songs of our new age. Let us seek joy. And create ever more moments, and minutes, and hours, and days where we can be full of it.

People sometimes ask (often ironically): What is the purpose of life? The question is impossible in some worldviews and leads to jokes in others (42). But for my own, the question can be answered straightforwardly. The Westminster Shorter Catechism gives: “Man’s chief end is to glorify God, and to enjoy Him forever.” Two jobs, but really one job. Glory, which is a kind of brightness. And Enjoy. In that relationship between God and man, we can experience joy most fully, not as an escape from the body but as the body’s highest expression. Not as a vacation from reality but as the deepest contact with it.

Whether we are at the beginning of the end of our species because the machines will rise and crush us, or whether this is the beginning of us as the joyous operators of infinitely capable machines, either way, the assignment does not change. Glorify and enjoy. Rise above the thorns. Seek the ice.

My four-year-old, Claire, while on the way to visit a family member last week, remarked to my wife from her car seat: “I love being in this life.” And my two-year-old, Josie, who cannot yet articulate the theology, knows it too, in the way that only a toddler can know something, with her whole body, before she has the words. She does a happy wiggle when things are going her way. This morning it was while eating oatmeal .

Two little children understand this better than I do. And perhaps this is part of what Jesus meant when he said, “Let the little children come to me and do not hinder them, for to such belongs the kingdom of heaven” (Matthew 19:14, ESV). Claire and Josie know something I have forgotten. They do not need a gold medal to feel it. They do not need to defeat the machines or solve the alignment problem or figure out the future of the species. Claire is riding in a car, looking out the window, and the world is luminous. Josie dancing with joy because of oatmeal.

I was on a bicycle once, on a fall evening, and the world was luminous too. I had just forgotten for a while. Let us all remember what we once knew.
