Mental health parity is the updated law of the land. Payers are in an unenviable position. They need to reach “parity” in mental health services, which means they’re supposed to be paid in the same manner as general medical care. Let’s dig into this idea and one proposed solution that, be forewarned, is dumb.

This is an absurd and terrible standard, to be excruciatingly clear. The reason is similar to the statistical principle of rejecting the null hypothesis. The null hypothesis is a core science concept in statistics, the math we use to test our biomedical science. The null hypothesis (in the figure below, H 0) says: “There is no difference between these two things, and any difference is due to chance and measurement error.

When we ask: “is this drug different from placebo?” we assume something crucial. It should be different and BETTER than a placebo. We don’t want interventions that are worse than placebo. No matter how hard the marketing firm leans in, it’s hard to sell:

“New Drug—Now, EVEN WORSE than what you’ve been putting up with.”

However, with mental health parity as a hypothesis, it does matter if the access to care is not just different. It also needs to be, in a good world, better. That is not the standard in the law. Ms. Linda Gomez, the assistant secretary for employee benefits, is the head of the Employee Benefits Security Administration in the US Department of Labor. She was recently interviewed by Clear Health Costs on the topic:

“ The question of whether or not it’s legal really depends on whether the insurance companies are asking for similar types of information if you are having a medical or surgical procedure rather than mental health,” she said.

Is it the sameness that is the heart of the law:

“If a plan can say, ’if a person has a certain mental health condition or addiction, and then they have a medical condition that is similar within the same type of classification, and we make them jump through all of these hoops ,’ then it’s fine. But the chances are very unlikely that they are requiring the same of both. That’s where it becomes a violation of the law.

The standard can be met with either fewer barriers to mental health care or MORE barriers to general medical care. Either approach solves the “parity problem,” which essentially dictates a null hypothesis = compliant health plans.

Ms. Gomez continues to provide examples of unequal care that would violate the rule:

Unequal treatment can be about the money, or about the number of visits, she said. Some insurers say you get six visits for therapy for depression, she said, but 40 visits for therapy for your knee. Or you pay $25 in a copayment if you go to see a specialist for your heart condition, but $75 a session for your therapist, or someone to treat bipolar syndrome. “Those are the financial pieces which are also illegal,” she said.

Thus, I have a simple solution for mental health parity problems:

Just pay more for mental health care .

Then, it will be hard to argue you are paying less.

I didn’t say it would be a good solution.

I regularly give talks on mentalization-based treatment (MBT). I have a decent slide deck on the topic, so I figured that would make a decent article. My readers will be the judge. I have one book on the topic… available here.

What is this ability that allows humans to understand minds?

We need the ability—from before we can speak—to understand our caregivers. We need to let them know we are hungry or have to poop, or need to sleep. It’s not much more than that. In turn, caregivers need to comprehend the non-verbal communication from their kids. Kiddo mind radar!

Feeling understood opens the doors to trusting new information. If you feel understood, it’s probably not a scam.

Sometimes, we miss the mark. Little kids, for example, are prone to imagining that what is in their minds is also what is in the minds of others. I want a dump truck for my birthday? So…does my mom!

But…Mommy doesn’t want a dump truck, does she?

No. She does not.

However, in describing the ability to work out, as we develop sophisticated brains, the ability to work out that Mom might want something else for her birthday…we make progress. We gain the ability to work out something that might be happening other than our first impression in the minds of our moms.

This ability starts with attachment relationships—close relationships.

These relationships start early! Secure attachment develops in the context of caregiver-child relationships. Now, to my photos of Legos, so as not to violate copywrite by having taken the pictures myself.

Oh, a caption would be helpful…let’s add that!

Secure attachment develops when caregivers are “getting it” sometimes. How much of the time? Did Mom guess that I needed to be changed? No. How about that… Am I hungry? Oh, yes. Repeat.

Sometimes, things don’t go according to plan… parents are less accurate than 30% correct. After such misattunement, insecure attachment can develop. What does that look like?

Well, thank you so much for asking! It has anxious, avoidant, and disorganized presentations.

And breaking it down yet further…

This data comes from The Strange Situation experiment by Mary Ainsworth.

Some kids, when placed under stress, act like nothing happened. As a one-year-old child, the kiddo is distressed when mom leaves the room and returns. Physiologically, all children show an elevation of heart rate and other stress biology that we can measure. The securely adjusted kiddo will hug Mom and go back to playing. The anxiously attached kiddo? They hug mom and won’t go back to playing. They can’t let go and can’t quickly return to play.

The avoidantly attached kiddo? They act like it’s no big deal. They don’t even engage with Mom in this experimental paradigm. They already learned they aren’t getting what they need. From a physiological standpoint, they are still quite distressed. But visually, they look like they are avoiding her.

Disorganized attachment is unpredictable. They don’t know what to expect from their caregiver. They are on her one minute and then bouncing off the walls the next, and as they get older, this disorganized behavior doesn’t let up. This is what the office could look like afterward.

It goes poorly. These patterns begin in early life, and persist into adulthood.

The salve? It’s feeling understood. It not feeling gas-lit or lied to.

What does 2+2 equal? Four? WRONG. What is wrong with you? Except, we know it’s 5. There you go. The mistrust, when systemic, leads to bad choices.

Like going with the man in the “free candy van.” Despots and abusive individuals learn to inculcate this mistrust, as Orwell depicted in 1984, take advantage of pervasive distrust for their own ends.

We learn not to trust things that are lies. We don’t learn truth from fiction, thought. We learn social trust vs. mistrust and calibrate our sense of what’s true based on our sense of truth.

Thanks for joining me in mentalizing — part one of the visually-assisted tour!

In part one, we establish that mentalizing is the ability to understand a misunderstanding . In part two, we outlined that emotions can get too hot or too cold. Part three of this thrilling series looks at the too-hot side of the emotional coin.

In what may be the last chance to use a video of Beyonce and Destiny’s Child without wincing, I present “Say My Name” as an example of emotions running so hot our feelings can become the facts…

Here, there is the suspicion for a cheating boyfriend. People don't like being lied to. This can make their emotions get hot . Most of R&B is based on these really hot emotions, when it comes to content for popular songs. Say My Name is no exception.

In an attachment relationship, our hot emotions get even hotter. Nothing can get you more upset than your own family or loved ones. Imagining you've been betrayed by people you trusted and loved is even worse. It's just impossible to hold your mind together in these very difficult circumstances. For people with more severe problems, is it impossible to deal with this sort of situation adaptively, but they can go off the deep end. It can be impossible for some people to respond without doing something self-destructive or even violent. We have a term for this, when it leads to violence, which is “a crime of passion.”

In Say My Name, we are presented with lovers, and one does not believe the other. There's a phone call.

Any other day
I would call and you would say
Baby how's your day
But today, it ain't the same
Every other word
Is "uh-huh" yeah "okay"

These are observations, just the fact of the moment, but it's pretty clear that the narrator of the song thinks there's more going on, which will be clear in the next two lines:

Could it be that you
Are at the crib with another lady

Now the above is not represented as an actual question. The narrator feels very strongly that this is what's happening.

Are there other possible explanations for why somebody might give brief answers? Of course there are. He could've just gotten a distracting phone call from his boss. He might've gotten a text message from his mother. There are 1 million reasons other than cheating why somebody might be terse or less than communicative. We've all been distracted. However, if you're worried that your partner is cheating on you, none of those alternate explanations are gonna be as salient as feeling like you know for sure that he's a cheating bastard.

In the next lines of the song, they lay out how unacceptable this is in their mind":

If you took it there
First of all, let me say
I am not the one
To sit around, and be played
So prove yourself to me
I am the girl that you claim
Why don't you say the thangs
That you said to me yesterday

When we're really worried, our feelings become the facts. and we demand proof. This can lead to lots of adverse circumstances, like throwing a fit when somebody was really just distracted. It's also completely possible that the narrators boyfriend is actually in the middle of cheating, and what she thinks to be the case is happening.

However, we can't know, not in advance, and although demanding proof is one way of going through your day, it can blow up in your face more often than we'd like.

Let's take a look at how mentalizing therapy, as a model, thinks about emotions they're too hot. We have two non-mentalizing modes that typify this sort of emotional state, which the very British manual calls psychic equivalence and teleological mode. I have renamed those for my purposes: “feelings equal facts” and “prove-it mode.” Both of these states are your mind’s ability to consider other options, the ability to stay curious, collapsing .

To our eyes, it looks like a feeling of certainty—a lack of doubt. We can feel fed up, confused, or want to argue back. This is not because you are a terrible person; it’s because you are getting sucked into a non-curious sinkhole! How you are feeling? That is data about the “feelings = facts” going on in the mind of the person you are speaking with.

The edge predators have? They don't follow the law. They can swiftly take revenge, and people can learn not to cross them. Sean Combs is accused of firebombing Kid Cudi , who displeased him. People who want to allow legal processes to resolve their complaints? They are doomed to wait years even after they get the courage to file something . The delays in adjudication are themselves traumatizing.

Justice delayed is justice denied. It’s also more trauma- ensured . Appropriate resources for law enforcement and courts means the distance between predation and getting what’s coming to you might shrink enough for potential evil-doers to think twice. Or, better yet, victims will be allowed resolution sooner.

We’ve all had enough. The adjudication of allegations should be treated with the alacrity that the victims deserve.

The Flow Device, as described in the trial below. The big round things go on your forehead.

Our editorial point of view is that medical devices that modulate the brain will be the future. Unlike Elon Musk and his “it’s coming” drum beat— my car didn't’ drive itself to end my lease after all —the medical device scientists, despite headwinds, keep bringing new innovations to market and publishing groundbreaking papers. This week is no different. Nature Medicine—which, I'm told, is an important publication with an impact factor in 2023 is 58.7—just published a trial titled:

Home-based transcranial direct current stimulation treatment for major depressive disorder: a fully remote phase 2 randomized sham-controlled trial

I’m going to be honest: this should have spelled out an acronym, such as the following:

H ome-based E valuation of A ctive and sham D irect current stimulation for T reatment of R eally I ntolerable feels in P sychiatry

Or the HEAD-TRIP trial. That would have been punchier. Scientists, Chat-GPT exists. It’s not for writing papers but great for punchy titles. For simplicity— and my personal amusement —I’ll refer to this as the HEAD-TRIP trial throughout the rest of this article. Oh, wait. There was a snappy name. I had to go to clinicaltrials.gov to find it, but it was worth it:

their name is better than mine.

The first question is, what did the scientists do? They used transcranial direct current stimulation to stimulate the brain. Direct current is like a 9-volt battery. Current flows in one direction from anode to cathode. The researchers position this low-current stimulator on humans’ heads as follows, and the current turns on and off at some frequency:

tDCS consisted of five sessions per week for 3 weeks, then three sessions per week for 7 weeks in a 10-week trial, followed by a 10-week open-label phase. Each session lasted 30 min; 1

Some people got active, some got sham, and it wasn’t in a lab—they let people do it at home. For the Electrical Engineering enthusiasts at home, this power was in the milliamp range:

the anode was placed over the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex and the cathode over the right dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (active tDCS 2 mA and sham tDCS 0 mA, with brief ramp up and down to mimic active stimulation.

That little ramp-up was to preserve the blind—it felt like an actual stimulation was coming, but it wasn’t. I will let us all hold our breath to find out if it worked to preserve the blinding!

I know what my readers are thinking. Come on, Muir, show us table one ! This newsletter might as well be called “the table one star-tribune!” I won’t disappoint you:

There was one failure of randomization—the ethnicity was not the same between the groups—but again, by chance, we would expect some differences if we made enough comparisons. I doubt this invalidates the trial. However, regarding all other relevant details, the sham and active groups were not different. These were “depression patients”…with 4+ prior episodes on average…but as we will see, they were not the sickest of patients with depression.

They were less suicidal than average…their base rate was 0.1-0.16, and the best numbers we have previously are from Maria Oquendo’s work at Columbia:

…up to 80% of depressed individuals report suicidal thoughts, yet the lifetime risk of a nonfatal suicide attempt in major depressive disorder is estimated to be around 16%–40% 2

This isn’t particularly surprising for a depression study in which current SI is excluded. For safety, not manic. Fine. They could never have gotten TMS or ECT….which makes sense for the ability not to bias results with more refractory patients but limits generalizability to the interventionists like your author, who will want to know if this worked in people when TMS didn’t. The trial ruled out those who had ketamine or esketamine, who had ever been in the psychiatric hospital, who had co-occurring OCD, who had ever had anyone think they had bipolar disorder, PTSD, an eating disorder, agoraphobia, a “primary” anxiety disorder, or have ever smoked more than 100 cigarettes in their life….or…

And they had a current psychiatrist who could keep an eye on things. Despite being an at-home trial, it probably doesn’t generalize to Medicaid populations easily, for example. Moving on, in the very carefully selected population of depressed, but not scary, depressed patients studied, did this intervention work? In short, yes:

The active treatment group was statistically better than the sham group at the endpoint. More of them were in remission, too:

Based on the HDRS ratings, the active tDCS treatment arm was associated with a significantly greater clinical response of 58.3% compared to the sham treatment arm (37.8%; P  = 0.017)… The active treatment arm was associated with a significantly greater remission rate of 44.9% relative to the sham treatment arm (21.8%; P  = 0.004)

Similar, but not as bad as MDMA trials, functional unblinding was an issue:

Before unblinding at week 10 (end of trial), participants were asked to guess whether they thought they were receiving the active or sham tDCS … A guess of active tDCS was made by 77.6% in the active treatment arm and 59.3% in the sham treatment arm; the difference was significant ( P  = 0.01).

There were even honest to goodness adverse effects, like any active treatment:

At week 10, reports of skin redness ((active = 54 (63.5%); sham = 15 (18.5%), P  < 0.001), skin irritation ((active = 6 (6.9%); sham = 0 (0%), P  = 0.03) and trouble concentrating ((active = 12 (14.1%); sham = 3 (3.7%), P  = 0.03) were greater in the active treatment arm relative to the sham treatment arm.

The Frontier Psychiatrists is not a politically themed newsletter. That being said, I love a good math-nerd opportunity. Today is one of those days! Today’s column is about “sampling” populations.

We use statistics in biomedicine because we usually do not plan on treating or testing the entire eligible population. If we did, we wouldn’t have to wonder what the chance was that the test result was incorrect. In medicine, we have the concepts of sensitivity and specificity.

Our imagined test has a margin of error, which we call sensitivity and specificity:

• Sensitivity = TP / (TP + FN)

• Measures how often the test correctly identifies a condition when it is present.

• Specificity = TN / (TN + FP)

• Measures how often the test correctly identifies the absence of a condition.

At a large enough population size, even a very sensitive and specific test, we will have enough false positives and negatives that it won’t correctly catch every case of illness, nor will it screen out every healthy person from further workup.

The tests have the chance for error. A deeper dive into screening tests is available in this previously crafted article about screening for lightning strikes:

This is why expensive and error-prone universal screening like preneuvo is a terrible idea. The test will get it wrong enough that people will be subjected to subsequent risk from excessive and invasive workups to follow up on an incidental finding that wouldn’t have mattered.

This problem doesn’t go away with repeated screening, per se—if you are emotionally unable to cope with a bad screening test result and will follow up to “just make sure” no matter what, more screening tests won’t help—because humans like to freak out about things that are terrifying and tend to follow-up with another test “just in case.” Often, that test is an invasive biopsy with its own set of risks.

So, too, with polling in an election with important outcomes as understood, albeit differently, by all parties. We have thousands of polls being run, state by state, across many populations in this election cycle. And we know something about the sensitivity and specificity of those polls—they mostly state that they have a margin of error of 3-4%.

If the actual underlying matchup were, on election day, 50-50 exactly (with one vote as the deciding vote, for example), for the sake of argument, then as the number of polls increases, those results should vary across a normal distribution.

The sampling goes away on election day—all votes cast are then the entire population of people who chose to vote, and we don’t need samples of likely voters anymore. We need to count the votes and hope there aren’t bloody conflicts over how they are counted.

Thus, given a perfectly 50-50 matchup, you’d expect to see a range of polls that predicted not only 50-50 sentiment but also some that—by chance—predicted Candidate A up by 4% and an equal number predicting Candidate B up by 4%, and vice versa. Dall-E made me this normal distribution:

It looks like there is some error in how that is labeled. Hmmm. Gosh, errors can creep in anywhere!

In a normally distributed and accurate polling environment, by random chance, 2.5% of the polls would (based on an underlying even match up) show Candidate A would win by 8%, (2 standard deviations out in one direction) and another 2.5% would predict the victory of candidate B, and the rest would cluster around that 50-50 number, following a normal distribution. Candidate A would lead in half of the polls and candidate B in the other half. This would be modified on a state-by-state basis, where the underlying probability wasn’t 50-50. But, if pollsters were honest with us, there would be more widely different polls than we see reported.

So, why are the swing state polls not as widely varied as the math says they should be? Well, Nate Silver calls this “Herding. ” Just like scientists have been known to manipulate their data with assumptions, so to do pollsters have that opportunity; in their case, it is called a “correction for likely voters.” Pollsters get credibility by “calling it” correctly, and they have been embarrassed before by being wrong— "Dewey Defeats Truman!” Like sheep heard together for safety, so, to, do pollsters want their polls to look like other people’s polls.

An example from Silver:

By contrast, the median sample size in individual polls in these states is 800 voters. In a 49-49 race in a poll of 800 people — assuming 2 percent goes to third parties — the theoretical margin of error for the difference between Trump and Harris is ±6 points.

So, what happens is a correction factor is deployed— we asked 100 people, and 99% said they would vote for Candidate A, but they were all drunk at the time and on a trip to the top of Mount Everest, so only 2 of them are likely to get down in time to vote, and thus only two of the responses are likely voters, and one planned to vote for Candidate A and one planned to vote for Candidate B, so using our “likely voter correction model,” this is a 50-50 polling result.

This year, we have a tight race! Anyone whose poll looks like it’s a blowout for Candidate A by 8% will be laughed out of the pollster parties ever onward. However, the statistical reality is that some of the polls should have said that. They are being manipulated by a “Heading” effect, in which pollsters manipulate their “post-processing” of the raw data to present a more believable picture.

This phenomenon means that the polls for this election are almost certainly wrong…again, quoting from the ’s blog, in a piece by :

However, that doesn’t mean the actual outcome will be all that close. If the polls are totally accurate we’re in for a nail-biter on Tuesday night. But a systematic polling error is always possible , perhaps especially if you think pollsters are herding — only publishing results that match the consensus. And because things are so close, even an average polling error would upend the state of the race.

Emphasis mine. Nate points out that the overabundance of polls showing this to be a very close race is evidence that the polls are wrong—they aren’t normally distributed! This means there is a systematic bias in the results we are seeing.

The election could go either way. Get out and vote. Because the too-certain polls only underscore an important truth—the only result that matters is the one that happens when the votes are counted. And it matters, now more than ever, that every vote is cast and counted.

Please, everyone, vote. Even if my preferred Candidate doesn’t win—I’m a Candidate A supporter!—we all must know how many people peacefully participated in the electoral process. That is one way to reduce the risk of claims of voter suppression or other nonsense that could allow a candidate to falsely claim things should have been otherwise.

GO OUT AND VOTE. Your vote counts—especially when the polls are highly likely to be inaccurate, as is currently the case.

Bipolar depression can be a nightmare. Welcome to The Frontier Psychiatrists. Today, we present yet more evidence that accelerated neuromodulation with fMRI guidance appears to be an effective treatment for bipolar depression.

For general audiences, I'm going to review with bipolar depression is and why it sucks . When you hear the term depression, it can refer to more than one underlying biology. In the psychiatry, we know that “unipolar depression” —when there has never been a manic episode—response differently to treatment than bipolar depression — depression in someone who has also had mania in the past.

Virtually all the drugs we refer to as antidepressants? None of these work in bipolar depression. It's a different animal. Zoloft, Wellbutrin, Paxil, Remeron, virtually all of them. None of these medicines bring relief from the unbelievable agony of bipolar depression. For years, transcranial magnetic stimulation has not been demonstrated (unambiguously) to be helpful in bipolar depression. One of the reasons for this is that bipolar depressive episodes are generally shorter. They don't go on and on forever, typically. The timeframe is shorter, and the severity is often worse. Suicidality is extremely common in bipolar depression , and the rate of death by suicide is 0.4% per year in individuals with bipolar disorder. 1

The problem with the short timeframe of bipolar depression is that it gets better on its own. So treatments are less able to demonstrate effectiveness because some people will get better on their own, whether on a placebo or a drug because that's what the disorder acts like —when following its natural history.

If you were to do a study on modifying the rate of heads and a coin-flipping experiment, you would have to beat the 50% number. The more people are randomly likely to remit on their own, the less likely you can tell the difference between something active and a placebo.

There is a corollary to the above. The speed with which people get into remission is faster in bipolar depression, but it's not that fast. If you had a treatment that worked very rapidly, let's say in a week, you could tell the difference more robustly because the rate of people remitting from bipolar depression by chance alone in only a week’s time is going to be lower.

Traditional daily transcranial magnetic stimulation is only effective over six weeks (a little earlier in some people). But that gives people with bipolar depression plenty of time to remit by chance —whether they have active or sham stimulation— which doesn't let you tell the difference in a relatively small sample.

The faster the treatment, the easier it is to tell if it works. The other “trick” study design is to take individuals who haven't responded to treatment before. These treatment-unresponsive individuals are less likely to respond to a placebo or just time elapsing. So, the ideal study design to tell the difference between sham and active treatment was conducted in treatment-resistant individuals and also worked very quickly.

The traditional treatments for bipolar depression include medications that have tremendous side effects. Currently, psychotic medications for acute bipolar depression include:

Symbyax, a combination of fluoxetine and olanzapine ,

Seroquel

Vrylar

Latuda

Caplyta

And all have adverse effects that are not trivial. Lamotrigine can't be titrated fast enough even to be considered acute treatment. As we've covered previously, lithium is an excellent medicine for people with disorders. We even had an epic series on Lithium monitoring (thanks, )

Still, it does not address the underlying depressive symptoms in an acute manner, even though it reduces the risk of death by suicide. So, what do we do about bipolar depression? Historically, not much, the treatments are affective, and have massive weight gain and other adverse effects like Tardive Dyskinesia as side effects.

But it also had proved elusive to prove that transcranial magnetic stimulation was better than the passage of time in people with bipolar depression.

Stanford accelerated intelligent neural modulation treatment ( SAINT ) is faster, making all the difference, at least when it comes to proving the point—it’s a treatment that takes fMRI and an algorithm and uses them to guide targeted TMS treatment over just five days.

Until a study is replicated, it's just a magic trick, as far as critics like myself are concerned. However, much to my delight, as a physician who happens to have bipolar disorder , when SAINT was used at two other sites with legitimately bipolar, I-diagnosed individuals (for those who remember my criticism of the other randomized control trial which, wildly overrepresented the dubious bipolar two diagnoses I recently reviewed)?

One noticeable thing is that in the nonresponders, we see that the placebo response wears off by a month.
