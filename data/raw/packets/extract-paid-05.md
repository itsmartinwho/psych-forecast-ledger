Thinking. It's hard to do just that.

Airplanes without WiFi are my favorite think-spot. But alas, they let you watch movies.

I’m a work-a-holic. This is not to insult those with an addiction to alcohol. I suspect, given my father’s almost lifelong alcohol use disorder, I have those genes too. This is why I never had a drink of alcohol, for those wondering how one person can get so much done when they read this newsletter. Sadly, not indulging in alcohol doesn’t make working less easy.

In our modern age, sometimes we need design to solve our problems. Our desires—and the lure of technology—is strong.

Sometimes, you need time to not work. Time to think. Be a little bored, perhaps?

Walking! That is good. But it’s not always walking time. Planes are good. But hard to get on and off an airplane every time you need to think.

One conversation with my friend Nate Lacktman recently helped us both re-see a tool for thinking. It was hidden in plain sight: the classic Eames Chair.

No. Not the Aeron. It’s a great chair too. This chair:

Share The Frontier Psychiatrists

AI prompt: Eames Chair

It’s an amazing chair. But, crucially, not for working.

It’s for thinking. It's a thinking prosthesis. For people who have a hard time doing just anything. As a human who both treats and has ADHD, I will note correlates in brain functioning related to craving from Shan Sidiqqi’s work in the human connectome project:

Left: the human connectome “craving” target in the brain. Center, Right: two different areas targeedt with the Brainsway TMS coils.

The cravings in the brain for environmental stimulation are a powerful driver of behavior.

But to not work? That would be uncomfortable!

Not working—time for contemplation but not generating anything—needs to be uncomfortable. Or, rather, we need design solutions like the Eames chair to push our behavior away from working.

Nate wrote this to me after our Eames chair conversation:

Dude. You improved my relationship with my Eames chair.

Once I started treating it as a thinking chair, everything changed.

The Eames Lounge Chair is an exquisite design in an era before ergonomics was a consideration. The chair is really comfortable and canted back at just the right angle to make it really uncomfortable to try to knock out anything on your laptop. It's even all wrong to hold a book and read! The footstool that comes with the Eames chair is so beautiful and so seductive that nobody can justify not having that goddamn footstool—which also means you are going to be positioned in exactly the wrong way to hustle.

I grew up with an Eames chair in my home. Dad had one. It's almost impossible to get anything useful done in an Eames Lounge Chair. Which is my point. I propose, with attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, we crave getting things done. We want to allocate our attention. Mostly, this attending is done by fixing our eyes on something. That something is what’s in front of our eyes. The headrest of the Eames chair tilts our head ever so slightly back! It is at a slight angle, to gently pull our glance upward. Away from work!

Slightly down? This neck-straining angle is the siren song of phones and laptops. Their notifications call us. If you've decided to read a book, your arms are going to get tired. The design was seductive at the time too, and subversive in its own way:

In contrast to Modernism and its celebration of the new, “at the opposite end of the utopian spectrum was the call for a return to nature”

Modernism was a repudiation of the Calvinism inherent in the arts and crafts design movement—it’s at MoMA, trust me. However, digital technologies and ADHD brains enforce their own relentless Calvinism on our work ethic. This is toxic for our ability to contemplate. Our post-post-modern return to nature? This is a return to thinking and not being able to do anything else.

The irony, for me, is that Eames and his mass production was the hustle culture of its day:

Charles Eames wanted to design “a chair which was a product of mass production and mass production would not have anything but a positive influence on it; it would not be a substitute for handicraft.”

Ray Eames was focused on scalable comfort. If pitching as a founder today, he would say he had a passion for scalable design. My argument? He was also a Med Tech innovator.

Eames’ design is not a solution for our stimulant supply crisis, in which we keep secret medicine available to pharmacies and sufferers alike. It is also not a solution for proposed DEA regulations that imagine an endless workforce of in-person evaluators for the purpose of making their diversion control less administratively unpleasant, for their agents.

However, the Eames Lounge Chair is a way to make people desperately want a beautiful piece of furniture because they crave design.

Then Eames made that piece of furniture just wrong for getting work done—in the future! Optimize for doing nothing (quoting Jamie Ducharme in Time):

To tap into true boredom, she suggests picking an activity that requires little or no concentration — like walking a familiar route, swimming laps or even just sitting with your eyes closed — and simply letting your mind wander, without music or stimulation to guide it.

It’s also crucial to unplug during this time…Our cultural attachment to our phones, she says, is paradoxically both destroying our ability to be bored, and preventing us from ever being truly entertained.

And the subtle, seductive angles of the Eames chair make doing anything at all less physically comfortable than doing nothing but thinking. it is a medical device! A prosthesis!

A prosthesis is defined thusly:

A prosthesis is a device designed to replace a missing part of the body or to make a part of the body work better. Diseased or missing eyes, arms, hands, legs, or joints are commonly replaced by prosthetic devices.

Many missing limbs are the result of environmental insults. Our “missing boredom” and associated harms are also a modern environmental insult.

To wit:

The diverse features of mobile phones increase the cognitive demand of users to use it. Such cognitive demand causes cognitive distraction. Cognitive distraction is defined as the user's difficulty to process two or more types of information at the same time (David et al., 2015). Phone calls, texting, and social media networking sites may cause a lapse in attention and concentration.

Share

To be clear, the pervasive impact of attention-hungry devices is a problem we created for ourselves. I am writing this on the phone right now. It's not like I'm kicking the habit. I'm also a realist. The best predictor of our behavior is what our brains tell us to do. This article, like this chair, is an argument for the profundity of design as we make choices to push back toward a healthier regulation of attention. If it's too easy to look at your phone, make it a little bit harder. If you work too much, sit down in a chair that is just profoundly bad at being a chair in which we will get things done. We don't have to go over the top and invent something. We have to think about our environment. We made park benches that couldn't be slept on, so people don't sleep on them. We need to make design choices so that our choices are healthier.

The Eames chair is expensive. I can’t afford one. I want an Eames-friendly life badly. But it’s an expensive chair, at around $6,000. It’s actually on track for ADHD therapeutics, like the triphasic amphetamine sale brand named Mydayis:

How much does Mydayis cost without insurance? People without insurance should expect to pay $449 for 30, 50 mg capsules of Mydayis—about $15 per capsule. This prescription provides enough doses for one month of daily dosing, so a year of Mydayis at full retail price will cost about $5,400.

So someone should start an ADHD durable medical equipment (DME) vendor for these so at the very least I can get the chair pre-tax as part of an ERISA-regulated plan.

From more than a generation before us, the Eames chair cries out to our behavior: make different design choices, and you can live a different life!

I’ve been getting this newsletter out daily. When you start writing, and people start reading it, they start asking you about your process. I have talked to a lot of my friends, colleagues, and strangers about writing. Like my pal, .

I write a tremendous amount. It's more than most people. I am aware of that.

I’ve got nothing. And a headache!

The turning point, for me, was realizing that regularity in writing is what builds the skill set. Nobody has great things to say all the time.

People spend tremendous time waiting for a good idea. Good ideas are rare. I’ve had a few of them, and only maybe. Execution of ideas? This is built with practice— practice with having nothing to say, essentially.

Today is a day where I write something, for the purpose of keeping in the habit of writing something. It's a little bit like running a code, trying to resuscitate someone with CPR, a task which is usually pointless:

To quote Samuels Shem, the pseudonym used by the author of the House of God:

Key concept," said the Fat Man, "to think that you're doing a shitty job. If you resign yourself to doing a shitty job, you go ahead and get the job done, and since we're all in the ninety-ninth percentile of interns, at one of the best internships in the world, what you do turns out to be a terrific job, a superlative job.

I had the great pleasure of having Samuel Bergman, the real name of Samuel Shem, tell me the following:

“The work you are doing is disruptive and threatening to the status quo of the House of Medicine. Be careful!”

Today, by saying nothing, and having nothing to say, I'm keeping my edge! I am ready to say something true. If something true comes along…I will be ready.

I encourage all of you to consider the degree to which this might be helpful for your mission. To those who are about to write, I salute you!

I wrote an article about before the bankruptcy about their Somyrst product, and now, no one can accuse me of being a shill for a bankrupt company. So with a little bit of editing for the current context, here is what I wrote:

Eulogy For Somyrst

Suicide is complicated. People kill themselves for all sorts of reasons. And mostly, we don’t get to know what those reasons were, because only a fraction of those who complete suicide leave us an explanation.

Suicide risk factors, on the other hand, are actually pretty straightforward.

Risk factors are things we can know about in advance of a rare event like completed suicide. We divide the conceptual earth into:

static risk factors that don't change

And:

dynamic risk factors that do change

Dynamic risk factors are either modifiable, because we can do something to change them, or not modifiable, because we can't.

When I was learning how to be a psychiatrist, at the end of every note when I was going to discharge someone from the hospital or from the emergency room, I was supposed to write a risk assessment. This assessment is about the risk of the person killing themself, and what things that less likely in your mind and support your decision to discharge. This process documented means you're doing the appropriate thing by discharging them from the hospital after considering all the risks. This is a kabuki dance, if I'm being honest with myself. Humans are terrible at predicting completed suicide based on these factors. We are bad a prediction generally, but particularly the timeframe in which the rare event could happen.

I'll provide some examples to get concrete… risk factors include (the two bolded items correspond to two Pear Digital Theraputics products, Somyrst and ReSet)

• Previous suicide attempts or self-harm behavior

• Family history of suicide

• Global Insomnia

• Personal or family history of mental health disorders, particularly mood disorders, schizophrenia, and substance abuse disorders

• Male gender (males are more likely to complete suicide than females, although females attempt suicide more frequently)

• Advanced age (suicide rates increase with age)

• Chronic medical conditions, particularly those associated with chronic pain or disability

• History of trauma or abuse, including childhood abuse or neglect

• Social isolation or lack of social support

• Recent loss or bereavement, particularly if it was a loss to suicide

• Financial stress or legal problems

• Access to lethal means (e.g., firearms, medications)

• Cultural or religious beliefs that stigmatize mental health treatment or suicide

• Geographic location or social environment, particularly if it is associated with poverty, violence, or substance abuse.

• Acute psychological distress (e.g., anxiety, depression, hopelessness)

• Current suicidal ideation, intent, or plan

• Recent psychiatric hospitalization or discharge

• Substance abuse or dependence, particularly if combined with other risk factors

• Access to lethal means, particularly if combined with other risk factors

• Poor treatment adherence or engagement in mental health care

• Loss of social support or relationships

• Major life events or changes, such as divorce or job loss

• Agitation, impulsivity, or aggression

• Increased risk-taking behavior or impulsivity

• Perceived burdensomeness and/or thwarted belongingness, which are associated with interpersonal theories of suicide

• Demographic or cultural factors, such as sexuality, gender identity, or ethnicity, which can impact levels of stigma and discrimination

• Chronic or terminal medical conditions, which can contribute to feelings of hopelessness and isolation

And when I was in training, I would look up lists like this every time, and then document a rather detailed assessment for each individual patient about this. I don't know that I changed anyone's life by doing so. I do know that I thought a lot about these risk factors in the process of learning to write these assessments.

Some of these are kind of pointless, in that you can't really do much about it. I am not going to change, in the psychiatric emergency room, one’s level of social support or relationships. But I can ask about them. And worry a lot. I can't change your divorce. I did learn to think more rigorously about what risk looks like.

One of the most important things that I can change— as a suicide prevention professional— is how much sleep you're getting.

In fact, if inpatient Psychiatry does anything useful for modifying suicide risk—which I think is still up for a significant debate over any longer time scale—the thing that we do the most good with is the ability to help you get some sleep.

One of the best predictors of completed suicide is global insomnia. This is true for veterans, and it’s true for everyone. Not sleeping is bad for your brain. And it’s bad for your risk of dying by suicide.

Sleep is complicated. Not sleeping is, similarly, complicated. But one of the things that’s not too complicated is the fact that people have behaviors that get in the way of actually sleeping.

I will prove it to you.

• How easy is it to fall asleep quickly by just laying down at noon?

• How about if someone was jumping up and down next to you on the bed screaming, “If you fall asleep, I’m not gonna give you 1 million dollars!!!!”

I’m guessing, absent narcolepsy, that the later situation would be a pretty difficult one for anyone to learn to fall asleep in easily. Sleep is a learned behavior.

When we say learned behavior, we mean sleep hygiene: the behaviors a person has around sleep that make it more or less likely that sleep will occur.

There is a method for taking good sleep hygiene behaviors and reinforcing them in a way that works reliably. This was originally developed as an approach called cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia. This approach works. It’s also rather clunky to have to get a highly trained therapist just to be able to sleep at night.

Ideally, there would be a technology that would take whatever the cognitive behavioral therapist was doing, and deploy it for all of us. Easily. Right now. Right here.

The best place for this technology would be on your phone. Because that’s where everything is these days. Your phone is where a lot of things not useful for sleeping are also. Your phone is already a one stop shop for not sleeping, but it could be a place where you could find things to help you sleep too.

It turns out this has already been done. Pear Therapeutics took a cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia, which has been shown to have great evidence in randomized controlled trials, and then they made a computer- and phone-based version of that treatment. They took that device, which we call a prescription digital therapeutic, and they subjected it to the same FDA approval process that every medical device goes through. And it got FDA approval! This application is called Somyrst.

If you want the best evidence-based treatment for insomnia, with very little risk, that works better than sleeping pills, it’s this.

If you don’t have insomnia, you’re less likely to kill yourself. It seems simple, but it’s a proven way to treat a condition that modifies risk of suicide.

This scalable suicide prevention device and the heroic regulatory efforts to make sure it was done in a safe and legitimate way is not something that “succeeded in the healthcare market.” Accepting the Matrix is a losing game.

It's a bummer. It should be recognized as the achievement it was. It might even be a call to action for change in healthcare payment models that play a relentless game of keep away from human health?

Jedi-Level Personalized Dosing of Lamotrigine (Lamictal) in Bipolar Disorder
A practical guide to a commonly misunderstood drug.
OWEN SCOTT MUIR, M.D.
APR 17, 2023
∙ PAID

9
1

As a child and adult psychiatrist with some expertise in bipolar disorder, not to mention being a person who also has bipolar disorder, people regularly ask me questions about the dosing medications for this condition.

I did kind of asked for it, in that I wrote a whole piece about Lithium dosing. So I decided to pull back the curtain on the next “super specialized” psychopharmacology trick in my bipolar disorder management strategy, which is a clever work around figuring out what dosage of Lamictal (Lamotrigine) might be helpful to which person.

Lamotrigine was developed initially as a treatment for epilepsy. As we now understand, from the work of physician-scientists like Chris Palmer M.D., both epilepsy and bipolar disorder can be understood as metabolic disorders.

“Not only do mental disorders have strong bidirectional relationships with one another, many metabolic and neurological disorders also have strong bidirectional relationships with mental disorders. These relationships provide important clues about the nature of the common pathway that will help us solve the puzzle of mental illness.”1

Lamotrigine is primarily understood to function thusly:

Lamotrigine is a member of the sodium channel blocking class of antiepileptic drugs.[68] This may suppress the release of glutamate and aspartate, two dominant excitatory neurotransmitters in the central nervous system.[3] It is generally accepted to be a member of the sodium channel blocking class of antiepileptic drugs,[69] but it could have additional actions, since it has a broader spectrum of action than other sodium channel antiepileptic drugs such as phenytoin and is effective in the treatment of the depressed phase of bipolar disorder, whereas other sodium channel-blocking antiepileptic drugs are not, possibly on account of its sigma receptor activity.

The sigma receptor regulates gene translation into proteins in the rough endoplasmic reticulum of the cell, and thus impacts myriad changes beyond just “cells firing less” by sodium channel blockade.

Lamotrigine is understood as a “mood stabilizer.” Here is data from a study published in JAMA on its use compared to lithium and placebo:

Most of the Lamotrigine reduction in mood episodes was driven, in that sample, by less relapse to depression. The FDA approval reads:

It may also be used as maintenance treatment in patients with bipolar disorder to help delay the occurrence of mood episodes such as depression, mania, or hypomania.

Now, the dosing super tricks you have all been waiting for…

Lamotrigine is often under-dosed in patients with bipolar disorder. One can check a blood level, as one does in patients with epilepsy.

The trough levels—as measurable 12 hours after a PM dose in epilepsy—are defined therapeutically as between 5 and 15 micrograms/mL.

Lamotrigine is processed through glucuronidation, generally thought of a second pass metabolism. This is regulated through UGT1A1 genes and the proteins they code for.

Share The Frontier Psychiatrists

Just as humans have rapid metabolizer variations in the liver’s cytochrome P450 system, we additionally have UGT1A1 Variants types with “gain of function” mutations. In non science speak, some people’s bodies burn through Lamotrigine quickly.

The way of testing this is a little bit clunky, but it's pretty reliable— and cheap! One can draw a peak lamotrigine blood level! To define this, the patient is directed take their dose in the morning. One hour—and no more—later they get a lamotrigine blood level drawn.

Remember, these blood levels are normed for trough levels. So if what you find is that, when drawn, the patient has a five or lower level—only one hour after ingesting a dose, not 12 hours after the dose—what we know is that such a patient is processing this faster than most people with epilepsy process the drug. This epilepsy cohort is most of the people who’ve taken the drug and gotten blood levels drawn, so that the group that recommended dosing strategies are based on.

If the result shows a very low peak level, it stands to reason that the patient is a rapid metabolizer. In those cases, a higher dose is eminently reasonable, because her body is clearing lamotrigine rapidly.

These levels are not a guide to “clinical blood levels” in bipolar disorder, as those have not been established.

The risk of Stevens-Johnson syndrome is mostly based on other genetic variants, largely in the HLA genes. Stephens-Johnson Syndrome, the life-threatening rash that every doctor learns to worry about, is a very rare event.

So that is my strategy! Instead of just going with 200 mg because somebody decided that was the right dose, get a measurement. Since we don't have to find levels in bipolar disorder, the measurement we get is to determine if a person is metabolizing the drug at a much more rapid than expected rate. The way we know this is by using the establish trough levels in epilepsy, 5-15, and add a little bit of logic: if somebody has levels lower than most people would have after 12 hours after only one hour, they can safely tolerate more medicine!

That's my personalized approach to dosing Lamotrigine, and I hope it's helpful to physicians, nurse practitioners, physicians assistance, and patients alike! A gracious acknowledgment to Dr. Howard Green, a mentor who taught me this approach.
