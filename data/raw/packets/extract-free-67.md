So, the authors of this well-thought-out study:

A Danger to Self and Others: Health and Criminal Consequences of Involuntary Hospitalization 2

They reasoned that in Allegheny County, PA, when a patient who might be an “edge case” for admission—where two physicians might disagree about hospitalization—enters the ER, we can, with careful checking of assumptions, determine that the doctor you see is random enough. You don’t pick who is working when you have a crisis. Physicians in their sample behave in reliable ways—some admit patients more readily, while others do so less so. Thus, there is a pseudo-randomized risk —the doctor you are assigned to varies in the rate at which they admit you to the hospital. They then followed the outcomes, which statistically could have “gone either way.”

Involuntary admission leads to—and this is a causal inference in a quasi-experimental design— a higher risk of death by suicide or overdose:

Our results find that hospitalization increases death by suicide or overdose in the three months after an evaluation. A 10 percentage point increase in a physician’s tendency to hospitalize yields an increase in the probability of death in the next three months of 0.98 percentage points.

And more likely to be charged for (presumably committing) a violent crime:

For being charged with a violent crime, our reduced form estimate finds that a 10 percentage point increase in a physician’s tendency to hospitalize is associated with a 2.6 percentage point increase in being charged with a violent crime within the following 3 months.

Which, I imagine, is cold comfort to the people who were the victims of the excess violence committed by individuals who were hospitalized— just to be safe —by physicians.

For mathy reasons, I won't get into, the authors felt confident enough that they could identify 43% of patients as “judgment call” cases, and their conclusion applies only to that population.

However, I will ask a question that is not answered, by design, in this study. The rationale to restrict the analysis to the percentage who could “go either way” is of statistical utility. This allowed them to use this cohort to evaluate the outcomes. In cases when 100% of physicians would “just admit the human”—people found screaming “I’m gonna do it” with a gun, for example—no reasonable physician is going to discharge that person from a hospital setting. The randomness played out only on “judgment calls.”

However, since the study says nothing about the other admissions, I will point to yet more data. I will then ask an existential question. Is it possible that involuntary hospitalization is, on average, harmful writ large?

More Data On Psychiatric Hospitalization That is Not Good

If we start from a position of equipoise with any probability, unburdened by prior strong feelings about what must be true, we could begin with 50/50 odds. The actual chance that any intervention is helpful lies somewhere under that distribution, and we are looking to discover the true probability of an event being helpful or harmful. This is, of course, different from the question “to what degree” the outcome will be helpful or harmful.

There is a branch of math, pioneered by the remarkable Judea Pearl, Ph.D., that allows us to use statistics to determine causality. His book, Causal Inference in Statistics, requires a high school level of algebra and an intro class in statistics to get through, but it is worth it. His popular press book “ The Book of Why ” is good enough for general audiences (Amazon affiliate links). I wrote a whole gaggle of articles for general audiences on the topic of statistics previously:

and another…

and others like this one:

It’s a favorite topic. Lay populations love to sound smart when they remind others that correlation does not equal causation . While true, it doesn’t mean that causation doesn’t exist. I’ll illustrate.

And I’ll borrow an explanation from an excellent article by Urja Pawar :

Let’s say my chances of going out depend only on whether it’s sunny and we have a dataset that captures the event of me going out, sunny/not sunny, and temperature value. As per Bayesian inference, the probability of me going out will depend on the prior probability of the sun shining … Whereas the frequentist inference will predict the chances of me going out based on the number of days I went out with features being sunny and temperature. 3

However, an absurd conclusion could also be drawn from the data—since I went out and the temperature was pleasant, it caused the sun to shine . Or, the more common version, “we can’t prove that we didn't cause the sun to shine with my morning walk, it’s highly correlated, but correlation doesn’t prove causation.”

Spurious conclusions—both positive and negative—can be drawn if we leave our good sense at home.

Some actions cause other actions. Furthermore, our confidence that a terrible thing is likely to happen isn’t established solely by randomized trials. That would be both dumb and unethical. We couldn’t randomize people to smoking vs. not smoking to determine the likelihood of cancer. Similarly, there are ethical limits to determining how risky hospitalization is. That being said, these constraints do not preclude us from using math and good sense to determine if there are certain activities we should avoid.

Now, back to the abysmal data on psychiatric hospitalization as it currently stands…Let’s start with a straightforward bit of logic: If psychiatric hospitalization was helpful, positive outcomes should show up in the dat a. If it’s harmful, the data on outcomes should be worse. Neither of these has to be causal, just yet. Still, no one is going to argue that skydiving without a parachute is helpful; it’s just that all the people who have attempted it thus far are sick and just happened to die when they hit the ground because they were already so sick, and this is just what happens when a sick person touches the ground. “Exploding ground touch” syndrome is prevalent, I hear.

From “Scope of Inference in Statistics”

Therefore, in all the correlations we can examine, if hospitalization is harmful, the data should demonstrate that people fare worse with the intervention. If it’s helpful, at least some portion should do well. Similarly, a random sample of people who fell out of planes will demonstrate people doing poorly, absent a history of parachute deployment, and going about their day while explaining "it’s no big deal” to other attractive people at the bar later in the evening, as they eagerly ask, “Weren’t you scared ?”

The largest-scale studies we have demonstrate that outcomes are worse for those hospitalized when it comes to suicide risk. Thanks, Denmark, as published in the Journal JAMA Psychiatry:

The design was a nested case-control study:

This BIG study looked at all of the people in the country who died by completed suicide between 1981 and 1997, and compared them to control subjects, who didn’t die by suicide:

Participants All 13 681 male and 7488 female suicides committed in Denmark from January 1, 1981, to December 31, 1997, and 423 128 population control subjects matched for sex, age, and calendar time of suicide.

The authors found that suicide rates go up with psychiatric hospitalizations:

Conclusions Suicide risk peaks in periods immediately after admission and discharge. The risk is particularly high in persons with affective disorders and in persons with short hospital treatment. 4

Now, the first response from defensive psychiatrists will be “but sicker people are hospitalized, of course it’s not causal!” However, if hospitalization were helpful, the numbers for bad outcomes would go down , not up .

The most robust argument we can make, on behalf of hospitalization now, is that the helpfulness is mild and its effects are hidden! You’d be forced to argue that those worse outcomes in this high-risk population would have occurred if they had not been subject to the tender ministrations of those helpful inpatient psychiatric hospitals.

OR, conversely, hospitalization might be harmful . That, despite how defensive we can be, might be the case. Let’s address any objections you might have regarding the data, drawing on all the other studies I could find that address this issue:

1. The Units are Locked, Right? For Safety?

We are going to start simple: do locks on inpatient psychiatric units demonstrably prevent patients from leaving and thus prevent harm for “dangerous” people? Spoiler Alert: No .

Here is the data: As far back as 1960, one hospital undertook a mirror image study design—first locking, and then unlocking, its doors:

One paper focussed on aggression with regard to locked doors. Folkard (1960 ) monitored the incidents of aggressive behaviour for 20 consecutive weeks: the first 10 weeks the door was kept locked, and the last 10 weeks the door was opened for the first time.

During the locked phase there were 249 aggressive occurrences and during the unlocked phase only 193. This reduction in aggressive incidents coincided with the decrease in actions taken by the staff (from 57 during the locked phase to 46 during the unlocked phase). Staff actions included sedation, E.C.T. or putting the patient to bed. Folkard (1960 ) noted that staffing levels might also influence aggression.

It makes “common sense” to think that the locks on hospital doors do…something. However, we have very little evidence to suggest it’s useful. The above study indicates that quasi-random assignment to a locked or unlocked time period could tease this out. We have ample evidence to suggest unlocked doors are beneficial, even if it’s very dated:

The literature on locked doors in inpatient psychiatric wards was inconclusive regarding the effects of locked doors. While there were ample empirical ( Scott 1956 , Wisebord et al. 1958 , Wake 1961 ) and non-empirical literature ( Koltes 1956 , Ryan 1956 , Snow 1958 ) from the 1950s and 1960s regarding the open door, there was very little on locked doors.

One would imagine locked doors prevent people from running away. In the UK, they refer to this as “absconding” and in the US, we tend towards the term “elopement.” However, we don’t have that kind of evidence. Psychiatric patients are resourceful—they can and do run away from locked units:

in a large retrospective study of absconding at All Saints Hospital in Birmingham, just under half of all absconds [ed: that is british for running away ] occurred from locked wards ( Antebi 1967 ). Somewhat similarly, Coleman (1966 ) reports 20% of absconds, and Richmond et al. (1991 ) 21%, at Veterans Hospitals in the USA were from locked wards. 1

The locks don’t work. This has been replicated in New Zealand and other studies in the UK, too: The acute psychiatric inpatient service in Christchurch, New Zealand, changed from two locked and two unlocked wards to four open wards. A paper was published on the outcomes:

Rates of unauthorised absences increased by 58% after the change in ward configuration ( P = 0.005), but seclusion hours dropped by 53% ( P = 0.001). 2

Other studies have found no difference in the UK:

one-way analysis of variance between the permanently locked wards ( n = 41), partially open wards ( n = 49) and permanently open wards ( n = 46) did not show significant differences in absconding rates between the three ward types [ F (2,133) = 2.8, P = 0.067]. 3

Ok, sure, Muir , they need better locks. OR, the locks on the doors are for the nerves of the psychiatrists.

What about other populations like veterans? I have heard they are in crisis also….

2. Inpatient Hospitals Correlated to 5-8x Higher Suicide Risk in Veterans in the US.

It’s not safer or more effective in veteran populations, as published in 2013 in the American Journal of Psychiatry:

A total of 153 suicides occurred among the 68,947 service members. The overall suicide rate in the cohort was 71.6 per 100,000 person-years, compared with the rate of 14.2 per 100,000 person-years in the general active-duty U.S. military population. Personnel released from a psychiatric hospitalization were therefore five times more likely to die from suicide. The risk of dying from suicide within the first 30 days after a psychiatric hospitalization was 8.2 times higher than the risk at more than one year after hospitalization. 5

Correlation, not causation! However, it’s not easy to argue that hospitalization is helpful. The data says higher risk. The debate is over whether the harm is causal or correlated. It’s not a debate about whether hospitalization is helpful .

Again, we are forced to ask the same question—either these are sick people , and despite the higher rates of death by suicide, it’s because we weren’t able to help quite as much as we’d like, but it’s still good we tried.

OR, it’s possible that psychiatric hospitals, replete with features to reduce liability for doctors, are anti-therapeutic and harmful to patients.

3. What About Comparing a Million People with and Without Mental Illness?

Taking a look at some more modern studies, we have a meta-analysis published in 2016 specifically related to suicide risk , again in the journal JAMA Psychiatry :

The cohort included 770 643 adults in the mental disorder cohort, 1 090 551 adults in the nonmental disorder cohort, and 370 deaths from suicide from January 1, 2001, to December 31, 2007. Data were analyzed from March 5, 2015, to June 6, 2016.

This is a lot of people. It’s not better:

In the overall population of 1 861 194 adults (27% men; 73% women; mean [SD] age, 35.4 [13.1] years), suicide rates for the cohorts with depressive disorder ( 235.1 per 100 000 person-years), bipolar disorder ( 216.0 per 100 000 person-years), schizophrenia ( 168.3 per 100 000 person-years), substance use disorder ( 116.5 per 100 000 person-years), and other mental disorders ( 160.4 per 100 000 person-years) were substantially higher than corresponding rates for the cohort with nonmental disorders (11.6 per 100 000 person-years) or the US general population (14.2 per 100 000 person-years).

To do just a touch of math, you are more likely to die by suicide after a psychiatric hospitalization than the non-mental disorder cohort by a factor of 2,100%.

And again, these are only looking at the 90 days after discharge suicide rates, comparing the general population to people who had been discharged from the psychiatric hospital. A total of 370 people died by suicide, and there were 90-day intervals with those populations above. Either… It’s a higher-risk population, and it helped, but despite all our efforts, it wasn't enough to move the needle towards life .

OR it was harmful, and increased suicide risk.

4. Is This Also a Problem in…Brazil?

It’s not just about the US health care system either; Brazilian hospitals have higher completed suicide rates after discharge, too:

This sample comprised 1,228,784 adult patients admitted to psychiatric hospitals. Of these, 3201 died by suicide within 365 days of discharge ….The highest risk of suicide was among patients with depressive disorders ([ adjusted Hazard Ratio], 3.87 ; 95%CI, 3.41–4.38) follow by opioid-related disorders (aHR, 2.71; 95%CI, 2.00–3.67), particularly among female patients. 6

So yes, yes it is, even in Brazil. Either it’s an adjusted hazard ratio of 3.87 in depression upwards, because even in Brazil, it’s impossible to do much to move the needle in these depressed people.

OR, it’s harmful and increases the risk.

Resolvers [ the people who acted like they were responding robustly to treatment in the hospital ] were the most likely to die by suicide post-discharge relative to all other suicide ideation groups. Resolvers also demonstrated significant improvement in all clinical outcomes from admission to discharge. 7

For the express purpose of fooling the rubes who believe the hospital helps, so they could leave the unit and kill themselves in the privacy of the post-discharge period, when suicide risk goes up.

6. Things Must Be Better In California After Cases of Self-Harm?

Nope:

We used 2009–2012 statewide data on 57,312 self-harm ED patients from California, linked to mortality records.

And found:

In 12-month models accounting for observed covariates via GRF [ generalized random forest ] methods, hospitalized males had 5.4 more suicides per 1,000 patients (95% CI: 3.0, 7.8), hospitalized patients aged 10–29 had 2.4 (1.1, 3.6) more suicides per 1,000 , and those aged ≥50 had 5.8 (0.5,11.2) more suicides per 1,000 , compared to corresponding discharged patients. Hospitalization was not significantly associated with suicide among females nor among patients aged 30–49 in GRF analyses. 8

7. What About The Risk Many Years Later?

Suicide rates are still higher years later.

The pooled estimate postdischarge suicide rate was 484 suicides per 100 000 person-years (95% CI, 422-555 suicides per 100 000 person-years; prediction interval, 89-2641),… The suicide rate was highest within 3 months after discharge (1132; 95% CI, 874-1467) and among patients admitted with suicidal ideas or behaviors (2078; 95% CI, 1512-2856). Pooled suicide rates per 100 000 patients-years were 654 for studies with follow-up periods of 3 months to 1 year, 494 for studies with follow-up periods of 1 to 5 years, 366 for studies with follow-up periods of 5 to 10 years, and 277 for studies with follow-up periods longer than 10 years . Suicide rates were higher among samples collected in the periods 1995-2004 (656; 95% CI, 518-831) and 2005-2016 (672; 95% CI, 428-1055) than in earlier samples. 9

At the end of that, you will note it’s getting worse over time.

8. I Found One Study Demonstrating A Benefit! In A Cohort of Veterans…

One study used machine learning to analyze just under 200,000 veterans with a suicide attempt. They found one group in which there was a benefit— those who had attempted suicide in the preceding 24 hour s, only .

Of 196 610 visits (90.3% men; median [IQR] age, 53 [41-59] years), 71.5% resulted in hospitalization. …For patients with SA in the past day, hospitalization was associated with risk reductions ranging from -6.9% to -9.6% across diagnoses…. Accounting for heterogeneity, hospitalization was associated with reduced risk of subsequent SAs in 28.1% of the patients and increased risk in 24.0%…. 10

9. Involuntary Admission and Involuntary Treatment Are Not Identical

This is a bit of a nit-picking point, but the legal bar to admit someone to a hospital is lower than the bar to administer treatment (like an oral medicine) against someone’s will. Thus, admitting someone doesn’t mean you can force them to swallow a pill. Practically, what ends up happening is the physician has to petition the court to allow “treatment over objection.” If the judge finds it appropriate, injectable medicines are often used. Or, practically, the patient chooses to take medicine with a lot of coercion (“if you don’t we will go to court to get an order for an injection”) but still with some choice on their part. Convincing is often easier than forcing , and more productive, even when it takes building some rapport and time to do so.

This is Exhausting!

I agree. But either imagine being admitted to a psychiatric hospital or remember when you were. The authors of our lead study explain some of the ways risk may be caused :

The process of involuntarily hospitalizing someone is an arduous and invasive one. Police officers execute the warrant and arrive at an individual’s location to bring them to a hospital. The commitment lasts for days, during which medication can be administered without consent. If the patient would rather not be hospitalized, the process can alienate the individual from their support networks, including family and existing treatment providers. If the patient was using substances such as opioids prior to hospitalization, a period of abstinence could lower tolerance and increase risk of overdose from renewed substance use upon release. And if the patient wasemployed, a sudden unexcused absence might jeopardize that employment and the stability that comes with earning a living.

Hospitalization has risks, and in the best data to date, in “edge cases,” causally, increased suicide and violence happen after these admissions.

To be clear, I don’t think it’s impossible that a hospital stay could be structured to help. However, when the standard of care is “give people oral medicines that don’t work,” and do so for a time span that is scientifically close to impossible in which they could be expected to take effect, it is hard to imagine any mechanism by which current inpatient treatment could be helpful.

The average length of stay is 5-7 days. 11

Virtually all oral antidepessants take week(s) plural to work. 12

Only one, Axome’s Auvelity, has a faster onset, on the order of a week.

We have faster-acting treatments, of course. We have IV Ketamine, Spravato, Accelerated TMS, SAINT TMS, and soon psychedelic treatments. However, almost none of these are commonly available in the hospital setting, almost entirely due to a busted payment model, which would force already money-losing units to lose even more money in their current “per-diem” contract structure. If they treated patients more quickly, they would make less money per patient, since they are paid per day, in 75% of settings..

When Dr. Aftab raised the point about whether, in edge cases, we should default to discharge…he was right. I ask, further, if we should either change what is happening in those inpatient units, such that it could be expected to work—for example, utilizing treatments like SAINT or other Accelerated TMS protocols, IV Ketamine, Spravato, or rapid-acting psychedelics, if approved by the FDA. We could also consider removing the locks from the doors and offering voluntary admission, as is the case with the rest of the hospital.

Now, I Go Off The Deep End and Call Two Mathematicians to Check My Math.

Feel free to skip this point if math makes your eyes glaze over; however, to stick the landing on the math point, I’ll start with a chart I had ChatGPT make and then checked with actual scientists like , to make sure I was understanding this correctly.

This chart illustrates Judea Pearl's causal inference framework applied to psychiatric hospitalization. We know candidates for inpatient care in psychiatric illness are more high risk, so it’s not just the hospitalization that increases risk in a population, it’s additional risk on top of the burden of illness.

The crux of this approach is to calculate the difference between these two imagined worlds, with the Average Treatment Effect (ATE) :

• World A : Everyone in this “edge case” group is hospitalized .

• World B : That same group is not hospitalized (for example, if we ban involuntary hospitalization).

We calculate the difference in outcomes between World A and World B. We define variables…

• Y: a bad outcome (suicide or overdose death)

• X=1: hospitalized

• X=0: not hospitalized

And we create an equation (you will notice the “do” operator from Pearl!)…

ATE = P (Y ∣ do (X=1))− P(Y ∣ do(X=0))

The Emmanuel paper gives us mathematical values for those probabilities:

• P (Y∣do(X=1))= 0.021 → 2.1% died after hospitalization

• P (Y∣do(X=0))= 0.011 → 1.1% died without hospitalization

Now we are down to simple subtraction: ATE = 0.021−0.011 = 0.010

That means:

Being hospitalized causes a 1.0% increase in risk of death within 3 months.

So, for every 100 people who are hospitalized instead of not hospitalized, one extra person dies as a result of that decision, in this “edge case” population. But the badness and the math don’t end there:

Recall, Emanuel et. al. noted that as the propensity to hospitalize goes up, so, too, does the risk, which is precisely what is being proposed by the administration:

If the Propensity to hospitalize increases by 10%, then the harm increases by 0.22 percentage points (i.e., risk of death goes from 1.1% to about 1.32%)

So, if you go to a doctor with:

• 40% hospitalization rate → 1.1% suicide/OD death rate

• 50% hospitalization rate → 1.32%

• 60% hospitalization rate → 1.54%

• This is a linear relationship

The slope is:

Δ P (Y) = 0.0022 per 0.10 change in hospitalization propensity.

That means the full model of risk is something like:

P(Y)= β0+ 0.022⋅Propensity

And, of course, this means that hospitalizing more people against their will—by legal fiat—is a terrible idea, as it will lead to more deaths:

At a 100% hospitalization rate, the risk increases to 3.3% for the three months after discharge. Although it’s based on a slope with some adjustment built into its calculation, it ought still to control for baseline risk. Thus, the total Adjusted Average Administration Harm ( AAAH , a thing I just made up) would be about 2.3% over the whole range of increased propensity, when controlling for the baseline risk.

What Does All This Mean?

What we are offering, with involuntary hospitalization, isn’t helping. The way it’s offered isn’t helpful—it’s causally harmful in a definable cohort. It may be harmful in more cases, not just the edge cases. The call to use more involuntary care is dangerous. We need to change what hospitals do. We need to update insurance coverage policies to take this reality into account. When what we are doing, as health professionals, is harmful, we have a duty to change our practice. Patients depend on us. This practice shift will be challenging for all the reasons Awais Aftab highlights—liability is a concern, the optics of that risk are a concern, and more. However, none of those is a good excuse to continue using a harmful intervention because we worry about what people will think if we don’t.

Of note, insurance plans generally bemoan hospital costs, because their claims data have long demonstrated how unhelpful this care is likely to be. Now, they have data to back this up. We need to care for people who desperately need it, of course. The forum in which we do so? Emanuel et. al. highlight that this decision is life-or-death important. Let’s open our outpatient doors, take the locks off psychiatric units, and take on, if we are health professionals, the emotional risk of following the evidence to keep patients safe. We have done this before—we no longer perform lobotomy, for example. That won a Nobel Prize. We pulled Vioxx from the market . We have course-corrected in the history of medicine.

It’s time to do the same with involuntary psychiatric hospitals.

I am a child and adult psychiatrist. Except in cases of adoption, children are related to their parents. Parenting is a stressful, anxiety-provoking business. Rare is the parent who wants to see their kiddo afraid or suffering—and if this is happening, there are much bigger problems. Anxiety, on the part of the child, can feel like a big problem.

However, in our world of helicopter parents and “trigger warnings,” it’s easy to forget that coping with anxiety is a learned behavior. Feeling—and acting— anxious is also a learned behavior, often with the lesson unintended.

Anxiety is often paired with avoidance. Anxious kids don’t want to feel that way. Thus, avoiding the anxiety-provoking experience is a high priority. Kids, however, don’t get to say what they do with their day. Their parents are, at a minimum, their ride to the birthday party.

Welcome to Family Accommodation (FA):

Family accommodation (FA), which refers to behavioral changes that family members make to temporarily alleviate a child’s anxiety, has been linked to child anxiety symptom severity. Although emotional vulnerabilities for anxiety such as emotion dysregulation, anxiety sensitivity, and distress intolerance have been associated with family accommodation, research… is limited. 1

One of those specific anxiety disorders is OCD, and in that illness, Family Accommodation makes symptoms worse , with a modest effect size:

Forty-one studies were included in the present meta-analysis, and the overall effect size (ES) for the correlation between family accommodation and OCD symptom severity was moderate ( r = .42). 2

When your kid is melting down, it makes all the sense in the world, as a parent, to try to soothe them, and remove the scary stimuli, especially when you “just can’t” with your kids’ meltdown either. This is common, or, in science speak, highly prevalent:

Accommodation was highly prevalent across all anxiety disorders and particularly associated with separation anxiety. Most parents reported participation in symptoms and modification of family routines as well as distress resulting from accommodation and undesirable consequences of not accommodating.

But when you change your family routines or “join in” on the avoidance, your child’s anxiety will get worse over time, and the demands to provide yet more FA will only increase.

We get more of the behavior we pay attention to! This is true for anxiety, but it turns out, it’s not true for depression, at least in one study:

Accommodation correlated significantly with anxious but not depressive symptoms, when controlling for the association between anxiety and depression. Factor analysis of the FASA pointed to a two-factor solution; one relating to modifications, the other to participation in symptoms. 3

The good news, published today by Sheppard et. al., is that working with your kiddos to increase their distress tolerance is effective in reducing the anxiety:

Distress tolerance significantly moderated the association between anxiety symptom severity and family accommodation , such that this association was stronger when distress tolerance was lower.

This means that more distress tolerance skills for the kid made it better for everyone. Further, OCD and other mental illnesses and distress in the parent predicted worse outcomes in the kids with OCD, as did higher levels of education: 4

With highly anxious kids, it may make sense to think about 1. treating the whole family, if they are anxious or suffering from OCD also, and 2. teaching distress tolerance skills all around.

With pediatric anxiety, it’s often the whole family unit that is the level at which we have to think about helping.

I had to click that link.

Welcome to The Frontier Psychiatrists, I’m Owen Scott Muir, M.D., DFAACAP, your author.

What is “murder-suicide?” According to the actual paper in JAMA Open:

Murder-suicide events occur when an individual commits 1 or more acts of homicide shortly before taking their own life

And the rates, historically—e.g., before 2004—were low:

prior research indicating stable population rates of approximately 0.2 to 0.3 per 100 000

“Stable rates of terrible things” is not a phenomenon to count on in 2025, it seems. The rates aren’t more common than thought. The rate is increasing. These data were only available from 30 states. They pulled this data together as follows:

Murder-suicide events were coded in NDVRS RAD based on police reports and other information such as death certificates, coroner and medical examiner records regarding whether one or more homicides were followed by suspect suicide within a 24-hour time period. Data on each death associated with the incident, including the suicide decedent and all homicides linked to them, were extracted for analysis. Primary weapon in each victim’s death, homicide decedents’ relationship to suspect, and the number of homicide decedents per murder-suicide incident were included in each study record base on available records.

The big demographic table is here:

Who’s in the sample?

A few things pop out right away: For example, among murderers who will end their own lives too, there are some shared qualities. The “suicide column” here is also the murderer. This is overwhelmingly a crime committed by men, and mostly white men:

2618 suicide decedents ( 2398 male [91.6%] ; 540 Black [20.6%], 1851 White [70.7%] ). 1

And they are white men killing their romantic partners, and sometimes their children:

Deaths between romantic partners were most frequent; 1707 homicide decedents (57.2%) were the former or current romantic partner of the perpetrator.

And the rates are going up, substantially, given the utter horror in question:

The rate rose to 0.5/100,000 in 2022.

If correlation equals causation, scratch off gambling would be protective, as the states with the lowest rates were both early adopters of legalized gambling:

Massachusetts (0.20 per 100 000 persons) and New Hampshire (0.16 per 100 000 persons).

And yes—this is my moment to plug one of my favorite podcasts when you want to follow a story that is both complex and not particularly upsetting, called Scratch and Win.

One surprising note about this data set is the preponderance of male victims (73%) —this didn’t used to be the case! Historically, from this study in the UK, it was male perpetrators and predominantly female victims:

Most perpetrators were male (88 %), and most victims were female (77 %). The incidents were commonly preceded by relationship breakdown and separation. 2

And in that prior paper, it was noted that mental health problems were common,

62 % had mental health problems.

But psychiatric care was not:

[25%] visited a General Practicioner for emotional distress within a month of the incident. Few had been in recent contact with mental health services before the incident (12 %). Self-harm (26 %) and domestic violence (39 %) were common.

These are horrible crimes—the kind of violence that makes the news. However, that very public nature and aprobium make the healing for survivors all the more complex. I’ve cared for murder-suicide survivors in my career, more than once. The experience is almost impossibly complex for survivors. Yet, with increasing rates of death and destruction, there are yet more survivors to care for.

AFSP has an excellent resource on the topic , and highlights this complexity in frank detail:

If your loved one was responsible for killing people, your compassion for victims may be combined with feelings of guilt and humiliation for not being able to prevent what happened.

We do not have published resources specifically addressing what to do in these cases. All the published literature I have found is related to epidemiology. This makes some sense, given how relatively rare the event is—or was at the 2022 rate of 0.5/100,000. We are in the ballpark with other “orphan diseases,” similar to rates of symptomatic Acute Intermittent Porphyria and “Mad Cow” Disease. We spend billions of dollars on some rare diseases, as long as it’s a drug, of course! My very strong suspicion is that the only robust healing tools for survivors are likely to be psychotherapy. However, the benefit to society of bulking up adept therapists is that they might also be able to treat the “emotional distress” of our white male perpetrators, before they do something awful, too.

Lenmeldy is a gene therapy for Metachromatic Leukodystrophy – MLD, which has about twice the rate of death by murder suicide (1/100,000), and has a cost of about $4.5 million per dose. There are many more survivors per murder-suicide death than there are cases of MLD.

Getting serious about preventing these deaths would ask us, as a society, to develop therapies, like MBT-ASPD , for example, that were focused on treating those with a history of violent offending. We’d need to have therapists with expertise in treating some of the least sympathetic characters: angry white men, often with a history of domestic violence, who have been avoiding therapy.

Given the increasing rates…perhaps we should invest more in elite therapists for those whose suffering is both profound and could explode—before it does?
