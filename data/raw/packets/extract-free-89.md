Yesterday, two videos landed on the Soft Shite Underbelly’ s YouTube channel, one of the most broadly watched channels on the Internet. It's not exactly MrBeast, as my kids point out, but with 1.6+ billion views, SWU is a behemoth of the YouTube ecosystem. A lot of the videos feature people telling their story who generally don't have a platform—people who are drug addict addicted, sex workers, inbred individuals from Appalachia, not exactly the cream of the crop. That said, Mark Laita, who created the channel, also likes to feature stories of hope, and that includes the videos he shared of the world yesterday, one of which included myself and a patient talking about her treatment experience and actually getting treatment in the same day , and the second was an interview with Nogga, the technician who administered the treatment. That video is included at the end of this interview with Nogga, and I talked to Nogga about his experience creating this content as a nice introduction to it for my newsletter.

I hope you enjoy getting to know someone I think of with the utmost respect and admiration, my coworker, Nogga.

I’ve been on the channel twice before: Once with carlene , and once solo , which are linked for ease of viewing.

Now…

The Nogga “BTS” Interview

Owen : Would you like to introduce yourself for our readers?

Nogga : I’m Nogga Schwartz. Owen’s “assistant” and colleague for some time now.

Owen : We went to Los Angeles recently to film two videos, it turns out, for the Soft White Underbelly channel. Can you tell the backstory of me approaching you about doing this in the first place?

Nogga : I heard Owen was going to be on Soft White Underbelly again, which is always awesome, and I had planned to go to Chattanooga. He reached out and said, would you like to come to Soft White Underbelly? I said absolutely, swapped all the plans, and came out to LA.

Owen : Start with who you are in your day job. What do you do at Radial?

Nogga : At Radial , I’m the guy who teaches everybody how to use every piece of machinery we use to give treatment. I train, I teach, and I sometimes treat some of the more complicated or experimental cases, because I’ve been doing it longer than most of our team. Owen’s always been a great mentor and explained everything to me, so I understand a lot of the mechanisms. I also do quality control, making sure people keep up with new information and new ways of using the machines.

Owen : The patient in question had asked to do this. It was her request. She knew I had been on Soft White Underbelly before. Her depression had returned after a prior treatment, and she wanted the world to know she could do better, so she asked for her retreatment to do this. That presented some technical challenges. How did you think through getting a TMS machine to Los Angeles, into a video studio, to do treatment?

Nogga : That was kind of a rigmarole. Neither of us is local, and we had to test the equipment to make sure it worked. We didn’t want any hiccups on the day of. It’s a very simple machine to set up, but if you’ve never done it, you don’t know what to look for. There’s only an indicator light to tell you if things are working.

Owen : A single light on the device! Which system did we choose?

Nogga : We ended up working with the Ampa One because of its portability, simplicity, and versatility. Of all the TMS devices, it has quickly become one of my personal favorites, not just in using it but in teaching people. People get it really quick, especially if they like video games. I flew out a couple of days early. I was lucky the hotel was willing to receive it, and I was close to the studio, so I went over, set it up, and ran through it. Because it was a brand new machine, everything worked. There were no hiccups, and I knew some troubleshooting tricks to recover quickly if there were issues, to keep things on time.

Owen : Not that one is better or worse, but we’ve worked with a bunch of TMS systems. What’s different about delivery of the Ampa One compared to other systems we’ve had arrive at our door?

Nogga : One, it’s small. One person with some strong arm muscles pulls a 40-pound box out of a larger box and plugs some things in. It’s really simple, and everything is controlled via an app on a tablet, which the Ampa team can see live whenever they like, to make sure things are running well.

Owen : They can “zoom” into the device itself.

Nogga : I believe they can watch sessions and see the data live on the back end, similar to how Prism can see what we’re doing remotely. Brainsway, when it’s connected to Wi-Fi, can see the data post-treatment. Ampa can see it while things are actively happening, so if there’s a coding or update issue, they’re a little quicker to respond.

Owen : The original version of the Ampa device we tried used an old MagVenture stimulator. Can you talk about the difference between the production device and the original research device?

Nogga : MagVenture makes a great device. There’s a reason they’re one of the top in the industry. They’re always coming out with amazing software and technologies, and their team is large and has been around a long time.

Owen : We use one of their devices as part of the SAINT system. It’s part of our workflow every day in the clinic.

Nogga : Absolutely, and it’s a wonderful device. But it’s like trying to mix Apple products with Samsung products. They don’t love to talk to each other. Everybody wants you to use their equipment with their equipment with their software. The beta Ampa device worked, but it was buggy, because two different companies were trying to talk to each other.

Owen : In a research setting, still, at the time.

Nogga : In a research setting—There was a lot of unplugging, replugging, and troubleshooting, even in the middle of sessions. Now that’s at a minimum. The most common problem, and I’ve talked to Ampa about this, is a very silly one: the case cover slightly covers the charge port on the tablet, so sometimes the cord can’t seat all the way.

Owen : It’s literally the cover of the tablet , not the tablet itself.

Nogga : It’s a one-millimeter issue, solved by surgically going in with a razor blade and making the hole slightly larger. Then the cable fits fine.

Owen : It’s not on the device manufacturers or the tablet manufacturers. It’s the cover manufacturer. Hashtag supply chain.

Nogga : Exactly, and it’s not all of them. Whenever I see the red blinking light for a cable issue, 90 percent of the time it’s solved by removing the cover and reseating the cable. When you line it up, it’s off by a hair, probably a minor manufacturing defect in the mold. The cable we use is thick because it needs to move data quickly, and the head of that cable just barely touches that piece of plastic.

Owen : So part of your job is having common sense about where things could go wrong, because the most common problem we have with that system has nothing to do with the system. It’s the piece of plastic around it that keeps it safe.

Nogga : It’s a silly engineering problem, and it’s my OCD that lets me catch those little details, which I guess is why you keep me around.

Owen : That and many other reasons. So we get to Los Angeles and get this thing set up. Walk us into the studio Mark has in Malibu. Tell the story of walking in the door.

Nogga : I didn’t realize the entrance was in the back of the building, so I figured that out after standing at the front door for ten minutes with all this equipment and my service dog, and you told me to go around back. It’s a really beautiful old warehouse building with concrete floors, tall ceilings, and skylights, so a lot of natural light floods the space. Honestly, it’s the kind of space I dream of acquiring for my own living situation. White walls and a strangely warm atmosphere, gorgeous leather couches, suede slipper chairs, and of course Mark’s larger-than-life portraits of luchadores and bikers, and even some of his underwater photography from his series on fish, which is my personal favorite. It’s like a gallery, but with a big kitchen and a couple of desks where people work. It feels homey, not clinical or sterile, not the kind of gallery where you feel like you can’t touch anything. They were really nice to let me come in.

Owen : It feels very lived in, probably because they spend a heck of a lot of time there. So we come in that morning. What happens leading up to the videos?

Nogga : We sit down, I set up, and I meet Mia for the first time. She’s having a hard time, but even in her hard time she’s a warm, wonderful person. Really fantastic, bubbly. She reminds me of my aunt, whom I dearly love. Party mom vibes, but also a mama bear, and she exudes that in every word. She’s fun and creative, and we connected immediately. It was easy to be open with her about my own struggle, and we found a lot in common. I explained what I was going to do, and she said she was very familiar. We got out the D-cycloserine, which I had brought from New York in a little baggie in my carry-on luggage.

Owen : It’s a sublingual tablet we have to get compounded specifically for our practice. You can’t get it from a regular pharmacy. It has to dissolve under the tongue to get into the bloodstream fast enough. If we didn’t have literal pharmacists as our head of operations, we might not have the skill set to deploy it. You can’t just prescribe it to a pharmacy and call it a day. We’ve had to source component molecules.

Nogga : Ryan spent a long time finding a compounding pharmacy that would fulfill our order at scale, so we can get it to all our clinics. It’s very precious, so I wanted it on me at all times. It did feel strange flying from New York with it. How do I explain this if TSA or the DEA asks? I’ve been caught with expensive, unexplainable materials before. Flying with equipment for my father, I got stopped at TSA in New York for two hours and almost missed my flight because I had radar equipment on me.

Owen : Oh my gosh.

Nogga : It was a whole thing. I’m used to traveling with a giant magnet, not dissimilar from the magnets in an MRI machine. So I was ready, and it’s just this little tablet. They’re thinking, what do you have, LSD? No, it’s an ancient treatment for tuberculosis. Nothing nefarious, I swear, in an unmarked plastic baggie. But nobody cared. Travel went fine. Maybe traveling with an adorable dog makes you less threatening.

Owen : “Why would a terrorist bring a dog?” (laugh)

Nogga : Exactly. So we got through fine. I pulled out my baggie, and we had 30 minutes to wait. Mia held it under her tongue while it dissolved. Mark came around and asked me some questions about the system, and I explained what Ampa was and what we would be doing with the M coil specifically, M meaning medial and L being the lateral coil. Then you got started with Mark not long after.

Owen : The treatment took place over the course of that entire day. The video compresses it into one hour, but we were there for the entirety of the day.

Nogga : Ten hours, easy. I got there at 8:30 in the morning and we finished around 6:30.

Owen : One thing that’s not obvious: Mia looks completely different at the end, but it’s also the end of what would be, for anybody, a really long day. I probably looked more rundown than she did.

Nogga : She came in crying. Her eyes were red and puffy. She was upset, she was sad, clearly going through it. She was very brave to talk about it and even laugh about some things, which is a credit to her character. A strong person who is very relatable.

Owen : Have you looked at the comments on those videos?

Nogga : I read some of the comments under my video. A lot of positive ones. There are trolls out there, of course. There was a lot of “what’s up, my Nogga,” which is a hilarious joke because of the spelling of my name.

Owen : Oh, Because it’s similar to a racist slur.

Nogga : Yep. Hey, my parents didn’t know how to spell when they came to the United States. I got two G’s and I kept them.

Owen : It could have gone worse. It is a beautiful name. One thing I noticed about both this video and the one I’m in with Mia: compared to other YouTube comment sections, which are not known as a pinnacle of human decency, the ratio of reasonable comments to trolls is shockingly in the direction of the reasonable.

Nogga : A lot of positive comments. There were questions about whether this is covered by insurance, and I went in and answered them myself: that’s something we’re working on. There were also a lot of questions about what I did. It’s funny, because had I known the videos would be separate, I would have done a better job explaining what TMS is. That was my one regret.

Owen : You were a big hit, so I’m sure you’d be welcome back.

Nogga : If Mark (Laita) wants to have me again, I’d be happy to chat more about what we do. Not only do I do it for people, I’m a recipient of it, and it has made huge differences in my ability to function better as an ADHD, OCD, PTSD-having person.

Owen : Knowing what you know from your own experience, how does that inform how you hold hope for the person when you’re administering treatment?

Nogga : One thing I can do is tell them exactly what they’re probably going to experience. When people come in, they’re scared. Like Mia said in the video, and like many of our patients have said: what is this? It feels like science fiction. Is it going to hurt? Is this shock therapy? First of all, this is not shock therapy. We’re not using direct currents. The goal of ECT, electroconvulsive therapy, is to cause a seizure to change the brain. Here, that is what we’re trying to avoid. It’s a one in 30,000 chance you’ll have a seizure, unless you forgot to tell us you’re epileptic, or you come in very, very drunk.

Owen : Or, even more worryingly, very, very drunk the night before, and don’t disclose it.

Nogga : Exactly, which is why we check motor thresholds every day. We did have a situation where somebody quote-unquote forgot they had three to four beers the night before during SAINT treatment, so we had to extend by an extra day. An honest mistake, I guess. But this is why we check motor threshold every day. If I see a ten-point difference, something’s not right, and I go through the troubleshooting list. We got successful MTs before. Why is it ten points higher? Did you have a seizure? Medication changes?

Owen : And the doctor is always looped in and gets to decide what to do, and usually the decision is come back tomorrow. At the end of the day of treatment and shooting, Mark offered you the opportunity to do an interview yourself, and you took it. What was going through your mind in that moment?

Nogga : I’ll admit I was very nervous. I’m a man behind the camera, behind the scenes. That’s how I operate.

Owen : Literally behind the camera. You were a photographer, professionally.

Nogga : Yes, for many years, and I still pick up a lens once in a while for friends. I’m not somebody who seeks the limelight. Even when I was in a band, I used to get terrible stage fright while performing. I did poetry slams as a kid, but admittedly I had the help of substances to make me feel braver, and I don’t use substances anymore. Being in front of people and being vulnerable about who and what I am, knowing there are people out there who don’t like who or what I am for whatever reason, is sometimes nerve-racking. So I was maybe slightly dissociated. I know I was rambling and said “um” a lot, as I’m doing now. When I’m not on camera, I speak quickly and confidently. When the eyeballs are on, it’s a little more difficult.

Owen : What has it been like to have that video out in the world?

Nogga : I’m glad it’s out there. There was a comment from a young trans person that really felt good, and it reminded me why I was in the band I was in, and why I think it’s important to be outspoken.

Owen : That band was an all-trans band, if I remember correctly.

Nogga : All trans, all Jewish, and we sang very silly songs about being gay and trans and Jewish. We were hilarious. Sondheim meets Tom Lehrer meets The Kinks. Absolute shtick rock. But that visibility was important, because there wasn’t a lot of positive visibility of trans people. Being out there, being alive, talking about it, being honest about my struggles and my successes, hopefully shows younger people that they shouldn’t punch their ticket too early. Things can change, and there are opportunities. The work we do plays into that. Queer kids have the highest suicide rates, the highest rates of substance addiction, homelessness, joblessness. And I don’t want this just for trans kids. I want it for everyone, because when you protect the most vulnerable, everybody gets better.

Owen : So the video was, in a way, part of the work we do. Being alive and living a good life as a trans person is a form of suicide prevention?

Nogga : It preserves life and culture and art and everything that comes with that. Every time we lose anybody to suicide, not just a trans person, that’s a light that could have contributed to a better world. That could be the next brilliant musician or engineer, and to lose them because they didn’t see themselves in the world, or because their brain chemistry was a little different and was fighting them and lying to them, as brain chemistry has a tendency to do, is a shame. That’s why I do it. In the video I talked about three of my friends, but really I’ve lost way more than that.

Owen : In a way, living comfortably as who you are is the message of hope you have for the world.

Nogga : Yeah, and I question it myself. I have my dark days. Like I said in the video, a month ago I was doing bad. Really bad. I was incredibly depressed. My partner was worried about me. My parents were worried about me. The saving grace is that I work in this industry now, so I can recognize it, I know my options, and I can access those options. And I’m really lucky I work with you as a colleague and a mentor.

Owen : Not for nothing, shout out to my dear friends at Acacia Clinics, who do a great job with patients. You were able to reach out, find good help, get it to take your insurance, and get it to work. It’s a remarkable thing. Living as our authentic selves is hope-setting, in a way.

Nogga : In the queer community, we say living is an act of resistance. Resistance against anything you want to resist. Against the darkness. You can be political about it if you want, but just living is everything.

Owen : The darkness is all of our enemies. Well, that was a very profound ending, and my children just got home, so I’m going to wrap. Thank you for your time.

Nogga : Anytime, Owen.

And now, the video.

Dear readers, I suffer from agita . This is only in part because I'm Italian. For the non-Italians in the room, agita is a relatively new word in the English language. It means “anxiety, stress, or agitation.” However its root doesn't come from the same root of the word agitation .

Agita , which first appeared in American English in the mid-late 20th century, comes from a dialectical pronunciation of the Italian word acido , meaning "heartburn" or "acid," from Latin acidus . ( Agita is also occasionally used in English with the meaning "heartburn.") For a while the word's usage was limited to New York City and surrounding regions, but the word became more widespread in the mid-1990s.

It's at this point that I remember-1the mid 1990s were, now, a long time ago.

This specific agita that I'm referencing? It's about bipolar depression. Regular readers will be familiar with the fact that the editor and most frequent author here on The Frontier Psychiatrists newsletter, Owen Muir, M.D., has bipolar disorder. For most people with bipolar disorder, most of the time spent sick? It is time spent depressed .

There is a paucity of options with strong evidence for bipolar depression. The most famous medicine for bipolar disorder, lithium (link to Dr. Chris Aiken’s awesome guide), which I still recommend people try as early in the disorder as possible, doesn't treat acute bipolar depression .

The only medicines with FDA labels for the treatment of acute bipolar depression are:

• Quetiapine (Seroquel / Seroquel XR) — bipolar I and II depression, monotherapy

• Lurasidone (Latuda) — bipolar I depression, monotherapy and adjunct to lithium or valproate

• Cariprazine (Vraylar) — bipolar I depression, monotherapy

• Lumateperone (Caplyta) — bipolar I and II depression, monotherapy and adjunct to lithium or valproate

• Olanzapine–fluoxetine combination (Symbyax) — bipolar I depression

And electroconvulsive therapy’s class II clearance includes acute bipolar depression in the language. Prevention of depressive relapse adds lamotrigine as one more option.

I'm gonna speed run the problem with the above acute drugs: they're all atypical antipsychotics, and that means weight gain, that means akathisia, that means Tardive Dyskinesia. They also just don't work for everybody. As someone has personally put up with every one of those side effects, I can tell you, with all the honesty that anyone could muster, the side effects are miserable.

We can go back-and-forth on the number needed-to-harm and relative risk of those medication's if we really wanted to, but I don't want to. I want to talk about something new that doesn't have any of those risks.

But before I talk about that new thing, I wanna point out that there's a problem. You see how short that list is? Do you see how limited the range of options are? That's actually because of an economic problem. There's a biological problem alongside it, but primarily we are talking about a problem with the market size of bipolar depression.

There are fewer people with bipolar depression than there are with unipolar depression.

That means if you're walking into the door of an investor for your New Thing . And it might help some kind of depression. You're not sure if it's gonna help bipolar depression, or unipolar, depression, or even work equally well in both? You're going to say it works for major depressive disorder a.k.a. unipolar depression, instead of bipolar depression. Because that increases the chance that you'll get the money to go develop The New Thing in the first place.

Here is a quick Claude-assisted graphic of the problem:

And that $1.7b number? It is an estimate based on all bipolar medicines (including maintenance and mania indications) not just a bipolar depression indication. The ration of the market size is 5:1, and so it’s slightly daft to go try to raise money for a smaller problem which also has the risk of bipolar therapeutic development.

We talk routinely about the unmet need of patients with depression. But not every FDA-labeled treatment for major depressive disorder causes obesity, movement disorders, and more. Bipolar patient's face even more pressure, and understandably, so, to continue taking medication, because the cost of relapse can be a life ruined.

If you don't believe me, just ask Kanye. 1

Bipolar depression is more of an unmeetable need, at least in Pharma and med device—because between two pitches, the founder who promises to go after a market 5x bigger wins, all things being equal.

There's another problem—bipolar depression trials are less likely to show a difference between active agent and placebo. About half of clinical trial programs for unipolar depression drugs submitted to FDA fail. In Bipolar depression, the drug-placebo gap is smaller (roughly 55% vs 39% response) 2 . High Placebo response regularly erases it this difference—remember, the natural history of bipolar depression is to get better pretty quickly, and most drugs work relatively slowly, and so the natural history of the illness, including spontaneous remission, is often working more quickly than any drug could hope to work.

Return returning to my earlier point about raising money to solve the problem? Ardent founders would have to pitch “smaller market, and…wait for it… higher risk !”

That's bad math for fundraising, and so we see, essentially, a raft of me-too drugs to address this crucial problem. These options are not enough. I've previously written about the promise of accelerated transcranial magnetic stimulation, particularly the SNT approach, in bipolar depression. It does look like that's likely to work—but somebody has to do a large enough trial, submitted to the FDA, to change the label, and that just starts the conversation with payers about coverage.

Today we have more data, that's hopeful, but unlikely to lead to meaningful coverage, because the academic researchers chose to study something. I bring you: IV ketamine. For bipolar depression.

The authors of the KET-BD study included the following participants:

The Ket-BD study (Ketamine for Treatment-Resistant Bipolar Disorder) was an investigator-led, double-blind, midazolam-controlled randomized clinical trial conducted at 3 sites in Ontario, Canada, from July 2022 to November 2025. Participants included adult outpatients (aged 21-65 years) with a primary bipolar I or II disorder DSM-5 diagnosis with a current moderate to severe major depressive episode ([MADRS] score ≥21), having had at least 2 failed trials of evidence-based pharmacotherapies.

The subject were randomized to:

four 40-minute infusions of 0.5 to 0.75 mg/kg of ketamine or 0.02 to 0.03 mg/kg of midazolam, adjunctive to a stable dose of at least one antipsychotic or mood stabilizer, over 2 weeks.

There were more dropouts in the placebo group:

Of the participants, five — one of whom was assigned to ketamine — withdrew before the study’s primary endpoint.

Which to me speaks to the lack of integrity of IV midazolam as a blind, but the data on dose #1 didn’t support unblinding at that time point:

47% of participants correctly guessed the treatment allocation after the first ketamine infusion “despite the ketamine arm having greater dissociation during infusions.”

And the treatment worked :

The researchers reported that the mean MADRS score at 14 days was significantly lower in the ketamine group vs. the midazolam group (between-group difference = –7.3 points; 95% CI, –12 to 2.5).

The split between bipolar I and bipolar II (my usual “did the Study just enroll people with depression who wanted to get ketamine by pretending to have bipolar mania disorder in the distant past?” BS filter) was not completely unreasonable. It looks like they enrolled sick people.

My colleague Nico Grundmann, at Ember Health, assures me that this is a routine part of their care, and that it works well. Here's another piece of data suggesting that that's the case. There's no FDA labeled treatment for acute relief of bipolar depression. There needs to be.

Dear Pharma companies, let's do this . Patients with bipolar disorder, like myself, need access to more treatments. Ideally those treatments will work quickly.

I feel pretty terrible that even as a Psychiatrist— and as clinical trial principal investigator— I have done precious little, personally, to advance the well-being of people who have the bipolar disorder that I am also living my way through. I feel guilty that I'm lucky enough to be able to get off-label treatments like transcranial magnetic stimulation and ketamine because, even though I'm not wealthy, I was able to scrape together the money. Most people aren't that kind of lucky. Most people don't even know if it would be worth their hard earned dollars or those of their family. I'm luckier than most. I haven't done enough, personally, to make it better for people like me. If this article reaches one person who gets to make the decision on which company to cut a check into next, and it's one that wants to make a big dent in bipolar disorder, feel free to call me, and I'll tell you it's a good idea. This will be less true financially than it will be emotionally, but I'm OK with that. Investing in the lives of people with bipolar disorder is the right choice.

To Those I’ve Hurt:

Twenty-five years ago, I was in a car accident that broke my jaw and caused injury to the right frontal lobe of my brain. At the time, the focus was on the visible damage—the fracture, the swelling, and the immediate physical trauma. The deeper injury, the one inside my skull, went unnoticed.

Comprehensive scans were not done, neurological exams were limited, and the possibility of a frontal-lobe injury was never raised. It wasn’t properly diagnosed until 2023. That medical oversight caused serious damage to my mental health and led to my bipolar type-1 diagnosis .

Bipolar disorder comes with its own defense system. Denial. When you’re manic, you don’t think you’re sick. You think everyone else is overreacting. You feel like you’re seeing the world more clearly than ever, when in reality you’re losing your grip entirely.

Once people label you as “crazy,” you feel as if you cannot contribute anything meaningful to the world. It’s easy for people to joke and laugh it off when in fact this is a very serious debilitating disease you can die from. According to the World Health Organization and Cambridge University, people with bipolar disorder have a life expectancy that is shortened by ten to fifteen years on average, and a 2x-3x higher all-cause mortality rate than the general population. This is on par with severe heart disease, type 1 diabetes, HIV, and cancer - all lethal and fatal if left untreated.

The scariest thing about this disorder is how persuasive it is when it tells you: You don’t need help. It makes you blind, but convinced you have insight. You feel powerful, certain, unstoppable.

I lost touch with reality. Things got worse the longer I ignored the problem. I said and did things I deeply regret. Some of the people I love the most, I treated the worst. You endured fear, confusion, humiliation, and the exhaustion of trying to have someone who was, at times, unrecognizable. Looking back, I became detached from my true self.

In that fractured state, I gravitated toward the most destructive symbol I could find, the swastika, and even sold T-shirts bearing it. One of the difficult aspects of having bipolar type-1 are the disconnected moments - many of which I still cannot recall - that led to poor judgment and reckless behavior that oftentimes feels like an out-of-body-experience. I regret and am deeply mortified by my actions in that state, and am committed to accountability, treatment, and meaningful change. It does not excuse what I did though. I am not a Nazi or an antisemite. I love Jewish people.

To the black community - which held me down through all of the highs and lows and the darkest of times. The black community is, unquestionably, the foundation of who I am. I am so sorry to have let you down. I love us.

In early 2025, I fell into a four-month long manic episode of psychotic, paranoid and impulsive behavior that destroyed my life. As the situation became increasingly unsustainable, there were times I didn’t want to be here anymore.

Having bipolar disorder is notable state of constant mental illness. When you go into a manic episode, you are ill at that point. When you are not in an episode, you are completely ‘normal’. And that’s when the wreckage from the illness hits the hardest. Hitting rock bottom a few months ago, my wife encouraged me to finally get help.

I have found comfort in Reddit forums of all places. Different people speak of being in manic or depressive episodes of a similar nature. I read their stories and realized that I was not alone. It’s not just me who ruins their entire life once a year despite taking meds every day and being told by the so-called best doctors in the world that I am not bipolar, but merely experiencing “symptoms of autism.”

My words as a leader in my community have global impact and influence. In my mania, I lost complete sight of that.

As I find my new baseline and new center through an effective regime of medication, therapy, exercise, and clean living, I have newfound, much-needed clarity. I am pouring my energy into positive, meaningful art: music, clothing, design, and other new ideas to help the world.

I’m not asking for sympathy, or a free pass, though I aspire to earn your forgiveness. I write today simply to ask for your patience and understanding as I find my way home.

With love,

I love me my headphones.

Owen Muir: Tell me a little bit about the backstory. How did you, Randy, even become a doctor?

Randy Pardell: When I was born, the pediatrician smacked my tushy and my mother said, “My son the doctor.”

Owen: Is this a true story?

Randy: That’s what she said through all these years. If we go through the backstory, my grandparents were immigrants from Eastern Europe. My grandfather on my father’s side owned a grocery store and had worked in sales, in produce. They had three sons, and all three became doctors. My uncle Seymour was a psychiatrist, my father was a dentist, and my younger uncle was an internist. On my mother’s side, my mother’s father was a jeweler. They had four daughters, and three out of the four married doctors.

So it was the family business. I have lots of first cousins in it too. Second and third generation folks who came into this country, escaping pogroms and other things, and saw the land of opportunity, a way to have the American dream.

Owen: Which part of Eastern Europe did your family escape, essentially?

Randy: Somewhere between Poland and Russia. Russia and Poland kind of shared borders at that time. One of my grandfathers, my mother’s father, was from Austria-Hungary, while my grandmother on my mother’s side was from Russia. So Russia, Poland, and Austria-Hungary, that’s my background.

Owen: And you’re second generation from that?

Randy: Third generation. My dad was born in the United States. My mother was born in the United States. They both were from Passaic, New Jersey. Corporal Agarn from F Troop is from Passaic, New Jersey.

They met after my father was in the war and came back. My great-grandfather on my mother’s side actually stole a picture of my father, because he knew my grandfather, and said, “This is the guy you’re gonna marry.” They went on a blind date and got married very soon afterwards. They were married for 62 years, a really loving relationship.

My mom was a Spanish teacher by training. She worked as a dental assistant initially, then was a stay-at-home mom. She and all of her sisters had problems with weight, and one of my uncles started giving amphetamines to all of the sisters to lose weight. My mom developed a psychotic break from that.

Owen: Oh, wow.

Randy: She ended up being admitted to Gracie Square Hospital and had ECT back in the early 1960s. That’s really the backstory of how I ended up getting into medicine and psychiatry.

She went back for two and a half, three weeks. I don’t have any recollection of that time. But she came out, and she was doing so much better, and she had this really great psychiatrist working with her, Noel Galen. He said, “You’re really smart. You should do something about getting a career.” So she went back to school, went to Yeshiva University, got her master’s and postdoc, and became a psychologist.

Owen: Oh, wow.

Randy: She was a disciple of Albert Ellis at that time. She wanted to make sure we all didn’t follow in the family neurosis, so we all were in therapy at very young ages. I remember very clearly going to the brownstone of Albert Ellis.

Owen: For the audience, who was Albert Ellis?

Randy: Albert Ellis was a really fascinating man. He did rational emotive therapy. It was sort of a cognitive behavioral therapy at the time. He would hold these Friday evening soirees where people would all come and share information, research, commentary, back and forth. All the intelligentsia of the psychological world would be together on that Friday evening.

Owen: Today we’d call it a live events motion.

Randy: It was a live events motion. He would have it every Friday, and it was really well known that this was the place to be if you were in the psychology intelligentsia of New York City. You would be at Albert Ellis’s on Friday night at his townhouse. My mom would go, and I remember one day she brought me to meet him. He comes down, this big shock of white hair, and introduces himself to me.

So she went back to school, and I got really interested in what she was doing, got very involved in psychology. I was the sort of kid that my friends used to always talk to, because I’d been in therapy and I could talk to them about things.

When I finished high school, I went to Horace Mann in the city.

Owen: Horace Mann is a well thought of high school.

Randy: A well thought of private high school in New York City. My first doubles tennis partner was Eliot Spitzer, the former governor of the state. Some really very smart people went there. It was a very challenging academic environment, but great. I did well there and ended up going to the University of Pennsylvania.

Owen: I’ve heard of that one.

Randy: At Penn, I was really interested in biology and psychology, and I was enthralled with one of my sophomore psychology courses. It was taught by Martin Seligman. Marty was a really brilliant guy who had a model for depression, learned helplessness. He put dogs in cages and would shock them uncontrollably, and they wouldn’t get out of the cages. Learned helplessness was his model for depression. Eventually he evolved to become the happiness guru, positive psychology, and learned optimism was the next rendition. But he was a wonderful lecturer and speaker, and I became his research assistant.

Penn had just started a biological basis of behavior major, a bio-psych major, which had biology, psychology, sociology, and anthropology all together. Penn really likes interdisciplinary interaction. I was in the first class of BBB majors and really enjoyed it. I was also president of the pre-med honor society, and got involved with the Ware College of Health and Society.

Then I graduated and went to NYU for medical school. I remember in my third and fourth years, I did really well, and all my internist professors said, “What are you doing wanting to go into psychiatry? You’re too bright for psychiatry.” And I said, “No, I love this stuff.”

I went to a Columbia-affiliated program at St. Luke’s-Roosevelt and had some really great training experience. I was a research associate for Alexander Glassman. Sandy Glassman was the guy who did the antidepressant blood levels. I was thinking about doing psychopharm research, but then he got real depressed when he couldn’t get a grant and had to fire people. And I said, “Ah, I don’t like this publish or perish. I’m a clinician at heart.”

One of my colleagues, a couple years ahead of me in residency, came up to the Hudson Valley and said it’s a great place to raise a family. My wife at the time, Jamie, and I moved up to Poughkeepsie, New York, and started working. Medicine was in my blood, psychology was in my blood, and I had a fascination with how the brain and body interacted together, and I became a psychiatrist.

Owen: That story would make a lot less sense if you ended with, “And that’s why I’m a vascular surgeon.”

Randy: That’s right. It’s a funny story. My first day of psychiatric residency, Alex Kammerer, the head of the outpatient clinic, takes me to the faculty dining room and says, “Tell me about yourself.” And I said, “My dad’s a dentist, my mom’s a psychologist, I grew up in New Jersey.” He says, “Really? Your dad’s a dentist? That proves my theory.” I said, “What’s your theory?” He said, “Your dad’s a dentist and my dad was a surgeon. You’re becoming a psychiatrist, and I became a psychiatrist. Surgeons are sadists. They inflict pain on their patients. But we as psychiatrists are masochists. Our patients inflict pain on us. It’s a great dyad.” I said, “That was very funny, Alex.”

But truthfully, I really appreciated the interaction. It’s an honor to work with our patients, to enter into their lives in a very meaningful and human way. I never felt like I’m any better than anyone else. We’re all on the same playing field. I might have some wisdom I want to impart to you, but you’re an expert in your own symptoms, and together we have to collaborate to make the best treatment plan. That’s how I’ve operated.

For the first 25 years, I really delved into the world of psychopharm, and was a, quote, “thought leader” for those years.

Owen: And how, I don’t want to be pejorative here, but how did they seduce you?

Randy: It’s really interesting. Back in December of 1987, Prozac came out. I was chief resident at that time at St. Luke’s, running the inpatient unit, and we put our first patient on Prozac. Now, we were the poor cousins of the rich internists, the rich surgeons. No representative ever wanted to see us at that time, because all we had was generic antidepressants and Ativan.

But then all of a sudden there was an explosion in the 90s. The SSRIs came out, the SNRIs came out, the atypical antipsychotics came out. I wanted to learn about it, and I think I was a little bit naive. I said, “Yeah, I’ll go. I’ll talk about it. I’ll learn about it.” They would take us away to these training programs where you would learn everything about the latest research and the FDA registration trials for the medications, and then I would go out and talk about them. I would really go into the PI, all the different aspects, and I really understood all the different medicines. So I would go out there, and I was a talking head.

That happened until I had another seminal experience. I taught a class every year for science and technology at Vassar College, on the influence of the pharmaceutical industry on psychiatry. I read Marcia Angell’s book and some of the other books that were coming out, and I recognized that when you’re a talking head, you’re somebody else’s person. You’re not your own person. The doctor doesn’t have skin in the game when they’re working for the pharmaceutical industry. It’s really the patient who’s paying for the prescription, and the insurance company. I didn’t want to be anybody’s talking head.

I also recognized we had terrible problems, like misrepresenting that olanzapine doesn’t cause weight gain. Those kinds of misrepresentations got Lilly in trouble, and other people in trouble, and there were major lawsuits. So I fell off doing that. For over a decade, I haven’t done any pharmaceutical sponsored talks.

Owen: For the audience, when you’re doing one of those talks, do they let you make your own slides?

Randy: No. You have to use the slides approved by the company and the FDA.

Owen: In theory, that’s to keep it compliant with the law. And that’s a good thing, right? We have regulation in marketing.

Randy: You would think it’s a good thing. A whole bunch of things changed over the past 10 or 15 years. One is making it very compliant. All side effects have to be talked about. You have to talk on label, which means you can’t overpromise that the medicine can do other things beyond what is on the FDA label. That was restricting in some ways, because you want to talk about your personal experience. If somebody brought up a question, you could answer it, but you have to tell them it’s off-label, tell them what your experience has been, and then get back to the information. That’s how they taught you along the way.

Owen: And how much did that pay, in the dollars of the day?

Randy: Somewhere from $750 to $1,500, I think, in the day.

Owen: Compared to how much you’d make seeing patients?

Randy: You have to look at the process. You’re leaving your office, going out for probably two or three hours. It was probably a wash on some level, unless you really focused yourself on going to all these different places and doubling it up.

Owen: This is kind of my point. It wasn’t a big money gig.

Randy: It was not a big money gig. But on the other side, there are people who did a lot of them and set their time aside. There are people who make $100,000, $200,000 a year talking, even in general practice. And you get bigger bucks if you’re doing it from an academic institution. So some people made a significant amount of money. And Abilify was $6.9 billion in sales annually at its peak, something like that.

Owen: So at least for you, it wasn’t more than you would make doing your day job.

Randy: No. Now, there are some aspects of it, Owen, where you’re out there in the community, you become a leader, it establishes your reputation. It’s kind of like doing some marketing for yourself. I got to meet people, I got to interact with people, which I think helped my practice and my reputation.

Owen: So it’s nice to feel like an expert.

Randy: Yes. It’s very flattering, and I think that’s what ends up happening. The representatives who come into your office say, “Oh, you’re a thought leader. Do you want to talk for us?” Right now I’m deferring, obviously. I’m not doing this at this stage of my career. But it’s something that is very flattering for a lot of doctors.

And I think it does a disservice overall to have the same people doing the same things over and over again, talking about the same things. How many lectures do you need about a particular medicine? It’s a little bit much. What the marketing folks in big pharma have definitely said is, “If I see a doctor, that doctor, or nurse practitioner, is more likely to prescribe that medicine.” So they want face time.

Now, the other side of it, and this is something I think is important: sometimes a new medicine comes out and we don’t know about it. I don’t mind speaking to a representative about something new that’s come out. Give me all of the literature, let me look at the PI, let me understand it. So I will talk to a representative if there’s a new medicine out, just to get a sense of what’s going on.

Owen: At a certain point that broke for you. You mentioned olanzapine and weight gain. Was there a moment where you thought, “This doesn’t make sense”?

Randy: When I was giving this lecture at Vassar College, and I did it four out of five years, you had some tough customers there.

Owen: Not easy physicians. They’re actually college students asking real questions.

Randy: They’re college students, and I had to justify it. “Why are you taking money for this?” And I understood that, for my own integrity, for my own ethics. Marcia Angell’s book was a really good one, which says a lot of the money being made by big pharma comes from academic institutions doing the original research. Big pharma buys the original research from the academic institutions and margins it up. It just didn’t make me feel good to be the thought leader for that group.

And also, the other thing I will tell you, Owen: I found TMS.

Owen: But what was that for you?

Randy: That makes a whole difference in the world. It was back in 2006.

Owen: Before it was even FDA approved?

Randy: Before it was even FDA approved. One of my patients, and I’ll give him a shout-out because he’s a great guy, was high up in IBM. I’d been working with him about 10 years, and he says, “I can’t stand the medicines. I can’t stand them anymore. Can’t you do anything?” I said, “Well, there is ECT.” He said, “I don’t want the memory loss associated with ECT.” I said, “I’ve heard about a new treatment called TMS, and at Columbia where I trained, they’re doing the clinical trials.”

So I just called and said, “Can I get the brain stimulation program?” And you know who picked up? Holly Lisanby. Holly was in charge of brain stimulation, and she spent about 25 minutes with me talking about the research, the promise, what to look forward to. I said, “That’s really interesting.”

Then in October of 2008, the FDA granted approval for the NeuroStar TMS therapy system, and my patient was right on it. He says, “So when are you getting your system?” I go, “Yeah, yeah, yeah.” “When are you getting your system?” So I contacted Neuronetics at that point, and Joy Patermo came up and we spent about four or five hours talking about TMS. I then went down to Philadelphia, where Mark George was giving the first talk. The first 75 people in all the United States who had TMS systems were there. Mark gave the plenary talk and I said, “I gotta be in this.” So we bought our first system, and in the fall of 2009 we started treating patients.

Owen: So you were in essentially cohort one?

Randy: I was cohort one. The first 100 clinics in the country that were doing it.

Owen: What was different about the sales pitch, as it were, of early TMS compared to what you’d heard from pharma?

Randy: There’s a real big difference in the fact that you don’t have an insurance company paying for it at that time. It was not reimbursed. Even though it was FDA cleared, all the insurance companies called it experimental. So we went through a big push fighting the insurance companies and doing appeals to the state, over and over and over again. It was really hard. And especially in my community, I’m not in the big city, I’m up in the Hudson Valley, where people didn’t have lots of wealth. People were taking some of their savings, borrowing money, and we were fighting to try to get reimbursement.

Finally, in 2013, Medicare gave TMS an approval, and then all the other insurances came around. That made a big difference. Now there are probably over 320 million people in the United States who have an insurance policy that covers TMS.

Owen: And you and your family have been instrumental in some of that, if I remember correctly.

Randy: I’ll give you another process. I had one site here in Poughkeepsie that I was running as a group practice, and then COVID hit. About a third of TMS practices in the United States paused, and we were one of the third. New York City was an epicenter for COVID, and we didn’t know how it was being transmitted, so we quickly went to telehealth and we held TMS.

I have two sons, Connor and Logan. Connor was working for an electronic health records company, and he has his master’s in healthcare entrepreneurship. He’s a real smart guy. I said, “I’ve been cutting and pasting Zoom links. It takes me an hour every day. Could you help me out?” He says, “Sure, Dad.” In an hour, he fixed it so I didn’t have to cut and paste Zoom links.

Then I said, “I was just elected president-elect of the Clinical TMS Society, and I’m not treating any TMS patients. Could you help me make it safe and start doing this?” And he says, “I’ll do it under a couple of conditions. I have to see if the business makes sense. And I want my brother Logan to be involved.” Logan is a lawyer who does employment law. They both came in and started working with me.

We started back after a couple of months treating patients, and started growing. Now we’re going to be opening our tenth site in Syracuse. Connor came full time in October 2023, and we’re building out general psychiatry, TMS, and Spravato sites throughout New York State.

As far as insurance, we really pushed. We wrote lots of letters to Letitia James, the attorney general for the state of New York, because it was very important for her to have good mental health care in New York. And we worked with the insurance committee of the Clinical TMS Society to write letters of support to develop a Medicaid policy for TMS. As of October of last year, there’s a policy in New York State for TMS for Medicaid.

Our vision is that in New York State, we want to provide accessible care that’s affordable. We take Medicaid for TMS, we take Medicare for TMS, we take managed Medicare, managed Medicaid. We try to be an open door for patients who are struggling with treatment-resistant psychiatric conditions.

Owen: Can you explain a little more about what TMS is, and why it’s important for patients who may be Medicaid beneficiaries?

Randy: First, for full disclosure, I’m also the president of the Foundation to Advance Clinical TMS, called FACTMS . While I was president of the Clinical TMS Society, the board of directors supported the formation of a foundation. It’s a 501(c)(3) charitable organization to increase access and awareness of TMS therapy around the world. So it’s very important to me personally, to the board of the foundation, and to my practice to provide affordable care for TMS.

Owen: Which, for the audience, also includes my wife on that board.

Randy: Yes, Carlene McMillan is on the board, and what a wonderful addition. So, transcranial magnetic stimulation uses an MRI-strength magnet, and we stimulate the areas of the brain that are either under-functioning or over-functioning, and we help balance the brain’s activity.

I was watching your podcast, Owen. I love the idea of fine-tuning the piano, getting the piano tuner. We’re tuning the piano so you can make good music. I often use the metaphor that the brain is an electrochemical organ, and you can affect the electrochemical accuracy of the brain through TMS. It’s like getting a tune-up for your brain. And when your brain’s tuned, it works really well.

It’s been FDA cleared since October of 2008 for treatment-resistant depression. It’s approved for augmentation for obsessive-compulsive disorder. It’s approved for adolescents age 15 and up as an augmentation strategy. It’s approved for smoking cessation. It’s approved for migraine headaches. And there are accelerated protocols, such as SAINT and the BrainsWay Swift, that are approved as well. There’s a lot of movement and a lot of excitement in the field.

As far as the Medicaid population, we know that the severely and persistently mentally ill struggle with being able to work, with having consistent employment over the course of their lives. A lot of times they end up needing social services and Medicaid and Medicare.

Owen: Just to zoom in on that. Medicaid is insurance for people who are not well-to-do. Medicare is for older people mostly, sometimes fully disabled people. But Medicaid is insurance, created at the same time as Medicare in the 1960s, that takes care of people who don’t have any money. And one of the things you’re highlighting is that one of the reasons people might not have any money is because they can’t work because of their mental health condition.

Randy: Correct. And one of the things I’ve been so amazed about: probably somewhere around a third of the patients we treat are on some form of public assistance. I find it such an important value to be able to help those patients. A lot of times people think, “Oh, TMS is only for the wealthy.” No, no, no. TMS is for patients who are really struggling. Depression and OCD go through all socioeconomic strata. It doesn’t matter if you’re poor or rich. But the extra burden of having a mental illness and struggling financially is onerous, and that’s why we feel so good about being able to support those patients.

We also get Medicaid transport. With the non-accelerated protocols, you have to come in daily through a period of six weeks, and we support our patients in getting to treatment.

Owen: So if you have Medicaid, and many people who have Medicaid are disabled in some meaningful way, you can also qualify for a service like medical transport. And you work with medical transport to help get patients who might otherwise be housebound to the clinic to get this treatment. What is the change like for someone who comes in on the bottom rung of the ladder economically, but also emotionally? They are definitionally depressed if they’re getting TMS, or maybe they have OCD or related conditions, because that’s what insurance usually covers. Walk me through the patient walking in the door on day one, and what they look like when they walk out at the end of treatment.

Randy: I would say the vast majority of our patients have a positive response to TMS. What does that mean? There are functional improvements. They have better mental energy, better physical energy. They’re able to do housework when they weren’t able to do housework. They have better concentration, better motivation, better interest than they had before.

I don’t know how many times, and I know you’ve seen this too, Owen: “It saved my life. It saved my life.” Occasionally you see that in the world of pharma, where you give a medication and, “Oh, that really made such a huge difference.” But this is every single day when I’m going to our patients and asking, “How are things going?” “It’s made such a huge difference in my life.”

And the quality of the change is different. A lot of times with medications, what we find is it takes the edge off the negative emotions, but it doesn’t enhance the positive emotions. Depression is the presence of negative emotions and the absence of positive. TMS gives you both. It gets rid of the negative emotions and enhances the positive emotions.

Owen: It’s a motivation transplant.

Randy: It’s a motivation transplant. It gets that dopamine system, the motivational system in the brain, back online again. One of our patients said to me early on, “I found myself dancing again. I found myself singing again.” Which is something they weren’t doing before.

And even some of the patients who say, “Well, I don’t know,” and this is something I often see: they’re used to being depressed. They’re not used to not being depressed. So I’ll see the husband: “Oh, she’s so much better.” “Well, I don’t know if I’m better.” “Didn’t you know that you were actually reading a book, which you hadn’t been doing before?” And eventually the patient says, “Yeah, I did make those changes.”

Sometimes you’ve got to break that depressive character. Once you get the brain back online, there’s the depressive character too, and that’s where psychotherapy comes in. You can really assist the TMS by engaging the patient in psychotherapy.

Owen: Can you tell me a little more about how you at ClearWave, your group, think about the role of psychotherapy? You grew up around and about psychotherapy royalty.

Randy: Right. We were much more heavily a medical model. At one point we just had psychiatrists, practitioners, a few therapists. We recognized that we wanted to build out our psychotherapy model. We currently have eight therapists working with us, soon to be nine, and we’re growing our therapy practice because we really do believe in the combination of psychotherapy along with TMS. Gerald Klerman back in the day said psychotherapy along with medication management gives you the best response to depression. We really do see that.

One of the areas where I see it being beneficial: if I feel like I am better, I want to go out, but I’m frightened. I don’t know what it’s like to not be depressed, especially if I’ve been depressed for 10, 15 years. That’s where I often say some cognitive behavioral therapy might be helpful, some motivational therapy might be very helpful while patients are getting their TMS.

Owen: “Might be very helpful.” What does very helpful actually mean in their life?

Randy: I think helpful means you have a wise person who understands your mental wellbeing, understands the challenges you’ve been through, and is going to be a guide to assist you in having as full a life as possible. The importance of a therapeutic and healing relationship is really very important. It may not matter what type of therapy you’re having. It’s about a holding environment, a caring environment, with a wise person who’s genuinely interested in you.

In that trusted environment, you start analyzing your perspectives. You start analyzing yourself, yourself in your relationships, yourself in your history, and start to say, “Maybe I can change my internal perspective and accept that I’m worthy, accept that I have value in life, accept that I am worthy of not being involved in dysfunctional relationships, and I’m going to work hard on managing that.” So I see therapists as wonderful guides for our patients.

Owen: On the journey to worthiness.

Randy: Yeah.

Owen: Let’s pivot here. I’m curious to hear your take on the many new innovations that have come out the door in the past couple of years, with more on the way. What have you seen that’s the most exciting and promising to you, as someone who has spent a lot of time with this one modality, TMS, now that we have all these other things?

Randy: I do love TMS as a platform technology. How we do TMS is really exciting. Being very close to some of the leaders in the field, I think we can be giving TMS much more effectively. Whether that’s using functional imaging and making it low cost so it can be used more effectively, or looking at the fact that you don’t just have to hit one spot in the brain, you might be able to hit more than one spot to get more efficacy. Making TMS more effective, so that instead of being 60% effective, it’s 90% effective or more. That’s one area.

I do think wearables might be down the road. There’s a trigeminal and occipital nerve stimulator. I thought that was pretty cool. I had it on me, tried it on. Having something that can be used at home, some app-based changes, would be very helpful as well.

We are also doing Spravato therapy, so I think the psychedelics are very interesting for making fundamental changes for patients. That’s also very exciting for our future. The standard monoamine serotonin reuptake inhibitors, the SSRIs, the SNRIs, may actually be in the past. We’ll say, “How did we ever do that?” in the next 10 or 15 years, when we’re doing much more transformational changes with neuromodulation and potentially some of the psychedelic treatments down the line.

Owen: If you’re talking to someone in your life, and this happens to me all the time, I’ll hear the stories from people who know me socially. They hear about what you do, and suddenly they become very interested. This, at least for me, puts me in a tough spot. I’m not your doctor, I’m your friend. How have you navigated being an utmost expert in the field, and then having people in your own life who are suffering? How do you help friends and loved ones navigate this crazy system when they come to you, and you know all that you know, but you kind of can’t be their doctor?

Randy: I will try to find trusted sources, other people in the community that I can refer patients to. One of the things I feel really good about in my community is I’ve got some great doctors who work with me. So if I happen to know somebody, I can say, “I’ve got this doctor here, this doctor there, that I can refer you to. It’s not me working with you, but it’s somebody I’ve vetted and feel is a really good doctor or nurse practitioner.”

I think it’s important to have appropriate boundaries. Don’t ever write a prescription for one of your friends, that kind of thing. But I usually say, “Look, I can’t be your doctor, but let me listen. I’m a human being here who has some wisdom. Let me hear what you’re going through.” That’s one of the wonderful things about being a psychiatrist and really being trained: being able to empathize, see the world through their eyes, and then understand what to do to help them. It’s usually giving them some advice, recommending appropriate resources and referrals, and even making that extra call. “Hey, I have a friend of mine, and I would really appreciate if you could take that person on.”

Owen: So there’s support, and listen, and phone a friend.

Randy: Phone a friend. That little extra warm handoff.

Owen: What’s it like working with your kids? It sounds like they’re integral.

Randy: It’s one of the most wonderful things, and at times very frustrating. I mean, you work with your wife, you understand. The wonderful thing is you never usually get to see your children work in an adult way. If you’re a parent, you hear about it, but here you’re actually seeing how your professional children interact in the world of a medical care business. They surprise me in really lovely ways, with professionalism and intelligence and support.

It’s very frustrating because I used to be in charge of everything. If I wanted to do a thing, I did it. But now it’s, “No, you can’t do that, Dad. You have to follow the chain of command. You have directors that handle that. You can’t just jump in.” So I had to pull away and say, it’s no longer just my mom-and-pop shop. It’s now an organization, and I have to respect its boundaries.

Owen: Pardell & Sons.

Randy: And Sons, yeah. Connor had worked in corporate settings, and Logan is an employment lawyer with his own practice beyond what he does for us. So we have to be appropriate in the way we interact with our staff. We’ve brought in some really lovely ways of providing support, encouragement, and benefits for our staff so they feel well taken care of.

Owen: What do you think your sons would say about working for you?

Randy: I think they really like it. I think they’re proud of what we’re building together. I often say we have our own lanes. I have the clinical lane, and I’m really passionate. I don’t feel like I’m slowing down. I’m revving up right now, Owen. I’m really enjoying the work we’re doing. They have their lanes: the legal aspect, human resources, and being CEO. Connor’s our CEO, and Logan’s our corporation counsel and head of human resources. Everybody’s in their lane. I respect their lanes, they respect mine, and that way we can be most effective.

Owen: But you report to your son.

Randy: In the organizational hierarchy, yep, I report to my son. We’re all co-owners, but I report to my son.

Owen: My dream and Carlene’s dream is to report to our children as well.

Randy: Well, I’ll give you some tips along the way. It’s really very lovely to see that they actually chose to come work with me. Which meant, like, I did okay. My wife and I did okay in raising our kids, that they feel comfortable enough to want to spend the time they do helping build something we’re doing together.

Owen: Many kids want to get the heck out of the house, and they are not necessarily coming back into the office.

Randy: But yeah, it’s great. Both of my sons have great wives, and I just welcomed a granddaughter, Sloane, three months ago, so I’m excited about being a grandparent. That’s all the rewarding aspects as well. And it’s very rewarding helping so many of our patients. That really gets me. Just like, “Let me go in there. Let’s keep on helping our patients.”

Owen: The story you heard on day one in the faculty dining room was off by a little bit. Surgeons inflict pain, as it were, on their patients to heal them, and maybe are sadistic in that joking way. And for psychiatrists, it’s not the patients inflicting pain on us at all, if we’re doing it right.

Randy: Right. They give us a lot of rewarding experience.

Owen: But we are a little bit masochist, because we have to go out and figure out how to get the insurance companies to actually pay.

Randy: That’s where the masochism comes in. That’s the one that hits you over the head.

Owen: I’m curious. You run a not-for-profit organization, so you have to be careful with what you say. Do you have sincere advice for our colleagues who are working at payers, for how they could think a little differently about TMS, to help them catch up with what you’ve described as a tidal wave of change over many years, all in the direction of more TMS, not less? Every time TMS has been evaluated, eventually it’s determined that this is a great option and more people should have access to it. What advice would you have for colleagues at payers when they’re reevaluating TMS, or any new technology, for how they can get the show on the road?

Randy: It’s really interesting. Just to let you know, the foundation is having a free webinar next month, on September twenty-ninth. facttms.org is our website. Sam Huber, who is medical director of the neuromodulation program at MVP Health Plan, is going to be one of the guests, I’m going to be one of the guests, and Ian Cook is going to be the moderator.

I would love TMS to have the openness that another neuromodulation treatment, ECT, has. You can do maintenance treatment. You can use it for bipolar depression. And I would love it, if there’s enough data that eventually comes, for patients who have auditory hallucinations, visual hallucinations, to be able to appropriately treat those severe illnesses with this wonderful technology.

What you usually see in the process is two different directions. One is the evidence basis. We need the double-blind randomized controlled trials. That’s why, in my heart of hearts, I would love for the Foundation for the Advancement of Clinical TMS to get enough money to support double-blind randomized trials for other conditions, like bipolar depression, like schizophrenia and other illnesses. Then we could say to the insurance company, “Evidence-based.”

The other is the value option. If somebody is not doing well, they have more hospitalizations, more medicines, more debilitation. It’s going to cost the insurance companies more to care for patients who are undertreated. And TMS is an observable treatment. You make sure the patient actually gets the treatment. Especially if we can get some accelerated protocols that go very quickly, done within a one-day to five-day period of time, you can definitely make sure the patient’s getting the treatment. Then you can say, “It’s effective, patients are getting it, and the cost benefit is there.” If you can provide that to our insurer colleagues so they can be convinced, you’re going to start to see more and more conditions approved by insurance companies.

Owen: So you really think it’s going to take yet more evidence?

Randy: The two ways I see it are yet more evidence, or you have large registries, like the NeuroStar registry, where you see patients who have significant benefits. It’s done in a naturalistic way, and the FDA is convinced. That’s just what happened with adolescent depression. The FDA was convinced that the majority of adolescents age 15 and up who were on antidepressant medications and got TMS did very well. So they gave the approval for 15 and up, and then most insurance companies came along. That’s the other avenue, registries, and that’s something I would like the Foundation for the Advancement of Clinical TMS to also support.

Owen: You both succeeded and failed as a parent. Neither of your children are doctors. Are there more than the two that I know?

Randy: Just the two boys.

Owen: They didn’t end up as doctors. They did end up in healthcare anyway.

Randy: They did. And I love being a doctor. I feel like, how cool is it, using science to help heal people?

Owen: And bringing motivation and joy.

Randy: Motivation and joy. It’s wonderful. Now, everybody’s got their own drummer. I’m thrilled that my kids are involved in the business aspect of healthcare. And this is where I like the chief medical officer role. You’re a chief medical officer, I’m a chief medical officer. You can have an effect on a lot of people through your operating procedures, your policies, your training. You can benefit thousands of people.

Owen: If you’re giving advice to our readers and listeners about whether they’re going to be a doctor or not, would you still say they should be a doctor?

Randy: Everybody’s going to ask, what’s in it for me? If you enjoy having the ability to have a personal relationship with somebody and using your skills to heal people, there’s nothing better than being a doctor.

It’s expensive being a doctor. You have four years of college and four years of medical school, and a lot of our physician colleagues come out with a lot of loans, and that can restrict some of your options. There are some lovely institutions. My alma mater, NYU, gives free tuition. Some of the medical schools are getting endowments for that. If you have that, that’s great. Become a doctor, I think it’s wonderful. But every person has to answer that question for themselves.

There is calling, there’s profession, and then there’s job. A job is nine to five. You do it to make money so you can support your family and have some life afterwards. A profession, you do the extra study, get some extra skills so you can make more money. But a calling is what you do through your whole life. It’s calling you.

I know you have that calling, Owen. It’s like, “What are you doing on the weekend?” “Well, I’m reading. I’m learning about my field. I want to know what’s going on, and I want to come back and teach this. I want to help my patients.” When you have that calling, and you definitely see that in medicine, there are a lot of people who feel like medicine’s a calling.

Owen: So for many of us, the advice about whether to be a doctor doesn’t matter, because for people who are going to do it, they kind of had to do it. It was a calling.

Randy: It was a calling. And it’s just so expanding. The science is expanding, the learning is expanding. And with AI, you can be a so much better doctor. I remember carrying my Washington Manual in my lab coat and getting through it, and now you have your little phone and you can ask the questions and get the answers. You can be a so much more effective doctor, and really be a good agent for the benefit of your patients.

Owen: And now a completely self-serving question. What’s your favorite Frontier Psychiatrists article? What have you most enjoyed?

Randy: I really enjoyed the interview you had with Mark Horowitz. I keep this right by my bedside. It was so nice, and I think refreshing, how open he was, and how open you both are, about the struggles, and the importance of recognizing we’re human beings too. We should be very closely aligned to our patients. We’re giving them a pill. What is that pill causing? What kind of benefits? What kind of side effects? Be very aware that some patients are going to have a real hard time getting off their medicine, and we have to be very sensitive to them. I really appreciated that part of the podcast. It was really wonderful.

Owen: Mark is a fascinating character, because he had the humility as a scientist to look past the answers that were in the literature, which he had a hunch were all wrong from his personal experience, and ran at it. Every once in a while I’ll see a thing he says on Twitter, where he has a personality, and it kind of wins. It can seem a bit much, but he’s an evangelist. He cares that people suffer less, and he can’t not care. It’s a calling for him that he came by honestly.

In full disclosure, I’m actually an advisor to his company, Outro Health, and good friends with his co-founder Brandon as well. We were just on the phone yesterday talking about how, to your point, we’ll be questioning why we ever did this with SSRIs. We studied them for six weeks and then prescribed them for 20 years. And there’s not a lot of evidence guiding that practice.

It also raises a question, as a closing thought, about how TMS is different. You said it added something. It didn’t just take away negative feelings, it adds positive feelings for some people. Is there anything else that’s meaningfully different that you’d want to notify people about?

Randy: It’s the absence of side effects, and there are no withdrawal effects. Patients can finish their TMS and they don’t go through withdrawal. The side effects are mild local site sensitivity and headache. There’s a loud click, so you have to wear hearing protection. There’s a rare risk of seizure. We’ve done probably a hundred and twenty-one thousand treatments, and the one person who had a seizure, it wasn’t due to TMS, they had stopped their Xanax. Now that we know the safety guidelines, it’s very unlikely to happen. Universally, patients tolerate the procedure very, very well.

In fact, people often ask, since we do more of the standard treatment, not the accelerated, “How do you get people to come for five or six weeks?” They like to come. They love our techs. “It feels like my spa day. It’s my time away.” I actually call it a mini day program. Patients feel supported and cared about. A lot of our technicians are pre-PA, pre-med, pre-nursing, so they get their time in with the patients. They really care. They’re very passionate about helping our patients. So all in all, it’s a win-win.

Owen: How do the readers find your group?

Randy: It’s clearwavementalhealth.com. You can find us on the web. And facttms.org if you want to donate to the Foundation for the Advancement of Clinical TMS. We provide patient assistance, so if somebody can’t afford to come, we will provide patient assistance. We also provide grants in psychiatry and neurology to support original research.

We’re looking at increasing awareness. We’re going to be redoing our website, having podcasts, having social events, and having our free webinars. I often say the difference between the Foundation for the Advancement of Clinical TMS and the Clinical TMS Society is that the foundation is the heart, and the Clinical TMS Society is the brain. Our heart is that we really want to focus on the patient: helping patients access TMS, becoming aware of TMS, helping families, and supporting groundbreaking research.

Owen: So there’s a pretty simple call to action. If you’ve heard Randy talk and you want more people to be able to get access to this, a donation to the Foundation for the Advancement of Clinical TMS means more people get access to TMS treatment at a lower cost to them, and there’ll be more research supporting the next person who needs it, so that their doctor can answer the question of whether this is right for them.

Randy: Absolutely. Well said. Thank you.
