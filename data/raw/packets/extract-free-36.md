I know what you're thinking. Because I'm a psychiatrist, and we're psychic. That's a joke. Readers of this newsletter anxiously await the next regulatory issue, on which I will reliably advocate for compliance or public comment to regulators.

Subscribe now

Today, the fans of “frontier-psychiatrists-brand public comment ” are the winners. We have a real doozy on tap. You have the opportunity to make a big change. Psychiatric and general medical care in hospitals are paid for in completely different ways. In fact, there are two different payment models for psychiatric hospitals on the financial backend. Which contract the hospital has tells you a lot about the incentives they have to deploy novel treatments.

In every other medical condition, the payment model follows the same basic principles: you may get a hospital bill, and it's got a bunch of line items, and it's for a phenomenal amount of money, and then it gets knocked down to this or that. The mystery? This is not how any bills from Medicare or other payers are paid. The bill you see is fiction…if you have insurance. The song and dance are all there to obscure the fact that everything is paid differently. The first system—and to be clear, this is the better one— is the DRG system, which has its own associated contracts.

• Diagnosis-Related Group (DRG): Under the DRG system, patients are classified based on primary ICD-10 diagnostic code, surgical procedures, age, etc. Each DRG group has a fixed reimbursement rate. Hospitals receive a single payment per patient per DRG. This system “encourages efficiency and cost containment, 1 ” but at least it is internally consistent. It’s the prix fix menu approach to hospital payments.

So, for medical hospitalizations, they're getting a lump sum . The hospital has every incentive to take care of you in the most cost-efficient manner, and they get some adjustments to how much they get paid based on how much it's likely to cost. All of the negotiations are around the multiplier the hospital gets for the management of any given DRG in their setting. These multipliers for any DRG payment are considered proprietary by third-party payers. 2

Most psychiatric hospitals don't work this way. There are two different models with two different fee structures:

• Per Diem : Under the per diem system, hospitals receive a daily rate for each day a patient is in the facility. This rate covers care costs, including room and board, nursing care, and other services. They get paid more for longer stays, and the risk is that insurance will stop paying on individuals the doctors don’t feel safe discharging, and the hospital has to eat the cost.

• Inpatient Psychiatric Facility Prospective Payment System ( IPF PPS ): The IPF PPS is a Medicare payment system specifically designed for inpatient psychiatric facilities. Similar to the DRG system, it determines payment rates based on a patient's diagnosis, age, and other factors.

There was no financial mechanism to pay for innovative treatment in psychiatric hospitals… until now.

General medical hospitals have a bunch of payments factored into the care of a heart failure patient. That care can involve MRI scanners, surgical interventions, Extracorporeal Membrane Oxygenation, and associated wizardry of modern medicine. If you come into the hospital with a heart attack, they factor in the cost of the cardiac catheterization lab that will save your life. If you need a heart transplant or a heart implant, that is DRG 001. This has a “relative weight” of 28.9651 , which is multiplied by a contracted rate, and according to Optum’s guide , has a base rate of $170,646.44 under Medicare.

Until the 2024 CMS update—which inspired this article—the separate billing schemes for psychiatry never imagined any technology being brought to inpatient psychiatric care. When I previously complained about the archaic nature of inpatient psychiatry, even the admittedly nerdy healthcare economics enthusiast author of this column did not know these were different systems from a payment standpoint, much less two separate systems.

The practical upshot? The whole game for hospitals is about getting the most financially viable care per patient. The healthcare payers had the incentive to deny days. Inpatient psychiatry was not motivated to get the person out the door quickly, efficiently, and well.

Enter New Technology Add-On Payments!

If you're reading this, you probably saw that coming! Is he about to mention transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS)? Is he going to mention accelerated TMS? Will there be a brain scan involved? Will Owen suggest Nolan Ryan Williams, M.D., receive a Nobel prize? 3

Dear readers, if you guessed yes, you have correctly identified my emotional conflict of interest. It is now time to support more breakthrough treatments paid for in more psychiatric hospitals. And encourage your local hospital to start incorporating breakthrough treatments into its behavioral health care pathways.

It's a fine time to tell the world that the SAINT neuromodulation system is commercially available. It's an excellent time for anybody to ask their health plan if this is covered under their plan in the outpatient setting? There are new Category III CPT Codes for that, after all.

In a hospital setting, if you're paid to keep people longer, it's hard to understand why you would use a treatment that would get them out faster. Especially if it costs more and you don't get paid more. This is why Per Diem contracting is so pernicious. It doesn't matter how good your intentions are if you're going to financially cripple, your hospital setting, or at least you're afraid you will; it's really hard to imagine you're gonna adopt some novel treatment that will reduce the number of paid days in your hospital. However, if your setting is paid a bundled amount, like every other hospital, stay because your hospital uses the IPF PPS contracting system, and there is an add-on payment to account for the cost of treatment like SAINT —the New Technology Add-on Payment system (NTAP) under medicare—it makes all the financial sense in the world to do the new thing. You get paid more. You probably get the patient out the door faster. That's better for them, and it's better for your hospital financially.

It's completely bonkers that rapid-acting treatments like SAINT are much easier to access in the outpatient setting than they are in the inpatient hospital setting. You should go to a hospital because you have a problem that's really severe, and hospitals can access even more technologies and treatments to keep you alive and get you well. But right now, you can get sent to an outpatient clinic, like Fermata in New York or Acacia Clinics in California, but not in most hospitals. That is beginning to change— MUSC, for example, has one of its hospital's first comercial Magnus Medical SAINT systems.

Psychedelic medicines are likely going to need to follow a similar path to payment in the hospital setting. We can all help this happen. Ask the question—in writing—if your plan covers fMRI-guided Accelerated Neuromodulation. Here are the CPT codes to inquire about—they go into effect on July 1st!

If you are near a hospital, ask if they have SAINT for their psychiatric inpatients. If they answer no, send them some fine articles on the topic. Make yourself a bit of a pest. There is no other way they will hear about it that you can count on as much what you are saying directly to them.

Hello world!

This is my debut post as a “frontier” psychiatrist and I’m going to write about the various costs that may befall a person with severe depression. I recently read a cost-effectiveness analysis that did good math on a fake scenario. If you’ve been reading this newsletter, you know that psychiatry doesn’t always follow evidence-based guidelines. And you also know that evidence-based guidelines aren’t always based on the best evidence. For policy-makers, sometimes it’s important to know what actually happens.

Over the past decade, I’ve worked in Silicon Valley as a psychiatrist—from inpatient to the emergency department to outpatient. I’ve worked at our county hospital and Stanford, and from a Federally Qualified Healthcare Center to my current private practice. For the sake of this article, I’ll imagine a patient struggling with depression in Silicon Valley and where she might go. Let me tell you a story about Sarah, a fictional composite character, and her journey.

(1) Sarah’s Very Bad (Health) Plan

Sarah is 23. She recently graduated college and then got a job at a startup! She moved to California. The startup offers equity and even a salary—but no benefits. She moves into a Hacker House in Silicon Valley, sharing a room with other scrappy young people making their way in the world. Hearing that such health insurance is important even for otherwise healthy people, she responsibly signs up for an exchange-based insurance plan (Covered California, a.k.a. an “Obamacare” plan) and gets to work.

Unfortunately, she begins to develop trouble with sleep, loses 10 lbs, has trouble working, and stops running (she was on the cross country team in college and loved it). After a while, she starts thinking—on repeat—about how hard life is and how it might be better to be dead. She realizes this might be depression.

She goes to her primary care ($30 co-pay / ~$150 to the plan), and he diagnoses her with moderate severity depression. He recommends therapy and medication. He prescribes Prozac for 2 months ($0 copay / $6 to the plan). She uses her now-limited energy to look for a few therapists who might be a good match and calls them. Some calls go to voicemail, and she never gets called back. The rest call back and say they don’t take her insurance. She decides she can’t afford private pay, gets discouraged, and stops looking. Had she more energy, she may have found a therapist.

The medicine doesn’t work. She goes back to her primary care ($30 co-pay / ~$150 to the plan), and he tries bupropion for 2 months ($0 copay / $10 to the plan). She’s 4 months in, $60 poorer, and no better off; the plan has spent $316. Each of the two medications had a good shot at working, but they happened not to work.

(2) The Hospital

Her startup fails to raise its next round of funding. They do layoffs. She gets the axe. Her depression worsens. By a lot. She starts thinking of ways she can kill herself. She has a harder time with basic tasks. She doesn’t have much in savings, and she needs a new job, but can’t manage to update her resume or apply.

She remembers a billboard she saw. “Hope has a new number.” She decides to call 988.

link

The person on the other line is warm and caring. She’s honest about how she’s been feeling. She keeps talking and is starting to feel a bit better. Then there’s a knock at her door. It’s the police. The 988 operator had acted according to training and discreetly dispatched the police to her location. She explains to the police she’s feeling a bit better and doesn’t want to go to the hospital. The police judge that she needs to be assessed at the nearest hospital. They handcuff her in front of her new friends and take her to the squad car (or depending on the city, the police make sure she is restrained in an ambulance).

She is hospitalized. This is where things get expensive . The first thing to note is that depending on where she is, the hospitalization may be dramatically more expensive. Numbers on this are notoriously difficult to find, but FAIR Health Consumer helps. In my story, I said she was in Silicon Valley. If she was in Palo Alto, the “average hospital” in that sleepy college town would keep her 15 days and bill her $298,617. I happen to know that the “average hospital” in Palo Alto doesn’t take her insurance. Let’s look at other towns in the San Francisco Bay Area that are within 45 minutes’ drive of Palo Alto:

On this first step of the acute-care journey, the first thing to note is that we’ve increased the cost by about 100x. We’re not talking about $3 / month meds anymore. All of this is extraordinarily expensive. But “extraordinarily expensive” needs to be quantified. Getting “caught” in Palo Alto with an out-of-network plan will result in a bill exceeding a quarter million dollars whereas other areas won’t crack six figures.

The other thing to note that is just bonkers (a technical term in the field of healthcare economics) is that for “the same thing” the price varies 20x. In Network vs Out of Network is a 3-10x increase, and driving 30 minutes in any direction also results in wildly variable prices. It’s also notable that neighboring psychiatrists have very different thresholds for how long to keep people: some as short as ~9 days on average; some for ~15.

My story illustrates how powerless and unpredictable the charge will be under the present system. If Sarah had her suicidal crisis when visiting her friend 30-minute-drive away in Fremont, she may have been admitted to an in-network hospital and been responsible for a bill that would be 1/20 th the price. Price transparency works when there is choice. Under the present system of psychiatric crisis, patients are not choosing.

One final thing to note is that Sarah may be responsible for $298,617 for a hospitalization she was coerced into that results in no actual effective treatment of her underlying condition that could not have been provided as an outpatient. As has been noted elsewhere on this newsletter, that’s a bad deal.

(3) – After the Hospital

Sarah is discharged after 8-15 days. Hospitalizations used to be much longer, but contemporary practice is to discharge sooner, after the patient stops saying she’s going to kill herself after leaving and after a “step-down” program is identified.

Usually post-hospital depressed patients are strongly encouraged to go to a Partial Hospitalization Program (PHPs) followed by an Intensive Outpatient Program (IOP). PHPs usually keep people for 4-6 weeks (5-days per week and ~20 hours / week); IOPs usually for 6-12 weeks (usually ~10-12 hours /week; commonly 3 days per week of PHP programming). The costs also vary and are even harder to get than inpatient costs. In our example area, I happen to know that $500 is a reasonable estimate, but estimates range $300 – $1,000 / day. If we compare the lower end of these ranges (4 week PHP, 6 week IOP, low cost of both), with the middle (5 week PHP, 8 week IOP, typical cost), and with High (6 week PHP, 12 week IOP, high cost) we get this:

Another 5-fold spread on the possible cost of after-care. This one is at least in theory selectable by the patient or steer-able by the plan in a world with price transparency. Depending on where the patient was hospitalized, the costs at this phase might exceed the hospital cost itself. The benefit of PHP and IOP is also a matter of debate.

An Alternative

Imagine, if you will, we had effective and rapid-acting antidepressants. Because you subscribe to this newsletter, you don’t need to imagine. They’re here. And as of last week, our office at Acacia treated the very first patient in the world with the commercial Magnus Medical SAINT Device, the first FDA-cleared accelerated, neuronavigated TMS treatment commercially available (we’ve been doing it off-label for some time now, too).

The prices of accelerated TMS are still in flux, but even at the most luxury of clinics, I’ve never even heard of a price as high as a typical hospitalization + post-hospital course in our area.

I recently had a patient that had 4 hospitalizations in as many months and was in a PHP continuously when not in the hospital. We did accelerated, neuro-navigated TMS. She went into remission and stopped going to the hospital.

This is the way of the future.

As a society, we’re in an awkward in-between. We’ve committed to paying for emergency care, but don’t actually have any. Care, that is. Fear of the EMTALA cudgel (now strengthened by recent Federal pronouncement ) makes emergency departments, hospitals, and plans very eager to pretend to do things. But they’re just pretending because access to the effective treatment is all still outpatient. So let’s stop pretending to do something now that we have things we can do.

The role of accelerated / neuro-navigated TMS in stable outpatients may be debated as it is indeed expensive and $3 / month Prozac is hard to beat when it’s working. But for patients that are nearing hospitalization, better and more effective treatment is the right thing to do. And, if there are any who don’t care about the right thing to do, it also saves money.

—David Carreon, M.D.

As a psychiatrist, people talk to me about their sleep…often. They want something to help. You can even buy over-the-counter sleep aids! Usually, these are antihistamines. They must be safe! They are over the counter!

Subscribe now

Define… safe?

Usually, we think of acute safety—will this drug kill me now? And even though enough antihistamines will plausibly kill you, the lethal dose is quite high. And add TikTok Challenges, and you get FDA warnings:

FDA is warning that taking higher than recommended doses of the common over-the-counter (OTC) allergy medicine Benadryl (diphenhydramine) can lead to serious heart problems, seizures, coma, or even death. FDA is aware of news reports of teenagers ending up in emergency rooms or dying after participating in the “Benadryl Challenge” encouraged in videos posted on the social media application TikTok.

FDA is investigating these reports and conducting a review to determine if additional cases have been reported.

However, there are slower ways to die, and the most important is the slide from obesity to metabolic syndrome to diabetes to end-stage organ damage to dialysis to death. Today, I’ll point out that “trouble sleeping” is a problem that leads to obesity.

The 2005–2006 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) trial has appeared in prior writing of mine, but today, we will look at what this data tells us about antihistamines 1 .

Prescription h1 antihistamine use

Ok, so that is very significant…but it’s not randomized, and it's retrospective, not prospective. It could be that the underlying sleep problem is the driver of both the weight changes and the prescription of the antihistamines. Adults are messy—surely, children treated with antihistamines don’t have the same catastrophic weight gain! Oh, wait 2 …they do:

Yes, if they are Hispanic youth, as studied in the above paper, they do. We have male participants getting, on average, 0.44 Standard Deviations more BMI and girls gaining 0.13 Standard Deviations. That is effect size, folks; you’ve seen it before in this newsletter. That translates to an 11.42% BMI increase per year…in children—in whom it’s harder to blame it on a desk job.

The bottom line is these medicines were approved based on short-term data; thus, phase IV data is almost inherently less likely to be randomized data, and we need other study designs to make sense of it!

Short sleep duration on its own is related to obesity in a variety of study designs 3 .

And we know from hit articles like “Bad Sleep:: -1” that poor sleep is a killer both here and now (especially considering nightmares)…

We have a bit of a bind… too little sleep will kill you, either through obesity or suicide, and make your ADHD worse in the meantime. Further, even not-antihistamine drugs like Ambien will also kill you. What will help you sleep better?!?!

It’s not about drugs, usually. We had Pear’s Somyrst , but market forces blew that up. We still have CBT-I, and the free VA CBT-i Coach app is a good place to start.

If a person has PTSD, there are an effective treatment—Prazosin— for their disrupted sleep, including, as recently highlighted by my friend Chris Aiken, M.D. :

99 RCTs with 10,481 participants were included. Prazosin may be the most effective treatment for insomnia (SMD = -0.88) nightmares (SMD = -0.44) and poor sleep quality (SMD = -0.55). Evidence is scarce and indicates lack of efficacy for SSRIs, Mirtazapine, z-drugs and benzodiazepines, which are widely used in daily practice. The components of greatest improvement were Sleep Latency ( P = .018, d = 0.71) and Sleep Disturbances ( P = .030, d = 0.66). 6

Let’s take sleep seriously, and the obesity poor sleep—and risky sleep medicines— can drive. Oral antihistamines are a risky option for long-term insomnia, and short-term use often leads to long-term use.

This newsletter has delved deep into FDA filings before…today, a brief overview of the FDA’s therapeutic approval process.

Drugs are submitted to the FDA for approval, with a specific label for the drug's intended use (for example, to treat a disease in a population). This label is crucial because it also limits what marketing is legal! For example, the claim for an imagined chemical that happens to be “water” might be: “for the treatment of mild to moderate dehydration.”

Phase 1

Phase one trials include human health controls to make sure the drug is safe for humans. It also allows for “signal” to design future trials. If we imagined “water” trials—going after a dehydration treatment label—it would be given to healthy people who very much were not suffering from dehydration. We might notice that individuals urinated more, but the “Adverse Effect” was dose-related.

Phase 2

In phase two, drugs are given in various dosages to individuals with the disorder in question. It moves from humans without the illness to humans with the illness the drug is intended to treat. Again, using our imagined drug, “water,” we might give 1oz, 8oz, 16oz, 32oz, and 128oz in the phase two trial. Thirst-reduction was noted in individuals given 8oz and 16oz. Still, no effect was noted in the 1oz group, and excessive urination and abdominal distention were noted, along with slight changes in blood levels of sodium in the 128oz group.

On flight of fancy, let’s imaging what these phase two trials might show? Having been dehydrated at baseline, the 16oz and 32oz dose groups might note a decrease in heart rate, and blood pressure might improve. It might sound trivial—we all know what water does! But you can imagine for any “new” drug—or new indication—we would want to be very sure it was safe before we let just any idiot with a medical degree prescribe the drugs for whatever they want.

A Note On Off-Label Prescribing

Recall—once a drug is approved, it can be prescribed “off-label,” because we expect those with licenses to prescribe to shoulder the task and liability of deciding what is medically appropriate. The bulwark work against this, in part, is risk evaluation and mitigation strategies , a.k.a. REMS programs. These feature mandatory monitoring for safety forn these new drugs. The other “blocker” for unintended use off label can be the exorbitant cost of new medications. The average cost of a new drug in 2023 was $300,000 a year per person. That is the AVERAGE. So, although it may be that physicians might want to prescribe some thing new for an off label indication, its becomes implausible for many patients due to the cost.

Phase 3

This lets investigators move on to phase 3—pivotal trials! In phase three trials—which need to be replicated to reduce the chance that a “fluke” in one study becomes an approved drug. The P value of 0.05 accepts 5% risk that the results are due to chance. We replicate trials to reduce this risk to 0.25%. Let’s zoom in on the studies to show how this plays out:

Thus, the FDA is willing, with medications, to accept no more than a 0.25% chance that the outcomes are due only to chance and there is no “real effect” of the drug on the condition.

The FDA also wants to know what the proposed mechanism of action is—and although it has to be plausible, they are not in the position of correcting the Study Sponsor (a.k.a. drug company) about their proposed label. When Lykos (formerly MAPS) submitted MDMA-AT to the FDA, their proposed mechanism was “MDMA potentiates the effects of therapy for PTSD” and thus, based on that understanding of the mechanism of action, there would be no reason to ask about what MDMA does for PTSD on it’s own. This does not have to be the same as the ACTUAL MECHANISM OF ACTION. Plenty of treatments don’t end up working as we would expect. The FDA isn’t in the business of dispassionate scientific inquiry—they are in the business of regulating drug claims and marketing, after gatekeeping to make sure completely bonkers or dangerous things don’t get to the unsuspecting public, willy-nilly.

In the case of water, we’d have to choose the primary end point—treats dehydration as measured by…

This choice of primary end point can end up mattering quite a bit in your eventual drug label. The oral version of semeglutide, for example, has this FDA label:

RYBELSUS is indicated as an adjunct to diet and exercise to improve glycemic control in adults with type 2 diabetes mellitus (DM2)

Which means they can advertise its impact on a blood value—HbA1c—but not that it help individuals lose weight also. That weight loss claim, which NovoNordisc can make about Wegovy—the weight loss labeled version of the drug—is also true enough as an outcome in individuals treated with Ozempic (for DM2) and Rybelsus (for DM2). However, the claims the manufacturer can make are profoundly limited by the label the FDA grants. To advertise past the label? That is a crime, as I have written about previously (for example, as it relates to Abilify).

In our imagined phase III approval trials for water, we might imagine it improves saliva production and reduces drymouth in those with dehydration—maybe the study subjects will drink water, and then spit, and the amount of spit is the primary endpoint?

“Water—an effective treatment for drymouth associated with clincal dehydration.”

One could not legally call it refreshing , or lifesaving , or any of the things that might also be true. Because the FDA Label is the difference between legal advertising and physician updating by Medical Science Liaisons (a.k.a. Pharma Reps about the science of Water for drymouth in those with clincal dehydration) that is legal, and criminal behavior. What we measure in the phase III trial limits what can go on the label for the drug, and what we chose to claim needs to be backed up by science and outcomes from clinical trials, which in turn limits the ability of industry to advertise for their compounds in medical use.

And yes, Dorothy, there is a very complex manual to train raters to develop a system that reliably uses such a complex tool. Here is a brief sample of how specific adherence manual is about how things are rated:

Skill level is not the same as effectiveness of intervention. A clinician may make an intervention at a high level of skill but with little effect.

If a clinician engages in interventions with sufficient frequency or extensiveness to rate the domain but the rater thinks more of the items/interventions should have been used, this is rated here.

The rater:

a) Notes the failure to deliver a range of items/interventions when working in a domain. Should more of the items appropriate to the domain have been used?
b) Notes the failure to appropriately stop delivering interventions, for example, due to increasing arousal because of the intervention.
b) Considers that an item/intervention could have been used more.
c) Decides whether how the interventions were delivered was neutral, negative or positive.

The HOW delivery of interventions/items is scored as follows:

+1.0 The intervention is used at a level to maintain clinical interaction and therapeutic alliance. Context and content are respected in terms of responding to the patient. Timing is appropriate. Multiple items used flexibly within the domain. Avoidance if necessary of interventions leading to excessive anxiety.

OR Uses active avoidance of intervention – for example, when the patient is manifestly within relational mentalizing process but, because of high emotional intensity, this work is reduced (‘Perhaps this is not the time for us to go into that.’ The clinician then moves the conversation to reduce anxiety).

+0.5 Timing is appropriate and context respected but hesitant delivery.
