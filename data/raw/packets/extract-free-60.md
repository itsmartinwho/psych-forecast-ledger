This is the final part of a story written by Jon Rosenberg, an author who your editor, Owen Scott Muir, M.D., DFAACAP, met in the context of running a clinical trial site for a multicenter FDA-approval trial. I will do a summary of Part I and Part II , to catch everyone up:

Jon’s battle with depression began in early 2007 while he was a student at Rutgers, though in reality, he had already been quietly suffering throughout most of high school. When the depression took hold, it was unlike anything before—a collapse of motivation, joy, and identity, marked by a level of emotional pain that defied easy explanation. Over the years, he endured multiple episodes, each one deepening his understanding of how isolating and destructive depression could be, even as he tried everything—medications, therapy, substances, and denial—to survive. From heavy drinking to self-medicating with marijuana, from strained social interactions to long stretches of isolation, he stumbled through college and young adulthood, chasing fleeting moments of relief. Every recovery was followed by relapse, and each relapse brought new, often brutal, lessons about the nature of his illness. After a devastating episode in 2011 that lasted nearly a year, he had no choice but to face the illness head-on—and gave a call to get screened for a clinical trial.

Anyway, the only upside to my chosen lifestyle was that depression did stay away for much of 2012-2018, with the exception of a minor to moderate episode that I already mentioned. And for someone like me, that was not something I expected or took for granted. However, In the early months of 2018 I began to feel the symptoms of moderate depression, and it coincided with the news that my boss was selling the restaurant to new owners and our jobs were not safe any longer. Besides for feeling down and pessimistic about the future, our boss really blindsided us with the quickness of his sale and exit. On top of that my boss underpaid me for my minimal severance payment and that was that with the only job I held for more than a year in my life.

It was during this period I began to walk three miles around my town for exercise, boredom, and mood improvement. Though it was helpful to get out of my room and get outside, the walking did not offer any miraculous benefit to treating my depression at that time. I pretty much suffered through most of 2018 with moderate depression, increasing anxiety, and less Interaction with the world. I went to my hockey games, but I missed a few more than usual. Sometimes, I cried before games, and though a few teammates seemed to notice by the way they looked at me, they did not say anything. My new doctor first raised the dose of Lamictal, but when that did not help, he took me off of it, and even years later, I still think that wasn’t one of his better moves for me. By the fall of 2018, I felt ready to try to work again, even though I was still feeling the weight of the depressive symptoms. Fortunately, one of my hockey teammates owned a company, and he offered me a position in the warehouse, which I was grateful to get. I worked through the fall into the winter, and by early 2019, my anxiety began to really get out of control. I felt nervous whenever we had a company meeting with only about 5-6 people. Still, I was anticipating a question or comment directed at me, and occasionally, when one question or comment was directed at me, I remember my face turning red and feeling completely overwhelmed and uncomfortable. Despite my history with depression, I was not ready for how big of a toll anxiety would take on me in 2019. In retrospect, I believe my weed habit played a big role. Once my company hired a few more workers, it got even worse. And I remember some days having to prepare myself and brace for my awkward “goodbye, see you tomorrow” to each coworker I passed by on my way out, which was done mostly with a red face and a fast beating heart. It was very difficult throughout the year to get myself to feel comfortable at work. I begged my psychiatrist for any anxiety medication, and I was only offered Buspar, which did nothing.

By the fall of 2019, the prodromal symptoms of depression began to be felt; the numbness, the isolation, and the distance I felt from my coworkers and environment made me feel lost, distracted, and stressed. I tried CBD oil, and it did nothing for the depression or anxiety. I battled as hard as I could to hold it together, but just the day after my birthday on December 8th, I did not report to work, and a few days later, I texted my boss and told him I was struggling with depression and I needed to take a leave of absence. Thankfully, he is a wonderful man, and he wished me the best while offering me a job when I got better.

One of the toughest parts of suffering from depression while occupied is that you disappear from your coworkers, and you still sense the social camaraderie that you are missing out on, and it hurts deeply. I tried my best not to think about the music my coworkers were listening to, the jokes being made, the satisfying feeling they had of earning money and improving their situation one day at a time. Since I fell apart in December, I thought about the Christmas spirit, the holiday parties, and all the things that added to my feeling of isolation and being too unwell to be with them. That’s where suffering a moderate to severe depression becomes a test of will to survive. Because if you let yourself think about the social environment you were just in, it destabilizes you further, and you wanna scream at how unfair it is to have such a terrible form of this illness. For me, when I got depressed in 2011 and again in late 2019, I tried my best to just focus on being home, forgetting my coworkers and job, and trying to heal as fast as I can. During this period of depression, I was very down, as usual. I drank a lot of green tea and ate a lot of dark chocolate, two things that were supposed to help depression ever slightly, and I walked many miles around my town trying to push my healing forward. I also finally returned to therapy, which I probably should have done years earlier.

Unbelievably, my very first session with my therapist literally went over three hours. When I left the office, I could not believe it was after 10 pm on that gloomy early January Friday night. I checked my phone twice to make sure it was after 10 pm. I was very grateful that the therapist, David, was patient enough and willing to give me the chance to try to release my demons, especially in the first session. I kept walking miles around town, and I was very proactive in this depression to make my way back to work where I felt I belonged. After grinding through the familiar feelings of despair, loneliness, shame, and a desire to once again feel healthy, I kept walking no matter how lethargic and unmotivated I often felt.

Finally, in late February, I returned about 10 weeks after I broke down and left my job. Though I was not 100% asymptomatic, I felt stable enough to give it another shot. Little did I know that just a week or two after I returned, rumors of a virus named COVID-19 would shape the year to come. Unlike half of my coworkers, I decided to stay at work and work through the pandemic and not take the boss’s offer to leave and return when I chose. In March, when my roller hockey league was cancelled indefinitely, I had nothing going on in my life except my job and my marijuana habit. I still had problems with Anxiety, and I started taking Ativan. It helped reduce some of my anxiety, so I was grateful for that. I also started taking experimental supplements for Depression like NAC and probiotics. I watched lectures on depression from the NIH and other institutions and doctors on YouTube. When I heard the names of possible experimental treatments, I took notes and figured I had nothing to lose by trying them. I even tried aspirin for a couple of months because there is a theory that inflammation plays a role in some subtypes of depression. But it did nothing for me. Despite these attempts, my mild depression didn’t get any better. I wasn’t stuck in a severe depressive state, but I didn’t feel content. I was grateful, though, that I returned to my job, and my life had normalcy again. I could only do phone sessions with David, which helped stabilize me and keep me going for a while.

Unfortunately, however, just seven months into my recovery from Depression, I slipped into a heavy one in the middle of September 2020. And this one struck hard and fast. This depression or relapse came on quickly. I felt mostly ok leaving work on Friday. By Monday, I could not get out of bed. I hoped it was a fluke and just called out sick like it was a one-day thing. But by Tuesday morning, I knew in my heart I was not only entering a severe depression, but the anxiety was high, the panic was high, and the pain level was intense from the get-go. Once again, I was pulled away from my coworkers, routine, income, and any normalcy in life. This depression did not have the normal prodromal phase; it went from feeling average to feeling outright suffering. In a panic on Tuesday, I called my psychiatrist and begged him to put me on lithium because I read it had a moderately good success rate for some types of depression. My doctor was surprised I asked for it, but he obliged.

A few days later, I texted my boss and told him I was relapsing and would need to leave work again. My boss was just as kind as he was the first time. He told me to get well and come back when I was ready.

A month or two later, when my hockey team, which I played on for eight years, was getting ready to return to action after the COVID hiatus, I chain texted the team and informed them I was going through depression and that as much as I loved playing hockey, I just could not be there at this time. The response was very positive, and I was glad I texted them, though in retrospect, I think texting each guy individually would have been better. Even though I was suffering, it felt good to know I had people in my corner.

Despite that, my level of suffering was high. I tried ketamine once more at a clinic close to my home. But this time, not only did it not have the same effect even for the 45 minutes in its grips, but I threw up as soon as the ketamine began to leave my system. So I gave up on it after that day. To say that I suffered in October and November is an understatement. I woke up in horror at the long day ahead, and I tried to watch old movies that might distract me for a few minutes. The suicidal ideation that I felt in my depression in college and again in 2011 was as strong as ever. Every day was a horror, waking up early at seven a.m., putting on the same movies, and burying my face in my pillow, wishing the pain would go away. My poor mother did not know what to do, but like many other times in my depressions, she said she would take away my pain if she could. And I very much appreciated that sentiment. There isn’t anything another person can say when you’re in depression to make you feel better. But there are things someone can say that could make it worse. A few days into my depression, instead of commenting on my speedily declining health, my father said, “Is your boss ok with you missing work like this?”

My father was the son of a WW2 vet, so not many things emotionally impact him. Though my father did not show too much concern through most of my depressions, the truth is, even if he would have, it would not have taken away the constant pain of being in a severe depressive state anyway. My mother was much more empathetic to my suffering. And when she saw me unshaven with a two-week-old beard instead of my normal, Clean shaven appearance, she almost broke down crying.

During October and November, I ate very little, I stopped showering and shaving, and I looked as bad as I felt. I was suffering terribly. At this point, my psychiatrist recommended that I admit myself to Mount Sinai or a hospital that had experimental treatments for severe depression. I did not want to go to a hospital, so despite my pain, I ignored my doctor’s advice.

By early December, my suicidal ideation was consuming me. I did not have any plan, but I just fantasized about dying naturally. Then, I tried something no doctor would recommend, but nevertheless, I felt I was running out of time. I read something about gabapentin as an “off label” medication for bipolar or anxiety, and I knew my father was prescribed it. So one day in December, when I felt I was really close to either admitting myself to a hospital or worse, I took two gabapentin capsules, and just ever so slightly, I was able to cry, text my sister, and have a little relief from the cauldron I was in. I tried it again the next day, and sure enough, for just a couple of hours, the psychological pain went down from 9 out of 10 to about 5 out of 10, and I knew maybe I had a chance to survive this. I called my doctor and begged him to prescribe it to me, at least just to try, and I’m grateful to this day that he was willing to try. From this point, my depression was subdued in the morning and early afternoon when I took the gabapentin, but in the late afternoon and evening, my depression returned and was still pretty heavy. But at least now I had hope. I had some time to breathe in this depression and not suffocate for 24 hours a day. Gabapentin was no cure, but for me, it was still a miracle. Any amount of relief from severe depression, one hour, two hours, or three hours, was critically important to instilling hope in me. Gabapentin gave me hope that I could hold on until this depression faded, and eventually, it did, sometime in the middle of 2021, at least, mostly.

Despite feeling somewhat better in mid-2021, I did not return to work. I did not feel totally secure in my recovery because I still carried a lot of pain that mostly presented itself to me in the second half of the day. I also thought maybe the casual party environment of my job with its 20-something-year-old coworkers just wasn’t a good place for me to fully recover, so I never came back.

Regrettably, I never returned to my hockey team after eight years, three championships ,over 400 games, and many good memories. My excuse was that the team did not even save a reserved spot for me, which I thought was wrong, but the truth was that I was not only a little embarrassed by my depression, but I was underwhelmed by the emotional support I got from the team after that first night I texted them. In the weeks and months that followed, the team seemed to move on from me, and I thought I needed to do the same.

The following year or so was uneventful. I did not have a job, and I was not playing hockey, but I was helpful living with my parents, driving them to doctor appointments, picking up dinner, cleaning, and doing the best I could to at least contribute to the house. It’s fair to say that after this latest depression, I limped along.
Unfortunately, in late 2022, I had yet another major episode, which I believe was my 9th. It started more traditionally than the last one, and I felt the symptoms come on progressively in October and November.

By December, I mostly stayed in my room, watching Netflix, but with the same preoccupied, unfocused haze I had in my previous episodes. I managed to take my mother to the airport to visit my sister in Canada for two weeks, and emotionally, I knew it would be tougher for me during that period. Shopping and making dinner or picking up dinner for my father when I was steadily declining was not an easy task. I got through December, but January was even worse. I started to have bad insomnia, and I was really down, and I still tried to keep up with my responsibility to drive my father to the doctor and hospital for his various health issues. Some days I felt I could barely stand, I was so depressed and lethargic, but what little drive in me I had left, I did it for my parents. The suicidal ideation was very strong that late winter of 2022 into 2023. I imagined myself jumping in front of a train or jumping off the palisades cliffs, anything that felt like a way out from this depression.

The only thing I could focus on reading at this time was about depression and psychiatry. I did a lot of reading on depression and anti-depressants, and I came across an Australian doctor, Dr. Ken Gilman, who recommended the MAOI antidepressant Parnate for severe depression. His confidence that it would likely work instilled hope in me at a time when I did not have any. I mentioned what I read to my psychiatrist, and he reluctantly agreed to start me on Parnate, though we titrated up painfully slow. He warned me of the dietary restrictions with taking an MAOI, I was willing to give it a try nonetheless. Slowly but surely, I was feeling a little better after a couple of months. I had some symptoms of depression still, but I was able to leave my room, reconnect with my friends, and try to rebuild my life slowly. Parnate did not work as miraculously fast as Cymbalta or Lamictal once did, but over time, it definitely helped push me out of the worst of my most recent depression. As time went on, my doctor added a few more meds to augment the Parnate, like Lithium and Latuda, and I gradually started to feel a little better. I was not cured, but I would say about 60 % of the severity of my Depression was decreased. Slowly but surely, I got through the rest of 2023 without a job but at least being productive and helpful in caring for my parents. In late 2023, my nephews even came to visit me, and I think, despite my symptoms, we had a great time. I took them to get the autographs of the New York Rangers, an old hobby of mine from my youth, and my nephews took to it. Experiencing once more the excitement of meeting some of the professional hockey players we all watched on TV was good for my soul, too. It brought me back to a time in my life before depression made its unwelcome entrance. Sharing that with my nephews was cool, and it began their burgeoning interest in collecting autographs.

In late 2023, I read about the miraculous Saint TMS trials from Stanford under the work of Dr. Nolan Williams, and I emailed a doctor whose name came up as a possible provider on the East Coast. His name was Dr. Owen Muir. Mere minutes after I emailed him inquiring about the study, he called me. We spoke for a few minutes, and he told me about the possibility of a free research study sometime in late 2024, but I might consider trying the regular TMS procedure in the meantime 1 .

I took Dr. Muir’s advice, and in January of 2024, I began a 6-week course of Trans transcranial magnetic Stimulation to resolve the remaining symptoms that carried over from my previous depression. I went in five days a week to an office in my hometown and did the 20-minute procedure. I’m not going to lie, it hurt my scalp a little at first but, thankfully, the pain subsided after a few days. By the end of the treatment, after six weeks, I felt slightly better but not cured of Depression. But the treatment motivated me to return to my old habit of taking long walks, which I stopped in 2023. I was also motivated to exercise with weights again and, for that, I am very grateful. I feel like the regular TMS gave me a push to start doing things on my own that would help my depression and I have no doubt taking my 3-mile walks really helps as well as exercising with weights. In the summer, I finally went back to the beach for the first time in many years, and it felt good to reconnect with the positive experiences of my past. Despite the positives, though, I still felt that I was carrying unresolved symptoms. At night primarily, I felt lonely and slightly down. I still did not have the confidence in myself to start working again. I had a lot of nostalgia and longing for the past before my life took the turn it did starting in 2007. I still mostly remained distant from my friends though at least I kept in contact with them. I was able to do the things I needed to do to help my parents, but I was still far from 100%.

Finally, in September of 2024, Dr. Muir’s office was ready to start an Accelerated TMS study, and I was ecstatic to be a participant. I came in two or three times a week for most of the day while having five separate treatments of intense magnetic stimulation directed at my dorsolateral prefrontal cortex for about ten minutes. Amid the two-week period of multiple sessions 2 , I honestly felt my mood improve to a level I had not had in a long while, but as the study tapered down to fewer treatments, I felt a slight drop off. Nevertheless, by the end of treatment, I did feel better than when the treatment started. By the end of the study, I finally felt confident to apply to jobs once more and try to resume my life.

For me, the year 2024 was a year where I felt fortunate to try some of the cutting-edge treatments now available for people with depression. And the aggregate effect of my meds, plus the regular TMS, plus the Accelerated TMS helped heal me from the terrible cluster of depressions that struck me from 2018 to 2023. I am not naive. I know there is a strong likelihood that there may be another depression just around the bend. But I tell myself, you can’t live in fear. You have to move forward and hope for the best, I can now say to myself and others. At the same time, it helps to know that every day, some groundbreaking research on depression is being done by doctors like Dr. Muir and Dr. Nolan Williams. I have more confidence now than ever before that if depression should strike again, there are many more options available to me now than when I first met this terrible illness in college in the late winter of 2007.

One of the real, honest-to-goodness delights in this life is meeting people with whom you really feel a connection. Many doctors spend significant time being very skeptical of business and finance. There are exceptionally good reasons for this. We're not trying to understand any of these things, and it seems like decisions happen beyond our control regularly. There are entire schools for business where a whole cohort of individuals learn to speak in the secret code. There are similarly such institutions for medicine. Ordinary people are kind of left out of the whole thing. We don't quite understand the details, or even the broad strokes of why money moves the way it does, even though it impacts our lives tremendously. A few years back, I had the utmost pleasure of meeting an executive at a health technology company with whom I just hit it off. To my surprise, it wasn't the chief medical officer, it was their chief financial officer. The money guy. The guy who had to put up with the headaches of accounting. Spreadsheets! Not the kind of thing that I usually like, not the kind of person I'm usually hanging out with. However, like all friendships, when you get along with someone, you want to spend more time with them, and when you feel that someone understands you, you are more motivated to understand them.

Kyle McAndrews was, at the time, the Chief Financial Officer of a company called Wave Neuroscience . They were working on a treatment that combined EEG and TMS, but he has moved on from there since then. He now lives in Manila, in the Philippines, with his family. He is endlessly curious and hilarious. We decided to do an asynchronous interview, and this is the first part, perhaps of many, of that conversation. He's done work in the frequently piloried field of private equity and worked in the kinds of companies that I am frequently praying will succeed in biotech. I hope you enjoy the conversation!

A photo of Owen looking at the fundraising environment, circa 2025.

I started by asking Kyle some questions.

OM: What interested you, as “a finance guy,” in biotechnology?

KM: A bunch of reasons! Healthcare is about 15-20% of the US economy, so it’s a big market with many opportunities. It’s a relatively steady “secular grower,” with fewer booms and busts than other cyclical or consumer-sensitive industries. This was became appealing to me, as someone who worked with a variety of industries, in the tumult of the Great Recession.

The industry is most financially attached—we say “levered” in finance— to the US, geo-politically. I keep an eye on the US anyway. This frees me from having to watch things all political developments all over the globe! If I were an Energy investor, I’d have to follow the goings on in all OPEC nations— across multiple continents —to have a view on “demand.” That is a lot of work. It’s also an industry that is regulated. Some investors dislike the ‘stroke of the pen’ risk—where one law can blow up a whole industry—but less competition means greater opportunities, and that risk is controllable. You can do well while also doing good for the world. Additionally, MDs and PHDs are my kind of quirky, and their technical gifts can combine with my business and finance experience to create something greater than we could by ourselves.

OM: What mistakes do you see early-stage biotechnology companies making?

KM: “Focus” might be the greatest challenge they face. If you’ve raised capital to start an early-stage biotech company, you’re confident the FDA will find it safe and effective, and the markets will pay for it (handsomely). Plus, by definition, it’s new, with many unanswered questions and potential future use cases beyond the immediate preclinical or pilot studies. The current fundraising environment is difficult, with investor confidence hinging on management’s tight grip of budget and spending requirements and delivering against development milestones. Diluting resources and management attention to parallel track additional trials, use cases, or foundational research narrows the margin of error when inevitable setbacks or delays present.

OM: How about later-stage companies?

KM : Later-stage companies might be questioned about executing the risk-adjusted bet of selling to a strategic partner vs. trying to commercialize themselves. Indeed, making the mistake of commercializing a product, perhaps as “a technology-enabled service,” but also raising money to have enough to complete your clinical studies is inviting havoc. This is the equivalent of trying to run two businesses simultaneously out of one bank account, and it’s almost impossible to get right.

OM: Any Advice for All Companies?

In all stage of company, governance—how you run your company—can break otherwise promising opportunities. Governance can be simplified into the selection & evolution of CEO and BOD members. Together, that group is responsible for attracting investor capital and delivering risk-adjusted returns to shareholders by managing the direction of the business and, ultimately, some liquidity event—a sale or acquisition. The CEO and BOD are also a bit of a mutual pairing: the CEO, if he/she can attract high(er) quality investors, can cycle out low(er) quality board members. Conversely, the BOD generally represents the interests of the larger shareholders; if they can attract a higher quality CEO with stronger ties to higher quality talent, customers, and investors, they should! …And if they can’t, they’re stuck.

OM: What is are the worst idea you’ve seen get funded? How about the best idea that couldn't get funded?

KM: For deals specific to me:

My former employer bought BYTE, a DIY at-home plastic clear aligner business, for $1.04B in January 2021, and in October 2024 shut the entire company down voluntarily when it turns out patients need to see dentists before using aligners so their teeth don’t fall out mid-case:

Dentsply Sirona voluntarily suspends sales of its Byte teeth aligners | Reuters

A deal we skipped, blessedly:

Sonendo (stock) was a hard pass and notable dog, <$1M market cap, after having raised $50M+ from august institutional growth investors such as General Atlantic.

Deal we skipped and wished we hadn’t #1:

There was a really tidy dental practice management software, Two Ten Health, which wasn’t purchased for a modest price out of hubris.

Skipped & wished we hadn’t #2:

Dentsply Sirona skipped purchasing DESS, sold to a European fund Miura Partners Sells Terrats Medical to Avista Capital Partners - Miura Partners , and then proceeded to flip it again to Avista Partners a few years later: Avista Capital Partners Acquires Terrats Medical from Miura Partners - Avista Healthcare .

I’m happy for the Terrats family though; Roger and his brothers have built a lovely business and continue to live well in Barcelona.

In Biotech:

• Pear Therapeutics is a notable ‘software as a medical device’ failure.

• Bluebird Bio raised 100s of millions of dollars and went public, only to crash to zero and be taken private by Carlyle and SK Capital earlier this year for $30M ( Bluebird Bio gene therapy sells itself to Carlyle and SK Capital ).

• 23&Me never found product market fit and is just about worthless after its head start.

• Separate from deals I’ve worked on, Adam Feurstein of STAT has written up about CRISPR as a conspicuous failure. Theranos, of course, also comes to mind.

At this point, we flip roles, and Kyle asks me some questions!

KM: As a medical professional involved with mental health or behavioral health, how do you manage a patient who might be an (un)reliable narrator of their condition or circumstance?

OM: Great question! One of our patients' assumptions is that doctors have never seen anyone else like them before. Only a portion of our assessment is based on what a patient tells us. We're collecting a remarkable amount of data from the second we encounter a human. We're watching you walk into the office. We're observing your gate, looking for problems with your coordination of that gate, and noticing the jugular venous distinction of your neck, which can be a sign of heart disease. Clinicians are doing a comprehensive mental status exam while you're talking to us. We're listening to how fast you talk, how close together the words are, if there are any logical disconnects between one word and another one sentence and another, the tone of your voice, where you're looking when you're talking, how long the pauses are between your words, how much you shift around in your chair, etc.

There's a lot of information about how you say something, not only in what you say. Thus, in a way, we're listening for the words and the music together, but neither is complete without the other. Physicians also seen tens of thousands of hours of patients before– and people aren't that dissimilar. They tend to fall in buckets. They are patterns in human behavior, and the longer we've been at this clinical work, and better we've been trained, the more we can recognize a pattern at its start, before we see every single thread. You don't need to see the entire map of New York State to know it's New York State. You could cover up everything but Manhattan and still understand where you were. I also know how to get to Florida or Europe from there. We have more information to understand because of our experience than simply what someone tells us. It is also the case that many people are ashamed or don't understand the underlying problems leading their search for help. That can end in an office, or ER, or a hospital. Thus, we're asking all of those questions in our mind while we're listening to your story. Next, we're asking you questions that we've asked many other people, and that's behind the curtains on how we put together an understanding, however flawed it might be, of what's bothering the person in front of us, and how we might be able to be helpful. Not all of it requires reliable narration; hyper reliable stories are the exception, not the norm. Honestly, I consider a good judgment to be a little bit withholding with most psychiatrists– you don't know if you can trust them yet, and you don't know what they will do. If you've had experiences they're negative before, it makes sense not to tell them everything all at once. It's a lot like a date. Do you want to display good judgment? Yes? Well, don’t tell someone everything . But also, it's good if we both acknowledge that maybe we won't say everything in that first visit. If you're planning on being be withholding with the doctor, maybe let them know you're holding something back, and they'll hear the whole story next time. Similarly, the doctor should make you feel comfortable when it comes to how much you choose to disclose in the first session.

KM: You have extensive experience working with health insurance companies as both a payor and a business partner. What might be 1-2 common misunderstanding(s) about their incentives?

OM : The most common misunderstanding is that health insurance companies want to save money. That's only true in very limited cases. The vast majority of health insurance companies at this point time are giant, vertically integrated corporations with revenue at a scale that boggles the mind. We're talking about an interstellar scale, or at least an interplanetary scale, of money.

Consider the annual revenue of every pharmacy benefit manager, 80% of which are vertically integrated with three of the most major payers, United, CIGNA, and CVS. Parkinson’s Disease (PD) is a neurodegenerative condition that leads to problems with both movement and thinking. This newsletter is daily enough and health-themed, and I woke up thinking about movement disorders. Thus, this article!

It's a disable condition in which not enough dopamine is made by the cells normally responsible for that. This dopamine-producing center of the brain, called the substantia nigra is unable to keep up with the brain’s need for dopamine. Dopamine is not the only chemical that makes us “feel good.”

As I have written in Inessential Pharmacology (Amazon link), dopamine is an essential regulator in several neural circuits. These circuits impact motor functioning, pleasure, reward-seeking, attention, and prolactin release, among other brain functions.

One of the themes of this newsletter, over and over again, is that the brain’s function is more about circuits than chemicals. Today, I'm discussing Parkinson's disease and the role of non-invasive neuromodulation with transcranial magnetic stimulation and trandcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) as additional treatment options for those suffering from Parkinson's disease.

First and foremost, Parkinson's Disease leads to problems with movement. As their ability to manufacture dopamine falls off, their ability to move is inhibited. Their gate gets slower, and their movements become stiff, rigid, and inflexible. All of the muscles, tendons, and bones work the same. It's a problem with the neural control of those movements—but it means people suffering get frozen. There can be cognitive problems as well as the disease advances, and eventually even dementia for some.

In the Journal of Neurological Sciences, at the end of last year, a lovely meta-analysis of the effectiveness of both TMS and tDCS was published as it related to the cognitive deficits of the disorder 1 . I’m going to skip over the many years of Deep Brain Stimulation work for now, except to say— it’s effective!

The results of that meta-analysis were not encouraging. They reviewed every study between 2000 and 2023 and found…nothing:

After database searching and screening of texts, sixteen studies met the inclusion criteria. No significant results emerged from any investigated cognitive domain when comparing NIBS and sham treatments.

Well… case closed . It just doesn’t work! Except…maybe we are missing something? Another group of authors (Zhang et al.) 2 , on January 6th of 2025, mere months after Giustiniani et al., completed the meta-analysis mentioned above.

One of the major issues that comes up, again and again, in this field is the low quality of the studies:

12.5% (2/16) of the SRs rated as high quality, 43.75% (7/16) rated as low quality, and 43.75% (7/16) rated as very low quality

Further:

Using the GRADE system, 16 papers were analyzed for 62 outcome indicators. The findings revealed that there was no high quality evidence present in the SRs.

At a high level, the GRADE system evaluates factors that increase or decrease the possibility that the data you are considering are not bull$*&#.

This includes risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness of evidence, imprecision—remember those confidence intervals I’m always including?—and publication bias. That, of course, is the likelihood that journals publish positive results and keep negative outcomes on the shelf.

Zhang et al. found that, among this steaming pile of not-good-enough data in PD studies, at least motor symptoms were improved—across both lower limbs and arms. In the February 2025 issue of the Journal Brain and Behavior , Angeline et. al. penned:

Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation in Pregnancy: Efficacy, Safety, and Future Implications for Perinatal Mental Health Care

The authors did a systematic review of 363 publications, of which 3 met inclusion criteria for their review, only focusing on women who were pregnant at the time and had major depressive disorder. The first thing to point out, for my readers, is that only three papers on the topic were available. There are TOO FEW STUDIES on a crucial issue for women and, subsequently, all humans.

The first study by Kim, et. al. , 6 was an open-label trial of depressed women treated with right-DLPFC inhibitory brain stimulation (not covered by insurance generally, and effective, but off-label from an FDA perspective). It included 20 participants, and the details I will drop in a footnote. 7

This first approach in an open-label design was effective:

Notably, 70% of the patients exhibited a positive response to the treatment, which is defined as a reduction of at least 50% in their Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HDRS) scores. Encouragingly, within this group, 30% met the criteria for remission, signifying a post-TMS HDRS score of less than 8. Furthermore, the mean HDRS scores demonstrated a substantial reduction from an average of 24.4 prior to TMS to 9.7 following TMS intervention ( p = 0.005).

With a solid open-label data set, Kim et al. proceeded to move to a Randomized Controlled Trial. This RCT encompassed 22 pregnant women who were diagnosed with major depressive disorder during their second or third trimester. This study didn't find a statistically significant difference:

the mean HDRS score decreasing from 23.18 prior to TMS to 9.27 following the twentieth session. A noteworthy 81.8% of active TMS participants achieved the predefined criterion for response, characterized by a 50% reduction in HDRS scores, in contrast to 45.5% of sham TMS participants. Moreover, remission, defined as achieving HDRS scores lower than 8, was observed in 27.3% of the active TMS group and 18.8% of the sham TMS group. However, it is pertinent to note that neither the remission nor response rates achieved statistical significance, which the authors attributed to the limited sample size.

Given the small sample size, I’d be suspicious if there WERE a difference—there were less subjects in this trial than in the SNT trial by E. Cole and colleagues 8 , and thus, in an underpowered study, the chance that you can demonstrate a real difference is hampered. These trials are hard to do! As philosophical aside:

Not all things that are therapeutic are therapies. Not all therapies are therapeutic.

Being paid attention to can be therapeutic despite not being a therapy. That is what sham treatment in a study protocol is! When it comes to safety, however, the study did add to our evidence base in a meaningful way:

This RCT did not identify significant differences in pre- and postintervention estradiol or progesterone levels.

And beyond lab values not changing…the neonatal outcomes were excellent:

APGAR scores, birth length, and weight, fell within the normal range. Furthermore, the authors noted that only five women in the study were concurrently using psychiatric medication, and all of them were on stable doses for at least 2 weeks prior to commencing their TMS treatment.

So that, alone, is news . A third trial moved to a more traditional TMS protocol to the Left front of the brain (details in footnote 9 ), conducted by Sayer et al. 10

Notably, NONE OF THESE STUDIES used a standard or FDA-cleared TMS protocol, which seems to me like a miss . They may have had excellent science reasons to chose the approaches they did, but at least according to this review, no one has studied in an RCT any FDA-labeled TMS protocol in women who are pregnant. This, of course, means that close to 100% of the pregnant women considering this treatment in the real world and their treaters have minimal data to guide them! Moving on to the outcome of this study, which were similar to the results of the prior studies:

The results indicated a significant reduction in mean HDRS scores, which decreased from 26.77 to 13.03. Importantly, 41.4% of the patients exhibited a favorable response to the TMS intervention, defined as a reduction of 50% or more in HDRS scores, while 20.7% achieved remission.

It's working about the same as traditional TMS in regular clinical samples of other depressed people but with a dearth of randomized or large-scale trial data.

The point of this review was safety, and here, too, there were no adverse outcomes:

there were no reports of adverse neonatal outcomes in association with this TMS treatment. This underscores the safety of the intervention concerning neonatal health.

Thank goodness we have one more RCT coming this year that didn't make it into the above review because it hasn’t been done yet. The protocol was published less than a month after it was submitted! In just February of 2025, the following RCT protocol was published before the trial is concluded, which I hope will meaningfully answer more of the most important questions we have about what to do for women who are pregnant and depressed:

Effectiveness of rTMS compared to SSRI as early treatment of depression – study protocol of a randomized controlled trial (Early-TMS)

The authors will compare iTBS stimulation to SSRIs in pregnant women with depression to determine which is better 11 . I know which I’d be betting on, but we don’t have to rely on a gut check or hunches when we have empirical answers.

Science will move forward. The one bet I’m willing to make? The science will absolutely LAP the payment models if we don’t raise all our voices to both our employers and elected representatives.

The bottom line for today? In addition to the above controlled studies, authors have reviewed all available case studies of babies born after moms treated with TMS. They found no evidence of risk 12 :

The available reports provide no evidence that TMS to mother during pregnancy has detrimental effects on the fetus.

We know there is risk with oral antidepressants. Defined, and definite risk. Depression itself has risks that are likely greater than the risk from medicines that treat it. The only biological treatment that has no risk we know of? rTMS, for now.

And soon, thanks to the Early-TMS trial, we will have a direct comparison.

“More research is needed.” It can be exhausting to constantly repeat this statement, but that is where we are today.
