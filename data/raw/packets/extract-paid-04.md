SXSW is all about disruption. I could just make that assertion, and, in the style of health technology companies, just move on. That is a set up, but I'll put my pedantic graphic in a footnote.1

Disrupting healthcare is difficult. But disrupting disruption? It might as well be the actual job description of established companies. When I see deeply disruptive products in healthcare, brought to us by colleagues at Johnson and Johnson2, I'm impressed.

This post is about a product they built in orthopedics, and it is both disruptive and deeply realistic when it comes to effective go-to-market, which in mental health care is almost completely absent.

They built a surgical robot. We have had surgical robots before. The da Vinci system is a very expensive surgical robot that does nothing to improve outcomes in any of the data I have reviewed.

This surgical robot does knee operations. Here are the things about this technology that I think is disruptive, and, spoiler alert, they're all about the details that take existing systems into account. Keep in mind, this is already FDA-cleared and deployed in hospitals throughout the United States and around the world.

• The above is the visual localization system that allows the robot to do its thing. It doesn't reportedly break constantly. It runs off of a standard 120v electrical outlet. No absurd requirements for power.

• That is the actual robotic device above a fake knee. It works. If it looks dumb above that black box, take a minute. It is a functioning healthcare technology that is built to clamp onto the existing bedsides in surgical operating rooms. The reason it looks awkward on that box is that setting it up on the case in which it is able to travel is not the real world. It's too busy being built to facilitate orthopedic surgery to be set up in a way that looks fancy for a conference.

• That hardcase is case is the actual case the system can travel in. It’a portable. Might it be more portable than the egos of orthopedic surgeons?3

• It turns orthopedic surgery staffing costs into capital costs, so it's moving dollars from staffing to depreciable expenses. Salaries for rural hospitals don’t depreciate, capital costs do. Is the only thing MBAs care about earnings before taxes, interest depreciation, and amortization (EBITDA)? Well, that and reducing headcount. This robot takes expensive orthopedic surgeons— expertise is extremely difficult to adaptively manage on a balance sheet—and turns it into something that MBAs can grapple with by making it something that can be written off over several years. On a balance sheet, it makes healthcare profits look better.

Disruptively acceptable payment models in health technology are the real disruption. The Overton Window rarely moves fast, and expecting payment models and accounting methodology to keep up with Healthcare Innovation is unrealistic. Johnson and Johnson stuck the landing, by understanding. They understood not only technology, and what is possible in the minds of the people. The people who are running the numbers on whether new technology is worth it.

Psychedelics, and their 27 breathless panels about the bold power of their imagined future, take note.

—Owen Scott Muir, M.D.

Hi there, AI. I know you're involved in everything I write, mostly because I use Siri for nonmedical writing, and that means that natural language processing has long been playing a role in the creation of the Substack. And I'm appreciative of that. Don't get me wrong. I even checked my grammar with you using Grammarly.

Here's the problem: I am really into regulation. I'm really into writing about regulation. I really like to advocate for changes in regulations.

The public health emergency has ended, and that is awesome. Except, it's not awesome if you need to prescribe controlled substances or plausibly need to interact with anybody who might need to get prescribed controlled substances. If you think hearing about ADHD is annoying now, imagine all of the people who can’t stop talking about it off their helpful medication —abruptly.

And given chatGPT is optimized only to do some things and not others, the limitations around that software have made it impossible for me to automate the process of advocating for regulatory change in a way that will be suitable and scaleable.

Artificial intelligence, in the form of Generative AI w/ ChatGPT, isn't disruptive enough. The irony is that very shortly, even this complaint will be indexed by an artificial intelligence product and turned into fodder for a future Generative AI on the topic of regulatory change.

What we need is Generative Advocacy! We need to be able to feed an algorithm like chatGPT any law we want to see changed. Maybe just let it scrape regulations.gov? Then we should type in the problems we see with the proposed rule and have it generate a statement fitting for public consumption!

If we want disruptive technology, we must disrupt regulations that are not in the public interest. And this is something that people with ADHD who aren't going to get their meds will be bad at. Generative AI can help.

Someone, anyone, let’s build the above product so that when we need to comment on regulations.gov regarding the end of the public health emergency, for example, as we have 18 days left to do so. Together, it'll be more accessible in the future.

For now, it's left to analog humans to advocate for change, so I strongly recommend everybody on earth go to regulations.gov and leave a comment about your concerns with the end of the public health emergency to controlled substance prescribing.

In short, the current role will be a disaster, so we need to leave comments to let the DEA know by clicking here.

The Online Reputation Management Hack For Healthcare Professionals
Surviving trolls, one positive review at a time
OWEN SCOTT MUIR, M.D.
MAR 25, 2023
∙ PAID
2

I am a physician. I am also an occasional troll for compliance. Negative reviews in healthcare are common, but unlike Denny’s managers, health professionals are limited in important respects to respond to criticism online.

One of the ways of dealing with this probem, while staying in compliance with all rules and regulations around Healthcare? Become a reviewer of things yourself. Google reviews are potent. The more you leave reviews, the more powerful you become…more than you could possibly imagine. It is a bit like the dark side, hate, and striking people down.

Except it’s the opposite. The more positive reviews colleagues, and, crucially, other things, the more of The Force you can muster.

Share The Frontier Psychiatrists

It is not lawful in most states to respond to patient reviews in a way that exposes protected information. Testimonials by patients also violate applicable legal statutes in NYS, and some (but not all) other states. You also can not solicit those reviews. If you look at fake reviews, it's pretty transparent. Fakers review a bunch of the the same business all at once. They are fake—except they drive algorithms that don't know this. The reverse is also true: by reviewing restaurants, gas stations, whatever, your reviews get flagged as genuine by the algorithms. This helps us push down the spam bots and trolls.

One thing you can do? Review your colleagues.

Review platforms like Yelp, Google, and other health reviews, and prioritize reviews from prolific reviewers. Reviewing others yourself makes your reviews more salient—to the bots, and human patients!

What happens when you respond to a physician review on Google, and then respond to thank your colleague for the review? This approach bumps up the search engine optimization of that review. Rob Dugger, M.D. from Sarasota Minds taught me this, and feel free to leave him a review right now to thank him!

Here are the 3 ways one can calculate engagement:

1) Engagement Rate = Total Engagements/Followers

Followers are those individuals whom your post could potentially be directly exposed to. This type of calculation measures engagement on a per follower basis.

Pros: Easy to calculate; follower base quality assessment.

Cons: Non-follower engagement is not taken into account.

2) Engagement Rate = Total Engagements/Reaches

Reaches refer to the number of individuals that you post is exposed to. This type of calculation measures engagement on a per individual basis.

Pros: Post quality assessment

Cons: Impression frequency is not taken into consideration

3) Engagement Rate = Total Engagements/Impressions

This measures engagement on a per view/display basis.

Pros: Useful metric for measuring cost per thousand (CPM)

Cons: Variable frequency can lead to inconsistent results (impression = reach x frequency).

Some of my best performing posts (other than the ones featured above) have been about my enthusiasm for the work of others, and this is a really good thing to lean into. Despite the fact that LinkedIn is basically “self-promotion as a service,” and actually end up going better if you don't do that much.

The relationship between traction on LinkedIn and driving readership to a newsletter is limited.

Being a good writer is rewarded either way with growth in readership. But you shouldn't confuse newsletter content with LinkedIn content or vice versa.

Most people do not pay for a newsletter.

Monetizing any of this is hard early on.

Regular and consistent posting is rewarded.

The perfect is the enemy of the good.

Do not let AI write your posts or your articles. Write it yourself. Unless it's all SEO bait, in which case go for it.
