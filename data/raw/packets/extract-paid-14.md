And now, our dispatch…

I was at Brain Stimulation 2025 when Paul Fitzgerald took the microphone—or rather, when the microphone was handed to him, which in a room full of TMS researchers is an act of considerable consequence. Dr. Paul Fitzgerald, for those of you who don’t know him, is one of the founders of the modern TMS-for-depression enterprise. He trained in Melbourne, has been doing this longer than most of us have known what theta burst stimulation is, and he has strong opinions. Correct ones, mostly.

A brief note, most of my columns are for general audiences, this column has an audience of TMS clinicians in mind. It's a bit too in the weeds for the general public. I do not mind you, dear members of the general public, poking your head inside our research conference back-and-forth.

What followed was one of those rare conference sessions that reminded me why I fly across the world to stand in hotel ballrooms drinking bad coffee. The questions were better than most papers I’ve read this year, the disagreements were productive, and nobody was selling anything. I was in the room. I asked a question — actually, I gave a speech, which I will tell you about — and I’m going to tell you about all of it.

The 6,000-Pulse Finding That Should Have Changed Everything

Let me start with the thing should raise a few eyebrows. There are so many unanswered questions in the world of brain stimulation!

Fitzgerald mentioned — almost in passing, the way experts mention things that should be headline news — that one of the single most significant efficacy findings in the history of TMS outside of the accelerated SAINT paradigm was a trial of theta burst stimulation over the left DLPFC using 6,000 pulses per session. In approximately 70 patients, they found a 45% response rate.

If you do TMS, you know that number is remarkable. The standard iTBS protocol delivers 600 pulses. The field has largely stuck to 600 pulses because that’s what the original Huang et al. motor cortex plasticity studies used, and that’s what got FDA cleared, and so here we are, in the year of our Lord 2025, still giving everyone 600 pulses and wondering why some people don’t respond.

The 600-pulse number, to be clear, was not derived from a careful dose-finding study in the human prefrontal cortex. It was derived from hippocampal slice data and motor cortex experiments, then essentially copy-pasted onto DLPFC depression treatment. Nobody thought too hard about it for a decade.

And here’s where Fitzgerald said something I think is genuinely important: the question isn’t just whether *more* pulses are better. **The optimal number of pulses is probably different for every person.**

A secondary analysis of a clinical dataset (Sarah’s data, as he referred to it) found no significant group-level difference between pulse conditions — but within subjects, there was striking individual variability. Some patients showed maximal plasticity at 600 pulses. Others showed it at 1,800. A few probably need something else entirely.

His analogy: “It’s like shoe sizing. I wear a certain size — you might not wear a size 12.”

Which is folksy, but the implication is serious. We are currently dosing every patient identically in a treatment modality whose entire rationale is inducing individualized neuroplasticity. If you were dosing a cancer patient, Fitzgerald noted, you would carefully monitor biological response and titrate. We don’t do that. We just give everyone the same thing and hope.

Separately: when asked directly from the floor whether clinicians should just increase pulses beyond 600 for standard iTBS, the answer from the panel was essentially no. There are randomized studies comparing 600 to 800 pulses showing no difference. The variability you introduce by going beyond protocol may create noise without signal. As Fitzgerald put it: longer is not necessarily better. The dose-response question is about the right dose for this person, not simply more.

-----

Are We at the Ceiling? (For Regular TMS, Probably Yes. For TBS, Possibly No.)

For conventional 10 Hz rTMS — the original protocol, 3,000 pulses over 20+ minutes — a randomized study comparing 50 trains versus 125 trains (roughly 15 minutes versus an hour of treatment) showed **no dose escalation effect**. More pulses, same result. The dose-response curve for standard TMS appears to be flat at the top. We’re already there.

For theta burst stimulation, the situation is potentially the opposite. Modeling work suggests the relationship between pulse count and plasticity induction isn’t linear — it has peaks and troughs. 600 pulses may be one peak. 1,800 pulses may be another peak, but smaller. And somewhere above that, you may actually start to *reverse* plasticity. There’s a Walter Paulus presentation from the day before suggesting that increasing TBS pulses beyond a certain point might flip the direction of effect.

So the dose-response question for TBS isn’t “more is better.” It might be “the right amount for *this person* is better, and that amount is something we currently have no validated way of identifying in advance.”

This is a problem.

The Interval Question: Why Are We Still Using a Number From a Mouse Hippocampus?

Accelerated TMS — delivering multiple sessions per day — has generated genuinely exciting results, particularly since the SAINT trial came out of Stanford. But accelerated protocols have inherited an inter-session interval of approximately 50 minutes from studies on long-term potentiation in hippocampal slices from rodents. Hippocampal slices. From rodents.

Nobody is saying this was an unreasonable starting point. But there is now at least one human study — Fitzgerald cited it as the Netvin study from around 2018 — that found something interesting: with a 15-minute interval, the first two iTBS sessions in a day don’t produce dramatically compounding excitability. Then the third session produces a dramatic, sudden increase in cortical excitability. Which suggests that **shorter intervals may not just be tolerable — they might actually work through a different mechanism**, and one that appears to be beneficial.

This matters enormously for clinical practicality. If you can use 15-minute intervals instead of 50-minute intervals, you can fit six sessions into a standard working day. Ten sessions becomes a very long day. The difference between a protocol that requires staff from 7am to 6pm versus one that fits into a sensible 8-hour day is the difference between a protocol that’s academically interesting and one that will actually get delivered to patients at scale.

Fitzgerald’s read: the **interval between sessions** is probably the most practically important variable we haven’t yet optimized. More important than pulse count per session. Possibly more important than targeting.

Bilateral TMS: Why Do We Do What We Do? (The LTD Story Is Shakier Than You Think)

Bilateral TMS — typically 10 Hz over the left DLPFC followed by 1 Hz over the right — has been used clinically for two decades. The rationale, if you learned it the way I did, was something like: high-frequency left-sided stimulation induces LTP in the hypoactive left hemisphere; low-frequency right-sided stimulation induces LTD in the hyperactive right hemisphere; together you’re correcting a pathological left-right imbalance in prefrontal activity.

That story is tidy. It is also, at this point, almost certainly wrong.

Fitzgerald dropped this relatively quietly but I think it deserves more attention: an fMRI study (his group, around 2006 or 2008) looked at what actually happens to brain activity after left-sided versus right-sided TMS. The prediction was obvious — left-sided treatment should increase left-hemisphere activity; right-sided treatment should decrease right-hemisphere activity. Clean, bilateral dissociation, confirming the whole theoretical framework.

That is not what they found. Left-sided treatment increased activity on the left — and also on the right. Right-sided treatment decreased activity on the right — and also on the left. The effects didn’t dissociate. The whole lateralization theory failed to map onto the actual neuroimaging data.

Fitzgerald noted that essentially everyone has now abandoned those left-right imbalance theories, and the field is looking for new frameworks. The bilateral protocol persists in clinical use not because the original rationale held up but because it appears to work empirically — for reasons we don’t yet fully understand.

This extends to the bilateral TBS question. The obvious bilateral analog would be cTBS on the right (inhibitory) followed by iTBS on the left (excitatory). If you ask why we use *continuous* TBS on the right, the answer is that cTBS is ostensibly designed to produce LTD. LTD on the right, LTP on the left — tidy bilateral theory again.

Fitzgerald’s assessment of the evidence base for this:

“Zero. Zero evidence.”

There was some early work suggesting hyperexcitability on the right when you stimulate the left, and vice versa, but the actual neurophysiological evidence that cTBS reliably produces the LTD you need on the right DLPFC to support the bilateral rationale is, in his words, not there. We’ve been doing it because it was a reasonable hypothesis that never really got tested.

This does not necessarily mean bilateral TBS doesn’t work — there’s mounting evidence it produces significant clinical benefit, possibly more than unilateral protocols. But it almost certainly doesn’t work *for the reasons we thought it did*. That distinction matters if you’re trying to optimize the protocol.

On the practical question of order: does it matter whether you do right-sided first or left-sided first in a bilateral protocol? The answer from the panel was essentially no — but by convention, most groups do right first. The original reasoning (at least in some groups) was that you want to prime the left side with contralateral inhibition before you activate it — a kind of disinhibition-then-excitation logic. That reasoning is speculative. The tolerability rationale is slightly more grounded: cTBS is more painful than iTBS. Forty seconds of continuous TBS is uncomfortable in a way that twenty seconds of iTBS is not. Some groups do right first precisely because you get the worse part over with before the patient has decided how much they dislike the whole enterprise.

Right-Sided Stimulation and Suicidality: Something Is Emerging Here

One of the more clinically important observations in this session — and one that didn’t get as much time as it deserved — was around right-sided stimulation and suicidal ideation specifically.

Fitzgerald reported that accelerated cTBS on the right produced equivalent antidepressant effects to standard left-sided protocols, but showed a **differentially greater effect on suicidal ideation**. This isn’t fully explained yet, but his hypothesis is that right-sided and bilateral stimulation may produce greater reductions in agitation and anxiety, and that this mediates specific benefit for suicidal patients — who are often, as a group, highly agitated and anxious rather than purely anhedonic.

This is clinically plausible. The suicidal patients who come to my practice are not, for the most part, the classically depressed patients who stare at the wall. They’re activated. They’re in pain that won’t stop. Reducing agitation and anxiety is often the therapeutic lever that matters most acutely.

There is now a named trial — the Kale Award trial — specifically designed to address whether bilateral stimulation is superior to unilateral for suicidal ideation. It’s years out from results. But the biological signal here is interesting enough that I think it’s worth paying attention to in your current clinical practice: if your patient is presenting with prominent suicidality alongside depression, the case for at least considering a bilateral or right-sided protocol is becoming more substantive than it was two years ago.

The Neuronavigation Question: An Honest Answer

Someone from the audience asked about neuronavigation — whether individualized coil localization improves outcomes over beam F3 positioning.

Fitzgerald gave what I thought was a genuinely honest answer:

the jury is still out, specifically for single-target DLPFC protocols.

The reason is geometric. When you look at the area stimulated by beam F3 done properly, and compare it to any of the neuronavigation targets, the overlap is very high — around 80% of patients end up treated at effectively the same site either way. To show a statistically significant difference in outcomes, you need a massive sample. A 40-versus-40 study showing a difference should not be believed. The effect size, if it exists, would be too small to detect at that n.

Where neuronavigation genuinely adds value: when you’re targeting truly different cortical locations based on network individualization; when you’re doing E-field modeling to optimize coil angle for the individual’s prefrontal gyral geometry; and as a training aid for coil stability.

That last point is worth dwelling on. The DLPFC, unlike the motor cortex, varies substantially in its gyral orientation across people. The motor cortex lines up such that a 45° coil angle works consistently across subjects. The middle frontal gyrus is different in basically everyone. Getting the angle right — maximizing the electric field penetration — may ultimately explain more outcome variance than the exact coordinates you’re targeting.

My own take: you can navigate your coil to the right place and still train your staff to maintain it there with great fidelity. Navigation adds complexity, and complexity increases training requirements. I think there’s a real argument for neuronavigation for E-field optimization of coil angle specifically. For target coordinates alone, in a single-target DLPFC protocol, I’m not sure the data support the premium.

How Many Sessions Can Patients Miss?

Registry data across about 6,000 patients: **gaps of up to approximately 14 days don’t appear to meaningfully impact outcomes.** A study from Adelaide randomized patients to three versus five sessions per week — the three-times-weekly patients took longer to get better, but got there to the same degree.

Practically: one or two missed sessions in a week is probably fine. Sustained treatment twice a week — I start to get worried, because the evidence base stops supporting it. A Canadian colleague made a reasonable point about resource stewardship in publicly funded systems: limiting missed sessions to five or so over a course and extending by a week to compensate is defensible both clinically and institutionally.

The Patient Who Responded to One Protocol and Relapsed: What Do You Do?

Someone from UCSF1 asked this: a patient responded to sequential bilateral TMS (10 Hz left, 1 Hz right), relapsed, and it’s very tempting to do something faster and more convenient for the re-treatment. Do you switch to TBS?

Fitzgerald’s answer was careful: just because TBS and standard rTMS produce equivalent outcomes at the population level does not mean they are equivalent for every individual. The TMS community’s guidelines are clear — if someone responded to a course of standard stimulation, it does not straightforwardly make sense to assume they’ll respond to a different modality. The response mechanisms may be distinct at the individual level.

This led to a broader complaint, one I share: why don’t we have maintenance treatment covered by insurance? I have patients who get better with TMS and then relapse six months later and I have to fight insurance for another course every single time. The maintenance literature exists. The problem is that the FDA has required a biological target for trial approval, which means maintenance TMS trials have been essentially unfundable under current frameworks. We missed probably 20 years of potential progress.

Insurance Won’t Cover TBS — And What To Do About It

This came up directly in the session, and I want to give it its own space because it’s a live problem for a lot of clinicians in the US.

Some insurers — not all, but enough that it was raised by a clinician in the room — will not cover iTBS. They’ll pay for it, and then claw back the money when they find out what protocol you used. This is insane. The non-inferiority data for iTBS versus 10 Hz is clear. FDA-cleared. Published. The evidence base is not the problem.

Corey Allen and colleagues have published on the idiosyncratic, inconsistent rules that insurers across the US apply to TMS coverage — decisions that bear essentially no relationship to the clinical evidence. The trials that established iTBS efficacy were rigorous. The insurers have taken that evidence and done whatever they want with it.

So what do you do?

I took the microphone. Here is what I said, and I stand by it:

The fact that an insurer tells you something isn’t covered does not make it legally true. You can appeal. And if you appeal enough times, you reach independent medical review — where actual physicians look at the actual evidence and render a decision that, if you’ve also filed a complaint with your state attorney general, becomes **legally binding on the insurance company**. Your state attorney general is your patient’s lawyer. That service is free. The more of those you file, the more you shift national coverage policy.

Furthermore: as of 2021, plan sponsors (typically employers who fund self-insured health plans) have a fiduciary duty under ERISA to ensure their employees receive covered benefits appropriately. Breach of that fiduciary duty is, depending on the circumstances, a crime with penalties including up to ten years in prison per case. I have written about this extensively in this newsletter. I will keep writing about it.

The path is: do the right thing medically. Document it. Appeal the denial. Appeal again. Appeal again. Appeal again until you reach independent medical review. Reference the data. Write to the attorney general. This is how coverage standards change. Not by accepting what the insurer says.

Their job, to be clear, is not to save your patient money. Their job is to not pay you. These are wildly effective treatments. They know that. Lower utilization of effective outpatient treatment drives higher downstream medical costs — and higher premiums. The incentive structure is not aligned with your patient’s health. Which means our job is to fight.

Adolescent Depression and TMS: The Approval That Happened Without an RCT

Someone asked about the FDA clearance of TMS for adolescents aged 15 and up, which was based on registry data rather than a sham-controlled trial.

I’ll tell you what I think: it was the right call. Every oral antidepressant approved for adolescent depression carries a black-box warning for suicidal ideation. TMS doesn’t carry that warning. The registry data showing safety and effectiveness in adolescents is substantial. The argument that every single indication must have a randomized sham-controlled trial before we use it in young people would, taken to its logical conclusion, prevent us from using most of what we do.

Fitzgerald: if we were talking about interleukin-based treatment or targeted oncology drugs, we wouldn’t insist on an RCT for every pediatric extension. There are circumstances where the ethical and practical barriers to sham-controlled trials are significant and the existing evidence base is sufficient. This was one of them.

Provocation Scripts for OCD: Required? Probably Not. Useful? Sometimes.

The deep TMS approval for OCD was based on a trial that used symptom provocation immediately before stimulation. The FDA doesn’t require this in current clinical practice, but whether you should do it anyway is a live question.

I shared my own experience from the floor.

I do a lot of accelerated OCD treatment — the whole course in five days. My patients are coming in already highly symptomatic. They don’t need me to remind them what they’re scared of. And for patients with comorbid PTSD using the H7 coil, the risk of a provocation script triggering a trauma response is real.

My personal protocol in those cases:

I have them look out my office window at the Empire State Building. Genuinely interesting fixation cross. No trauma risk. I’m getting approximately ~40% remission of OCD. The subsequent post-marketing data still shows meaningful clinical effects. My hunch is that provocation matters most for patients with lower baseline symptom severity. For the patients who reach me, that is rarely the constraint.

The Bigger Picture: Why We Need a Registry

Across this entire conversation, every thread converged on the same conclusion: randomized controlled trials cannot answer the questions we most need answered right now. Individualization, protocol switching, optimal maintenance schedules, substance interactions, coil angle, pulse number — these require sample sizes and within-subject designs that no single trial can deliver.

There’s a registry being built, announced in this session. The goal is to aggregate non-invasive brain stimulation data from centers worldwide. If you’re sitting on a mass of TMS data and you want to contribute, there’s a poster and an oral presentation. Find it. The field needs your data more than it needs another small RCT.

I’ll end with the question someone asked near the close of the session:

When do you stop? If cost is no object and the patient is partially responding, when do you decide you’ve done enough?

The answer:

you don’t stop TMS treatment if they’re still improving.

There’s registry data suggesting patients on linear response trajectories continue to improve if you continue treatment. The analogy used — which I use myself — is oncology. If a patient is still responding to chemotherapy, you don’t stop at session 30 because that’s what the protocol says. You keep going, with monitoring and consent.

And then someone from the audience said: “What if they got response but not remission? Would you stop at 36?”

Fitzgerald:

“No.”

This is the right answer.

Coverage from Brain Stimulation 2025, Kobe, Japan. This was a Panel Session on Midline and Medial Targets in Neuromodulation, and the audience for this post is my fellow clinicians. As usual, I invite the general public to join us as we think through the science.

Scientists whose work is referenced include Colleen Hanlon, Abraham Zangen, and Andy Leucter. Those links point to their Google Scholar profiles instead of listing every reference individually.

About half of patients with treatment-resistant depression who enter a traditional TMS course will achieve remission. That’s genuinely good medicine — better than most pharmacological alternatives for this population. But it also means roughly half do not respond, and the field has spent years asking the same uncomfortable question: what are we doing wrong for those patients, and can we do better?

A panel session at Brain Stimulation 2025 in Kobe tackled this question from three distinct angles — clinical outcome optimization, electrophysiological biomarkers, and a systematic mapping of underutilized cortical territory — and arrived at a shared conclusion: the standard left-DLPFC target, for all its merits, is probably not the right answer for everyone.

The Rumination Problem

My friend (and the very distinguished) Professor Andy Leucter, M.D., opened from UCLA with a population that many clinicians recognize but rarely discuss explicitly: patients whose primary experience of depression is not low mood but relentless, consuming negative rumination. These are the patients, he noted bluntly, who come in and describe the various ways they’ve thought about ending their life in the past 24 hours — not as a plan, but as an obsessive cognitive loop they cannot interrupt.

In a study of 150 patients, Leucter’s group found that while depression severity and rumination were correlated at baseline, and while both improved with standard left-DLPFC TMS, they appeared to follow partly independent trajectories. Depression severity did not predict improvement in rumination — suggesting, as Leucter put it, that rumination and depression may sometimes need to be targeted separately.

This framing set up the rest of his talk: a case for the right orbital frontal cortex (OFC) as a supplementary or alternative target, particularly in patients who are not responding to standard treatment. The theoretical foundation comes from what has been called the nonreward attractor theory of depression — the idea that a nexus of pathologically increased connectivity, centered on the right OFC, may sustain negative ruminative thinking even when standard antidepressant interventions are applied. By stimulating (and potentially down-regulating) this area, the thinking goes, you may be able to break up this attractor network.

Leucter was careful to flag the evidence base honestly. There is a systematic study from 2024 comparing left DLPFC alone versus combined left DLPFC plus right OFC stimulation, which showed that both active treatments were effective and both separated from sham — but the advantage of dual targeting was modest and disappeared entirely at four-week follow-up in unselected patients. The lesson, in his view, is not that OFC stimulation doesn’t work, but that selecting the right patients for it matters enormously.

His group’s approach is measurement-based: they track symptom change every five sessions and use early non-response as a decision trigger. Patients who show less than 10% improvement at sessions five through ten are flagged as high-risk and eligible for protocol augmentation. In these early non-responders with comorbid OCD and depression, adding right OFC stimulation after session ten produced a meaningful change in trajectory, with patients beginning to improve after a period of initial stagnation. And in a separate sample of ruminative patients without comorbid OCD, the same augmentation strategy produced significant improvement in both rumination and depression scores.

An ongoing open-label randomization is now comparing three augmentation strategies for early non-responders: right OFC stimulation, right DLPFC stimulation, and theta-burst priming of the left DLPFC. Early data suggest the strategies may converge on similar endpoints at the end of a full treatment course — but with an important caveat: the OFC approach seems to take longer to “kick in,” suggesting that if it’s going to be used, starting earlier in the course may matter.

Leucter closed with an honest provocation: when you look at a connectome of the default mode network and examine where increased connectivity is most strongly concentrated, the right OFC is not, in fact, the obvious winner. The precuneus and posterior cingulate — neither of which is a current clinical target — may show even greater connectivity abnormalities. “This is a rich target space,” he said. “We have evidence that it’s worth exploring. We don’t have the answer yet.”

One editorial note from your author: OFC targets can SUCK when it comes to pain and discomfort—it’s right about the eye with plenty of afferent sensory nerves. The posterior of the head—where the precuneous lives, is lightly enervated and has precious little muscle to worry about contracting. It might prove more effective and almost certainly would be more tolerable.

Reading the Brain’s Response: EEG as a Targeting and Prediction Tool

Dr. Zangen’s talk approached the same fundamental problem — who benefits from which target? — from an electrophysiological direction, reporting results from a large multicenter randomized trial comparing lateral (H1 coil, left DLPFC-predominant) versus medial (H7 coil, mPFC/dmPFC-predominant) deep TMS for treatment-resistant depression.

The study enrolled 159 patients who had failed adequate pharmacological treatment in the current episode. Patients were randomized to four weeks of either lateral or medial deep TMS, followed by two weeks of reduced frequency, with EEG recorded at baseline and after four weeks. The primary clinical results were, by Zangen’s own characterization, initially disappointing: the two targets were equivalently effective, each producing roughly 50-60% response rates without a significant difference in speed of onset or trajectory of improvement.

But the disappointing headline masked something more interesting underneath.

The first layer was clinical. A cluster of six symptoms drawn from standard depression rating scales — combining anxiety, agitation, and core depressive features — appeared to predict differential response. Patients with high scores on this symptom cluster responded better to the lateral H1 target; patients with low scores responded better to the medial H7 target. This finding was then cross-validated in an entirely independent European dataset comparing deep TMS to other modalities, where the same symptom cluster again predicted differential benefit. “This gives us a clinical roadmap,” Zangen suggested — not for all patients, but as a first approximation of who might benefit from which target. The tool is called C-DEPTH, fyi.

The second layer was electrophysiological, and here the story became more mechanistically interesting. Resting-state EEG measures — coherence, power, and asymmetry — proved largely unhelpful in predicting response to either target. But evoked EEG responses, measured during the stimulation trains themselves, told a different story.

For medial stimulation, the key finding was gamma band activity. Patients who showed greater gamma engagement during stimulation — measured in the inter-train intervals — showed greater reductions in gamma power over the four-week course, and these reductions correlated with clinical improvement. The logic: if the tissue is engaging, and that engagement decreases over time (suggesting modulation of excitability), the patient is more likely to respond.

For lateral stimulation, the predictive measure was alpha asymmetry. Greater increases in left-lateralized alpha reduction (indicating increased left cortical activity relative to right) correlated with greater clinical benefit.

These are not just mechanistic curiosities. Measuring evoked responses in the first treatment session, before any meaningful clinical change had occurred, Zangen’s group was able to predict response at four weeks. This is the kind of early signal the field has been searching for — a way to identify, within days of starting treatment, which patients are likely to benefit and which may need a different approach.

Zangen also touched on earlier OCD work that set up this analysis. In a study using medial deep TMS for OCD, the group found that patients with OCD showed dramatically elevated error-related negativity (ERN) responses — a neural marker of performance monitoring — compared to controls. Treatment with H7 medial TMS actually increased this already-elevated ERN, and the magnitude of that increase predicted clinical improvement. The interpretation:

…the ERN elevation in OCD may not reflect pathology per se, but rather a compensatory coping mechanism. By engaging the medial PFC through stimulation, the treatment appears to amplify this compensatory signal — and the more it amplifies it, the better the patient does.

Mapping the Unmapped: A Systematic Look at Midline Targets

The final talk in the series (which I remember being given by Dr. Colleen Hanlon, on of my favorite colleagues) zoomed out to ask a question the first two had implicitly raised: if we’re starting to take medial and frontopolar targets seriously, how much do we actually know about this entire territory, and how much remains unexplored?

The answer, from a systematic review of 177 trials targeting the midline cortical strip, was: less than we’d like to think, and more than most of the field realizes.

The midline strip — running from the supplementary motor area (SMA) anteriorly through the pre-SMA, dmPFC, mPFC, and frontopolar cortex — has been targeted across a surprisingly broad range of conditions. Parkinson’s disease leads the field, followed by OCD, depression, addiction, and anxiety. But the literature is fragmented in a critical way: there is no consensus on how to name and delineate these areas. Using the 10-20 EEG system as a coordinate framework, the same scalp location can be found in papers labeled as “mPFC” in one study and “SMA” in another. This nomenclature problem makes cross-study comparison nearly impossible and likely inflates apparent heterogeneity in the literature.

The most detailed mechanistic argument in the talk concerned addiction. Here, a dual-target model has been gaining traction. The frontopolar cortex appears to sit at a connectivity hub with two opposing relationships to downstream reward circuitry: positive connectivity with areas involved in craving and reward salience, and negative (regulatory, top-down) connectivity with areas involved in impulsive action. A large meta-analysis pooling over 2,000 trials found that the most effective TMS protocols for addiction-related outcomes were those whose electric-field (“eField”) modeling showed cortical engagement in the frontopolar zone — providing convergent support for the model from an entirely different methodological direction.

Connectivity mapping at the individual level adds another layer. Frontopolar connectivity profiles — specifically the balance between the positive reward-circuit connections and the negative inhibitory connections — predict cue-reactivity responses at the individual patient level. A test-retest study tracking connectivity over two weeks without intervention confirmed sufficient reliability to support clinical individualization: patients’ connectivity profiles are stable enough that a map taken today will still be meaningful at treatment onset.

The talk closed on a genuinely open question. Even after 177 trials, large portions of the midline strip have simply never been studied. Tolerability mapping of these unstimulated regions — conducted by probing feasibility and discomfort across the full midline territory — suggests most zones are well-tolerated at standard intensities, with discomfort increasing predictably toward the posterior midline at higher intensities. There is a clinical signal potentially sitting in this unmapped territory, and the field has largely walked past it.

A Pattern Worth Naming

Taken together, the three talks painted a picture that is directionally coherent. The left DLPFC is a good target for many patients. However, the bimodal distribution of TMS outcomes — Leucter showed the canonical two-peak histogram — reflects genuine biological heterogeneity that a single target cannot resolve. The medial and frontopolar cortex are not alternatives to established targets so much as additional tools, each with a plausible mechanistic rationale and emerging evidence, that can be deployed selectively for patients who are not on track.

What remains lacking — and what this panel was in many ways a call to build — is a systematic, prospectively validated framework for target selection. The clinical symptom clusters Zangen’s group identified, the early evoked-response predictors, the connectivity-based individualization approach, and Leucter’s measurement-based augmentation protocol: these are all pieces of a future precision psychiatry workflow that does not yet fully exist.

The midline, it turns out, is not just the space between the targets we already know. It’s where the next generation of the field is likely to be built.

More Coverage from Brain Stimulation 2025, Kobe, Japan!

When TMS researchers talk about “targets,”—that is, “where in the brain should we stimulate?”— the conversation usually gravitates to the familiar: the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex for depression, M1 for movement disorders and somatic pain, and the orbital frontal cortex for OCD. This is an article written to an audience of brain medicine doctors and scientists, in that it’s a little in the weeds for general audiences.

But at Brain Stimulation 2025 in Kobe, one of the more thought-provoking presentations asked a different question entirely: what about everything in between?

Share

The midline strip of cortex — running from the supplementary motor area (SMA) forward through the pre-SMA, the dorsomedial prefrontal cortex (dmPFC), and all the way to the frontopolar cortex — has been accumulating a surprisingly rich evidence base, largely without the field noticing it as a coherent zone. A systematic review presented at the conference identified 177 trials targeting some portion of this midline territory, covering conditions ranging from Parkinson’s disease (the most common target) to OCD, depression, addiction, and anxiety. And yet, the presenter noted, this body of work has never really been stitched together into a unified framework — in part because the field can’t even agree on what to call these areas.

A Naming Problem with Real Consequences

Using the standard 10-20 EEG coordinate system, the midline strip can be parsed into rough anatomical zones: from the motor cortex landmark (Cz) anteriorly to FCz marks the SMA; FCz to FPz (in rough 5% steps along the nasion-to-inion axis) encompasses the pre-SMA, the dmPFC, and eventually the mPFC and frontopolar cortex. Simple enough in theory. In practice, the systematic review found that researchers were labeling the same scalp coordinates under wildly different anatomical terms — a study might claim to target “mPFC” while their coil was sitting squarely over what most would call SMA, or vice versa.

This isn’t just taxonomic fussiness. If different labs are comparing “mPFC stimulation” protocols that actually differ by 3-4 centimeters of cortex, the noise in the literature becomes almost uninterpretable. The presenter’s call for a community consensus on midline terminology was one of the more quietly urgent moments in the session.

Addiction and the Dual-Target Model

The bulk of the presentation focused on addiction — where the midline has attracted growing mechanistic interest. The key insight, built from a converging series of connectivity analyses, is that the frontopolar cortex (sitting at the far anterior end of this midline zone) may function as an integrative hub with two distinct and opposing relationships to downstream reward circuitry.

On one side, the frontopolar cortex shows positive functional connectivity with areas implicated in craving and reward salience. On the other, it maintains negative (regulatory, top-down) connectivity with regions involved in impulsive action. This dual-target model — sometimes traced back to earlier proposals about combined frontal poles and striatal connectivity — now has meta-analytic support: a large analysis pooling over 2,000 trials found that the most effective TMS protocols for addiction-related outcomes were those whose electric field modeling showed cortical engagement in precisely this frontopolar zone.

The implication is that the frontopolar cortex isn’t just a reasonable target for addiction — it may be the mechanistic linchpin that ties together what have seemed like disparate TMS findings across substances and behavioral addictions.

From Population to Person: The Individualization Problem

Knowing the right region in aggregate is one thing. Knowing where to aim the coil for this patient is another. The presenter’s group has been tackling this with resting-state fMRI connectivity mapping, using individual frontopolar connectivity profiles to personalize targeting within the broader midline zone. In a sample of sixty participants, individual differences in frontopolar-to-reward circuit connectivity predicted cue-reactivity responses — supporting the idea that connectivity mapping could one day guide not just target selection but coil orientation and dosing as well.

A separate test-retest study, tracking connectivity over two weeks without intervention, found that individual connectivity profiles are stable enough to be clinically actionable. This matters because a target that shifts substantially between sessions would undermine the rationale for personalized neuronavigation.

What Nobody Has Tried Yet

Perhaps the most provocative part of the talk was its closing observation: even after 177 trials, large portions of the midline strip remain essentially unexplored. The presenter’s group has been systematically probing tolerability and feasibility across these understimulated zones — and finding that many of them are well-tolerated at standard intensities, with discomfort increasing (predictably) as stimulation moves toward the posterior midline at higher intensities.

The subfield of interventional psychiatry has a tendency to converge on a small number of well-validated targets while leaving adjacent territory almost entirely unmapped. The midline story suggests there may be a significant clinical signal sitting right in front of us — or rather, right above the nasion.

A Global Effort

The presenter closed by noting that midline TMS research is genuinely international — with contributions spanning the US, China, and researchers across multiple continents — and announced a collaborative network (INEMS: the International Network on Non-invasive Electric and Magnetic Stimulation) with a planned workshop at the upcoming Brisbane meeting. If the field is going to make sense of 177 disparate trials and build something coherent on top of them, coordination across groups will be essential.

For those of us in interventional psychiatry, the midline is no longer just the space between the targets we already know. It may be the next frontier!

There’s some things you can’t go back in time to accomplish. One of those things, as a writer? All the interviews you wish you had done with your people who passed into that dark night.

I have to go back in my mind, imagine what they would say. I can never ask them. I can never get the real story. Some people, of course, can’t narrate their experience well anyway. Even if they were ensconsed in this mortal coil, the “real story” might be beyond their ability to verbally represent.

My mom is still living. She’s here, regularly, in the pages of this newsletter. There’s still so much more I need to ask her, and there are so many great excuses to not do it today. I’m busy. Despite being in her 80s, she’s busy. Vita is running an arts-not-for-profit. She’s got a generation of jazz musicians to grow from teenage fumblers to Grammy winning artists—again. She’s already done it at least once.

Like I said, there are good reasons my mom and I don’t just sit down and do endless interviews about her entire life. But none of us get endless time on this earth, and if we wait too long, for anything we want to do, this time may run out. It’s true for my mom and I, and it’s true for everyone else, too.

Narrative nonfiction requires, at least when you’re reporting on the internal experience of other people, conversations with those very people, notes taken, recordings made. Transcriptions thereof, time to think about what was transcribed, and the laborious process of turning those things into a story that can be the spark for someone else’s mind to explode with a new possibility. Podcasts (like mine) are even worse, you need to get them on tape with a fidelity that’s suitable for subsequent audience.

Some tasks can’t be automated with AI. Artificial intelligence isn’t a time machine. It never went with us to the waterpark that one time, and it never remembered what it was like to have that burn on your hand from the fire you were trying to start while on your first time camping. It can’t remember what’s most noticeable to you, because the only person who noticed it as the most noticeable was you. It can confabulate memories you might’ve had, but never know which ones you carried with you to this moment.

One of the things I’ve learned about writing, having done a lot of it now, is that it’s not so much about generating text. It’s about generating thoughts, or catching them like fireflies. You whittle those thoughts down via one mechanism or another into text. The written word is the final product, we all agree. But it is a lot more like oil than it is water.

For oil to be useful, you have to drill, pump, transport, refine, transport again. Writing is as refined as gasoline. Done right, like gasoline for cars, it just gets your mind to travel from place to place in a way that wouldn’t have been possible otherwise. I don’t know that I’ve ever looked at gasoline, intentionally, in my life. I sure as hell haven’t seen electricity. The power behind our lives is mostly invisible. But electricity doesn’t start with a spark, and it doesn’t end with the pixel on the computer screen you’re reading this on. There’s a whole process. So, too, with the human thoughts behind writing that can touch our hearts or change our minds.

One of the problems of being a writer is that I now spend my life worrying about losing the opportunity to write about whatever it was I was gonna write about. I’m a certain kind of writer, non-fiction for the most part. I’ve only dipped a toe into fiction, the tiniest pinky into poetry, and been up to my ankles into songwriting. Most of those things can be done in retrospect.

I’m a staunch proponent of hysterical realism, who has gone on to become an accidental journalist, with some remarkable stories of my own to share.

I fret that I will change things by how I write about them. I hope, very much, that I will do the same for the things I’d like to see happen. I’m most unprepared, however, for the loss that I feel when I realize I won’t get another shot at the story I want to tell, because the people who could have confided in me are lost. Sometimes they are still alive, and the moment has passed. Sometimes they are in elysium.

Writing about the real is a race against time I will surely lose, and only capture a whiff of the moment I had hoped to communicate. I see the appeal of fiction, but I know that most great fiction is thinly veiled journalism anyway.

I mourn for what I will miss. Maybe, next, I can try to let go of what can’t be. Observing the process will change it, and some stories can’t ever be told.

In December 2024, Definium Therapeutics started a Phase 3 trial of DT120, the molecule formerly known as LSD, for generalized anxiety disorder.

Four months later, in April 2025, they started a separate Phase 3 program. It was an evaluation of the same compound, this time for major depressive disorder. The depression study read out top-line last week. The anxiety study? The one that started first? It is still running.

This almost never happens—trials take a predictable amount of time, like olympic athletes running around a track. Little differences are common, but lapping the other runners isn’t. The fact that GAD’s program was beaten to the finish line in another indication is news, in and of itself. The data from that trial, EMERGE, are similarly remarkable.

A psychedelic medicine program just ran its second pivotal indication faster than its first, in a field where pivotal trials traditionally run two to four years.

Your author loves a rapid acting treatment. Heck, we have a whole event series dedicated to them, with the next RAMHT event happening at Psych Congress, on September 14th, 2026. Get your tickets now!

RAMHT @ Psych Congress

First, we had SAINT TMS promising rapid relief from depression in just 5 days of treatment. Although that 5-day number is always true for the treatment itself, it’s only true for the response to treatment for 60% of patients. The rest of patients have a response that “can’t be rushed.” The final remission number, which is somewhere between 50-79% depending on the sample we look at, remit between 1 and 4 weeks later.

Every day with severe depression can’t end soon enough, as evidenced by the rate of suicide in sufferers being on the order of 200/100,000 lifetime risk from MDD (or more, with common comorbidities considered). This horrific outcome is hard to prevent.

The momentum continued with data from the “AINT” trial, covered by both this newsletter, and here in this article too.

Last week, we had the topline of data from the team at Definium Therapeutics, with friend of the newsletter Dan Karlin, M.D., in the CMO chair, reading out data on their trial for lead asset DT120, the compound formerly known as LSD, née, Lysergide Tartrate ODT. For more on LSD, I conveniently senior-authored a review last year for you, dear readers, in the American Journal of Therapeutics:

Preliminary data suggest that LSD may be effective for the management of alcohol use disorder, anxiety, and depression. In trials of LSD for treating anxiety and depression associated with life-threatening illnesses, 77% of participants demonstrate durable relief at 1 year post-treatment. Top-line data from a large-scale phase IIb trial (n = 198) indicate that 50% of participants experience remission from generalized anxiety disorder after a single 100 μg dose of LSD.

Much to the surprise of us all, their readout on their concurrent MDD clinical trial program happened before their GAD readout, which is expected later this year.

The data is remarkable, let’s just get that out of the way. This is what the above text looks like when laid out in a graph:

This is among the most perfect separations between placebo and active you could hope for. The critics will quickly note that functional unblinding is highly likely. Well, of course it is. This is a psychedelic medicine, and their other clinical trial programs have active controls to solve for that problem. Not every study can demonstrate everything.

Why the timeline matters so much

The potency of the effect got the headlines—well it should, it’s the biggest deal in depression treatment ever. There are quibles to be had with that number: ”functional unblinding” is a just concern.

The schedule, however, should get the real attention. Here is what the clincal trials registry actually shows:

• DT120 GAD pivotal (Voyage, NCT06741228): started December 11, 2024, primary completion projected July 2026. ~19 months.

• DT120 MDD pivotal (Emerge, NCT06941844): started April 14, 2025, primary completion May 20, 2026. ~13 months.

The MDD trial started 4 months later than the GAD trial. It finished 2 months earlier. It ran roughly 6 months faster.

Perhaps the most important thing about this remarkable outcome is that it came out before the anxiety data. This is a team working at breakneck speed, such that a clinical trial program started later read out top-line earlier. For investors, that means it’s possible to speed-run a clinical trial program, which means money is moving through the system faster. Returns on investment are quicker, and the time value of money goes up. For patients waiting for relief, there’s also the hope that they can get relief faster, and this is a rare place where investment dollars and patient outcomes are highly aligned on the same outcome.

For those of us not deep in the weeds on clinical trials, none of this is usually fast. Anxiety trials take a long time to enroll, because although many people have anxiety, most people who have anxiety also have other problems, and they don’t belong in an anxiety monotherapy study. The fact that they got a depression study done even before the anxiety study finished is just not what we see in life sciences traditionally.

How fast is fast?

Among recent FDA-approved or late-stage small-molecule MDD programs, here is what pivotal trial timing looks like:

• Auvelity (AXS-05), Axsome Therapeutics. GEMINI pivotal (NCT04019704): started June 2019, primary completion November 2019. About 5 months. n=327, 6-week endpoint, no long-term extension built in. Definium is following up to a year, by contrast.

• Caplyta (lumateperone), Intra-Cellular Therapies. Two pivotal adjunctive MDD trials (NCT04985942, NCT05061706): each ran roughly 31 months, n≈485 per trial, with 6-week endpoints.

• DT120 (Definium). Emerge MDD (NCT06941844): started April 2025, primary completion May 2026. About 13 months. n=149, 12-week endpoint with a 40-week open-label extension already enrolling.

A casual read is that Auvelity’s 5-month GEMINI was faster than DT120’s 13-month Emerge. However, an apples-to-apples comparison is that DT120 ran a longer endpoint in Emerge, with the extension phase already happening.

Caplyta, the closest modern analog in design, took two and a half times as long per trial. For a psychedelic program, with future REMS concerns, dosing-day logistics, and a comparators that have been the slowest in psychiatry?

13 months is the number that should make the rest of the Life Sciences uncomfortable, while filling patients and clinicians with hope.

The other outstanding thing about these data is that the amount of time people were still woozy after the initial dose was not 12 hours. Most people were done in five or six hours, even though they had to wait until the very end of the dosing session. The actual amount of time patients are likely to sit in an office is not outlandish.

So we’ve got a huge difference between active and placebo, the treatment seems like a big deal, and lo, psychedelic medicines as a field of endeavor have been validated for financial markets. Just after these results were announced, the company successfully raised an additional $805 million to bring the drug to market.

Capital flows at trial speed.

The $805 million did not appear because investors all got back from a Lysergide Retreat in central america.

I believe capital moved, robustly, because investors saw a team that could compress a Phase 3 program from 30+ months to 13.

Speed of capital follows speed of evidence. If the next program in this company’s pipeline runs on the same clock, the implied cost of capital for psychedelic medicine drops—this is financially material. Every other CNS program may get benchmarked against it.

I’ve seen a lot of hand-wringing from a lot of investors over a lot of years. Is it possible that this will work? I think the answer is yes. I think the psychedelic medicines are coming. I think their effect sizes are going to be undeniable. They will be different in different patient populations, but we have at least 7 psychiatric disorder treatments—lysergide, comp360, ibogaine derivatives, methylone, DMT, 5-MEO-DMT, MDMA and it’s enantomers, all of which are looking as remarkable as GLP-1s are for metabolic disorders. Along with both invasive and non-invasive neuromodulation, we have a whole suite of options that necessitates rethinking how we address brain disorders.

And Definium’s Emerge? It’s a “great clinical trial outcome,” by any measure.

It is, however, great before we’ve even optimized workflows, business models, care pathways, patient experiences, adjunctive therapies, or any of the rest of the “must haves” when psychedelic research becomes psychedelic medicine.

Bigger, better results are possible for our patients. I’m so glad I lived to see it. That wasn’t always a given for me, and it wasn’t always a given for many of our patients who have been waiting so long for relief.

I just dropped a prior article which is not behind a paywall. This is that article before I asked Fable 5 to make it much more concise. This article? It’s worse, unreadably so, according to my wife. Too much math!

I present it for posterity, and for those who want to see inside the disagreements in my peculiar marriage.

Carlene, last weekend © Owen Muir, 2026

I’m not a venture capitalist. I’m not an investor. I don’t have much money to my name. I’m a doctor, and I started my career later than most. I enrolled in medical school at age 27, graduated at 31, did four years in general psychiatry residency, and a two-year child psychiatry fellowship after that.

That would’ve been foolish enough, financially, except I started trying to build businesses. This is the story of the businesses we should build to ensure a better world with more effective treatments for psychiatric patients.

There are risks involved. One of those risks is that you can build a business, and try to sell it, and then someone can (allegedly) just not pay you for it. That’s happened to me already, and is currently the subject of active litigation.

I’m happy to be involved with some excellent companies now, and I hope they’re gonna go somewhere exciting, both for our patients and financially. I’ve had a lot of time to sit around, with very little money in the bank, and just notice things.

Here’s what I noticed: pharmaceutical companies and medical device companies are only gonna keep creating miracles if they make a hell of a lot of money in the process. Doctors need the appropriate incentive to get in the middle of this process, and start getting paid like the valuable members of the system they are, instead of having all the money go to pharmacy benefit managers and other large, complex conglomerates.

Cut the doctors in.

That’s the argument. The rest of this piece is the math.

Full disclosure, before the math: I’m a C-level exec at Radial and Neurolief, and an advisor to companies in health tech and life sciences. I am, in other words, arguing my own book. Discount accordingly.

My old clinic in Brooklyn was the first to dispense Spravato in that borough, and the second in New York City. Spravato was the first of these “in-clinic medications.” This is a big change for psychiatry. Yes, we already had TMS, but that hasn’t exactly blown up either.

One of the benefits of small molecule, one-daily-pill psychiatry is that it was a capital-efficient way to get wildly profitable products to market.

Let me lay it out for you, not as a doctor, but thinking about this as a business person might.

The old way: doctors as a free distribution channel

Individual psychiatrists, in their individual offices, create an expense for drug companies. They are a distribution channel. Drug companies don’t have to pay for this distribution channel, but they do have to hire medical science liaisons and pharmaceutical salespeople to visit all these individual doctors. Why are they visiting? So that the doctors will prescribe the drugs, which are dispensed by pharmacies, which take a cut of the revenue.

We have to do a quick aside through the supply chain of drugs in America. It’s painfully complicated, but it’s worth it to give you a good picture of the financials of small pills versus the psychedelics and medical devices that are coming, and to understand how different these supply chains are, and where the expenses and revenue differences are.

It all starts with the wholesale acquisition cost. We’re gonna imagine a pill that has a wholesale acquisition cost of $100. Now we’re gonna break down where the money goes, in the traditional pathway from the pharmaceutical company through a traditional pharmacy and into the patient’s medicine cabinet, without the doctor ever having any part in that flow of drug and, most importantly from the perspective of the businesses involved, money.

The cost of goods sold (COGS)? It’s between $1 and $10.

Only pennies of that are the actual pill. Another two to four dollars goes to the wholesaler. Here’s where it gets weird. There’s something called the pharmacy benefit manager. We’re gonna imagine this is a commercial health plan, for the sake of this example, not straight Medicaid or straight Medicare.

The pharmacy benefit manager gets a rebate (money directed to it) from the drug company, for the purpose of getting the drug onto a higher tier of the formulary, and thus having a lower co-pay. This “rebate” adds up to between $25 and $50 of the cost of that hundred-dollar pill. There are also administrative fees from the pharmacy benefit manager, and something called a group purchasing organization, and those add up to a couple percent of the wholesale acquisition cost, another $3 to $5, approximately. Now, in order to keep the co-pay down so the customer doesn’t walk away from the pharmacy when they find out how much their hundred-dollar-per-pill drug costs, there’s co-pay assistance and patient support. This is the manufacturer buying down the cost to the customer at the point of sale in the pharmacy. It doesn’t actually make the drug any cheaper for the health plan, which the consumer is also paying for through their premiums and deductibles, but it does feel like it costs less. The marginal pill cost ends up being pennies at scale. But it costs the manufacturer another $50 to $70 just to bring the drug to the end consumer: the rebate, the wholesaler cost, the pharmacy dispensing costs, and let’s not forget they’re paying salespeople to go out and meet with doctors, and advertising on TV, radio, buses, the sides of cabs, and the rest of it.

The profit is still tremendous, like $30 of profit on every hundred dollars of list cost, paid by the customer directly in the form of a co-pay, and by the perverse admixture of health plans and pharmacies and pharmacy benefit managers and wholesale distributors and the rest.

30% margins is a pretty good business.

You know who doesn’t make any money out of that? The doctors. I know it sounds crazy, because we all know how doctors are on the take, in the popular conversation, but really, we’re being plied with praise, pleasantness, academic prestige, and feeling like we’re helping our patients. Doctors aren’t all about money, after all.

Doctors are totally left out of the above loop. The drug reps who visit them are giving them pamphlets; maybe they’re asking them to speak about their drugs for a couple thousand dollars here and there; maybe they get to read from some slides, and feel like they’re helping their colleagues understand something new. But the doctors actually prescribing the vast majority of these small pills? They’re generating massive profits for pharmaceutical companies, pharmacy benefit managers, conglomerate pharmacy companies, health insurance companies: basically everyone but themselves.

This is a remarkably successful business model, despite all of its complexity.

At it’s peak, for aripiprazole (which eventually hit 6.9b in annual sales), the sales ramp looked like this (compared to a less initially successful launch of esketamine):

One might ask: why don’t doctors want to get into the actual business of medicine, and get some money out of the deal? I know, right? Doctors are terrible at business, and I should know, I’ve been pretty terrible at business myself!

The alternate universe: buy-and-bill

Let’s look at an alternate way this could work, in the world of psychedelics, to incentivize doctors to actually think about business for once. I know, I know, we’re in the business of helping our patients, and all of that is true, but if we don’t become a viable part of the actual business of business, our patients don’t get to have any good new stuff.

J&J’s Spravato was the beginning of this process, at least in psychiatry, but it won’t be the end of it, and that, as you can tell, is what I’m arguing should happen.

In this alternate universe, which we call “buy-and-bill,” the medical practice contracts with a specialty distributor (which is legally a pharmacy), which dispenses a drug to a certified clinic, which purchases and holds the inventory using its own money. The drug is administered on site, the patient is monitored on site, and the payment comes through the health plan, on the medical and behavioral health benefit side of the ledger.

In this setup, the money flows as follows:

The clinic bills the patient’s medical benefit, not the pharmacy benefit, using a drug code (these are HCPCS J, G, or S codes), or, sometimes, a combination code that includes both the drug and the medical service rendered.

What makes this especially painful is that G codes are temporary codes used by Medicare, S codes are temporary codes used by commercial payers, and J codes are permanent codes used to describe dispensing drugs in clinics and hospitals. But these J codes only exist to describe the infusion of drugs that cannot be self-administered by a patient.

There are also the Current Procedural Terminology codes, a.k.a. CPT codes, a product of the American Medical Association. These codes describe physician time doing something—anything—and are billed to the medical benefit, or to the behavioral health benefit if it’s a psychiatrist.

So there are multiple different ways of describing how you bill for dispensing Spravato… and all of them have different economics.

Medicare decided to create G codes that account for both the acquisition of the drug and the dispensing of the drug, with two different codes for the two different doses. G2082 and G2083 are bundled codes covering the office visit, the esketamine itself, and the two hours of observation: one code for the whole encounter, tiered by dose.

Under commercial plans, they use an S code: Spravato’s is S0013 (“esketamine, per mg”), which many commercial plans require instead of the Medicare G codes. S codes were created by the Blue Cross Blue Shield Association and private payers for things they need to pay for but Medicare doesn’t recognize.

The crucial difference between the S code version of Spravato and the G code version is that the physician either gets paid a bundled rate for both the clinical care for two hours and the drug, versus the drug separately (the S code) plus the clinical care, which is documented in a separate evaluation and management services note (”E/M”). That means either extended time for the physician if they’re personally laying eyes on the patient the whole time (99215 for the first 40 minutes plus 99417 for each additional 15 minutes, so 99215 + 99417 x 4 for Spravato’s two hours of monitoring), or an E/M code plus an observation code for other clinic staff to do the observation (99415/99416 for the first 60 minutes and each additional 30 minutes, respectively).

I know all this is really in the weeds, but it’s gonna make sense just …now.

Every year, drug prices go up. Every year, physician reimbursement attempts to go down. Medicare’s payment formulas were designed to not blow up the entire federal budget with healthcare expenses, and, not surprisingly, have not succeeded. Every year physician reimbursement gets recalculated a little bit, but in the direction of stable or downward. Compared to inflation, it’s definitely downward over time. But drug costs just keep going up.

And there’s one more problem here: this whole scheme only works up to two hours, because you can’t bill more than 4 units of 99417, thanks to a limit on the number of times you can use the code in a single day, called a Medically Unlikely Edit, or MUE.

This means that MDMA, LSD, Psilocybin, Ibogaine, Methylone, and all other psychedelics with the exception of DMT and 5-meo-DMT don't have an outpatient code with established reimbursement that can be used to cover the treatment. We have CPT category III “tracking codes” approved, but they need to have rates negotiated from zero and only work out to 6 total hours (thanks to their MUE).

The other financial problem for practices? The lifetime value of a customer, or LTV, is relatively high for Spravato. Patients get an induction course of eight doses in one month, and then they're often getting a dose every week or every other week thereafter. The above compounds might have repeated dosing, but on the order of 1 to 4 doses in a year, not twice a week for the first month and more thereafter.

Thus, if your brain is locked in the existing payment models…there is a problem. Let's take a look at the problem, as it currently exists, below.

The squeeze

So let me summarize. First, your eyes are probably bleeding out of your head if you’re a non-physician, because that was also painful to read. The bottom line: doctors buy drugs, and they get paid something from a payer. Medicare keeps paying the same amount while the drug goes up in price, so they make less and less profit per unit of physician time watching a patient get Spravato. Or they can get the drug through the specialty distributor under a commercial plan, use an S code, pay for an increasingly expensive drug, and hope the S code reimbursement keeps up, and then get paid a decreasing amount every year to observe a patient getting this treatment.

The alternate version of this is just ordering the drug from a pharmacy, often a pharmacy owned by the pharmacy benefit manager, which is in turn owned by the health insurance company, so the practice doesn’t have to carry any of the cost of buying the drug, dispensing the drug, and wondering if the reimbursement for the combination of time-blah-blah-blah works out. They can just bill their standard rate and let the pharmacy deal with it. And the pharmacy, because it’s often owned by the payer, and the payer owns the pharmacy benefit manager, works out the math on the back end so that they all keep making plenty of money.

The independent physician gets squeezed.

The pharmacy benefit manager / payer / pharmacy conglomerate also gets squeezed, but not quite as much, because they’re negotiating with themselves for a the contracts. Some of these companies have seen that it makes sense to just acquire a bunch of the physicians, too. Most of the major payors are doing this, but I’m gonna use the biggest, UnitedHealth Group, for my example. UnitedHealth Group’s Optum employs about 10% of American physicians. They own their own pharmacy benefit manager, OptumRx, and their own specialty pharmacies: Genoa, Optum Specialty Pharmacy, Diplomat, and others. In this example, Genoa handles most of the Spravato business. So United has to negotiate with itself (the PBM, OptumRx), and then with itself again (Genoa), and then with itself again (the physician group), to decide how they should divvy up the total amount of money from Spravato.

The part where you tell me this is a terrible idea

Some of my readers, at least, will know the history of “buy-and-bill,” and the following comment is intended to blunt their objections, or to raise one relevant comp for those of you who haven’t been following healthcare economics across different sectors as thoroughly as others. Those in the know are already preparing an angry comment, and it’s about oncology.

Selfless colleagues in oncology, those who care for the sick and the dying, have not been ignoring the business aspect of their business like psychiatrists have. Oncologists have been buying and billing infusion chemotherapy for decades. That’s where those permanent J codes come from. Medicare pays them the average sales price of the drug plus 6%.

6% of a $500 drug is $30. 6% of a $15,000 drug is $900. You can see how a thoughtful physician, reviewing the evidence, might conclude that the more expensive drug is, on reflection, well, clinically superior. There’s a huge bias built in to use new and more expensive drugs. The data about the economics of oncology suggests that this is exactly what happened, at least some of the time.

Congress also noticed what happened. Everyone noticed what happened in their premiums. “Buy-and-bill” became a phrase you can say at a health policy conference if you want people to look at you with disdain. But do you know what people don't complain about? The lack of access to oncology services. Oncology is expensive, because the drugs are expensive, and the doctors make a little bit of money on the drugs. But it means there are plenty of oncologist offering infusion drugs, and even the newest infusion drugs, instead of not offering anything new, because it doesn't fit in with their business model. The world of oncology without this 6% bump on the administration of a drug is a world where all oncologist are prescribing methotrexate and then staring blankly at you when you talk about immunologic infusion treatment.

So what I’m asking for here is an incentive. Doctors work well with incentives because they’re humans. And all humans respond to incentives. That’s why we have the dismal science of economics in the first place.

The entire premise of this essay is that psychiatrists are likely to respond to incentives in the same way as every other human, and act accordingly. They will prescribe more of what I suspect will be better treatments. We already know this possible with transcranial magnetic stimulation, but since the barricades are up so high from the payer side of the equation, and nobody has heard about the treatment outside of readers of this newsletter, it hasn't taken off as something with a 70% + remission rate should. Everyone has heard about psychedelics however, the world is primed, excited, and curious.

How do we keep Psychiatrist from becoming the money-grabbing villains of the oncology universe? The psychiatry and psychedelic version of “margin-on-drug” is not different because of our inherent virtue. It’s because psychedelics are all going to come with guardrails. These are in the form of Risk Evaluation and Mitigation Strategies (”REMS”). These are guardrails about how the drugs will have to be dispensed. Spravato has one such REMS. It requires monitoring time. We’re doing it because it’s dangerous to just give someone Spravato and let them wander the streets. The Medically Unlikely Edit of four on the 99417 code won’t let us bill beyond the two-hour monitoring time. I can’t infuse a patient with extra ketamine just to make more money. The dose is the dose, the observation window is the observation window, and the certified clinic is accountable and audited for both. Novel psychedelics coming to the world of clinical care, if the universe has been listening to me rant, will come with monitoring built in, likely with even more auditable video. Not only will pharmaceutical companies bring these drugs to market with the ability to have a sense of what’s happening in the room, but so will the third-party payors with whom we partner (with occasional consternation) to bring care to our patients.

Compare that scenario to the rebate maze from the top of the essay, and ask yourself if you’d prefer the perverse incentives of tiny pill rebates to the tune of billions of dollars going to Big Health Companies, or physicians incentivized to provide treatment that’s more likely to get patients to robust response or remission?

The pipeline drugs that are coming behind Spravato, based on psilocybin, LSD, MDMA, and all the drugs you’re used to hearing about over the past couple of years? Almost all of them will require longer monitoring than we can currently account for. None of these drugs are gonna go through a retail pharmacy.

You can’t roll up to your local CVS and get handed a bottle of psilocybin with a sticker that says “may cause ego-dissolution.” These treatments are in-clinic by design. The clinic is the distribution channel. These clinics are going to need extra rooms, and even more extra rooms, because if they don’t have enough extra rooms, we’re not gonna be able to help all the people who need it. Those rooms may need special cameras, IT infrastructure, comfortable furniture for an entire day of treatment. Somebody has to pay for all of that, and whoever pays for the rooms owns the economics of the distribution channel.

There are three candidates:

• Physicians, or

• the existing conglomerates that are already acquiring physicians at the rate of about one in ten.

• Nobody but the vastly wealthy gets access to these treatments

I started this essay complaining about my finances. I’ve had a lot of time to notice things. And this, dear readers, is the thing that I noticed. My finances are not a wreck by accident; it’s because the financial infrastructure to make money as a psychiatrist, or any other doctor treating patients with mental illness, is busted. It was designed for pills that go home in a bottle in your pocket, and it wasn’t designed to address set, setting, and systems of care.

For the first time in my career, a class of drug can’t get to the psychiatric patient without the clinicians. That’s leverage.

We, the physicians, nurses, physician assistants, therapists, and other health professionals, we finally have leverage over how an entirely new way of delivering medical care gets built. Industry needs certified clinics the way they used to need a pharmacy’s shelf space.

At long last, we are the scarce resource. This is real power, in economic terms, and it gives us the opportunity to build the businesses around our clinical practice that are meaningfully different from everything we’ve seen before.

Doctors are terrible at business. We can stay terrible at it, and Optum and its colleagues will be happy to handle all the details and pocket all the dollars. Or we can get good at business while we still have the leverage. I’ve already made my choice.

The time, my friends and colleagues, is now. If not for ourselves, then for our patients. I’m willing to bet they’d prefer the set and setting of a clinic owned and operated by physicians over the set and setting you’d expect from the companies that brought you prior authorization of Zoloft.
