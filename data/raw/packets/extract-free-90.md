A Mistrial

On Friday, September 4, 2026, in Plymouth Superior Court in the Commonwealth of Massachusetts, a jury of 12 people was unable to come to a unanimous decision in the case of Lindsay Clancy. Judge William Sullivan had repeatedly instructed the jurors — nine women and three men — over the course of seven days and about 38 hours of total deliberation that their decision needed to be unanimous. After evaluating the testimony of 70+ witnesses, the jury reported, “with a heavy heart,” that it was unable to reach a unanimous decision.

There is no debate about what happened in this case. On January 24, 2023, in Duxbury, Massachusetts, three children’s lives were ended by their mother, Lindsay, within a 45-minute period. The only debate, and the issue the jurors were unable to find unanimity around, was whether Ms. Clancy could be held criminally responsible for what she did.

Per the defense, 11 jurors favored finding Clancy not criminally responsible. The 12th, a man, would not be persuaded.

Judge Sullivan declared a mistrial.

“It felt like they were filming the Salem witch trials.”
— Carlene MacMillan, M.D.

On September 29, a status hearing on the case will be heard by the judge, and the district attorney, Timothy Cruz, will decide whether they are going to move ahead with a second trial addressing the same facts with different jurors.

Everything I was witnessing from the sidelines as a psychiatrist seemed a kind of madness to me. It didn’t make sense that 12 civilians, lacking expertise in the complex medical issues at play, would be asked to determine the fate of a woman who so ardently tried to end her own life after those of her children by jumping out of a window headfirst. Clancy is now paralyzed and bereft.

I did what I usually do, and I called my colleagues to try to make sense of it. Here are the people I talked to, imagining I would find a unanimous understanding, at least among my expert colleagues. I include disclosures, so that the audience can make up their own minds about our biases, which are myriad.

I approve of collage. This is Ms. Clancy in front of a composited Duxbury, MA shoreline.

My Editorial Process

Over the course of four subsequent articles, I will lay out what I learned, weaving together the interviews that I did. I will edit for clarity per my agreement with the interviewees. None of the physicians have evaluated Lindsay Clancy. I have deliberately removed any opinions of my interviewees on what the verdict should be, because a re-trial is likely, or at least plausible. These opinions are intended to be based on our knowledge of the general state of medicine as it applies to the care of women in the peripartum period, and although they reference this legal matter, they are not intended to be understood as definitive. None of us were expert witnesses in the case, nor did we watch every moment of testimony as the jury in the matter did.

All of us struggled with ambiguity as it relates to the matter of Lindsay Clancy’s culpability, and the horror of her children’s deaths.

The Question

My readers can’t judge whether psychiatry failed in this matter until they understand what the jury was asked to decide. Almost nobody watching one-minute clips on TikTok or Instagram got those instructions either. The question was never “did she do it?” Everybody, Clancy included, agrees on that point.

The entire trial was about one 45-minute period — the window of time in which Lindsay Clancy ended the lives of her three children and subsequently tried to end her own.

The Commonwealth of Massachusetts does not use the term “insanity.” Instead, the standard under which Ms. Clancy was tried — set by case law in Commonwealth v. McHoul (1967) — considers only “a lack of criminal responsibility.”

The Model Penal Code test, as adopted in Massachusetts, asks jurors to determine whether, as a result of mental disease or defect, the defendant lacked substantial capacity either (a) to appreciate the wrongfulness or criminality of the conduct, or (b) to conform their conduct to the requirements of the law.

The volitional prong of the law, (b) above, is what most of the coverage in this matter missed. It’s not just about whether you knew it was wrong. It’s about whether you either didn’t know it was wrong or couldn’t stop yourself because of a mental disease or defect.

This is different than the insanity defense in many other jurisdictions, including the federal courts, which only require part (a). It’s also why command hallucinations may have played such a substantial role in the defense.

The burden of proof is also relevant here: once the defense raises the question, the Commonwealth of Massachusetts must prove criminal responsibility beyond a reasonable doubt. That is to say, the only argument a prosecutor can make is that the person was, in fact, sane in that 45-minute period in which the deaths of the children took place.

Clancy told a hospital chaplain about hearing voices — command auditory hallucinations are in the legal record — but the state was forced to argue that during the 45 minutes in question she was sane enough both to know what she was doing was wrong and to stop it, but chose not to do so.

My friend David Carreon began our interview by talking about the impossibility of the task laid in front of the jury in this matter:

David: The entire question of guilty, not guilty, or degree of guilt is: what happened in that 45 minutes? What was her state of mind? Which is an incredibly hard question to answer generically.

Owen: What were you thinking for 45 minutes on any given day of your life? Much less the most challenging one. Most of us couldn’t answer that question.

David: Right. And so some of the legitimate complaints about Massachusetts law are — how do you expect anybody to meet that standard?

Not a Yes/No. A Menu.

It turns out not only was this an almost impossible task to begin with, but the jury had four possible answers they could choose from — it wasn’t just guilty or not guilty by reason of insanity; there were three possible guilty verdicts that could’ve been rendered.

The Four Options

David: The jury had four options. Option one: not criminally responsible — everybody agrees she did it, but she lacked the capacity. If not, then three options: first-degree murder, second-degree murder, and manslaughter. So, you could say that she badly managed her own mental health — that she should have followed the treatment recommendations she was given, and instead put herself in a reckless state that endangered her children.

Owen: Basically like driving a car while intoxicated. You could be parenting while impaired.

David: Right.

Owen: It seems like the jury had too many options here.

David: One might argue that’s another reason we ended up with a hung jury.

12 ordinary citizens were handed a four-way menu and an unfamiliar legal standard with which to deconstruct, in retrospect, what was going on in the mind of a woman who was demonstrably unwell some of the time — and they split, according to the defense, 11 to 1 in favor of not criminally responsible. When unanimity is the only acceptable answer, that means we’re not done yet as a society, and Lindsay Clancy is not done yet with this legal matter.

“Everybody’s Fighting About the Wrong Thing”

Will Sauve, M.D. is a frequent guest and a dear friend. His time in the Navy was well spent. He often likes to say that knowledge is the opposite of anxiety. He’s here to diagnose the discourse, not the defendant.

Will: There’s a ton of anxiety. We have colleagues who are terribly fearful that a clinic case with a bad outcome could turn into being hauled up on the stand. And there’s another side that’s terrified the person in court is somehow going to “get away with it.” And I don’t know that anybody’s focusing on the fact that this entire court case is just trying to understand: does somebody need to go to prison, or do they need to go to the hospital? There isn’t really an argument about the facts of what happened. They’re trying to determine whether someone can be held responsible under the law — and if they can’t, what kind of care they need, potentially for the rest of their life.

Owen: And if she’s found not guilty by reason of insanity — she’s going to a state psychiatric hospital for God knows how long. Which also has locks on the doors.

Will: Correct.

A Not Criminally Responsible verdict is followed in practice by civil commitment — this means you’re spending at least the next six months of your life in a hospital against your will, with annual renewals thereafter, and there is no maximum for how much time you could spend in a psychiatric hospital against your will, as long as a court finds the mental illness continues and dangerousness persists. Clancy has already spent three years in a psychiatric hospital prior to this trial.

Hinckley was found not guilty by reason of insanity in 1982 and spent 34 years at St. Elizabeths Hospital, then lived under court-ordered conditions with his mother from 2016 until his unconditional release in 2022. This was after shooting then-President Reagan in 1981 in an attempt to impress the actress Jodie Foster, the object of his obsession.

Will: We were taught that if he’d been judged guilty, the crime probably carried a twenty-year sentence, and he probably could have been out on parole in a fraction of that. But because it was not guilty by reason of insanity, he went to a hospital where there was no parole, no appeals, no nothing. It was purely a medical decision about whether he needed to live there for the rest of his life.

Even being found not criminally responsible in this matter does not mean freedom for Lindsay Clancy. It means spending as many years as the doctors and judges subsequently deem wise. There is no verdict that means that Lindsay Clancy gets away with anything .

Trials Are How We Punish, Not How We Learn

What we’re watching on TikTok, Instagram, or maybe even the news? It’s not truth, it’s an adversarial system that’s been televised and clipped up by influencers to get us to watch more. Everything a defense attorney says is designed to generate doubt in the minds of the jurors, and potentially, as collateral damage, outrage in the minds of viewers regarding that one moment.

None of the health professionals are on trial. Even if it feels like they are. This adversarial machinery produces verdicts, but it doesn’t produce understanding. In fairness, it was never designed to produce understanding. It was designed to bludgeon 12 people to a shared understanding of the truth, and in this case, it failed to do so.

The Goldwater Rule

Will: Isn’t it kind of similar to the Goldwater rule? The whole reason a psychiatrist can’t diagnose someone they’ve never met is that it’s not possible to know everything you need to know from watching someone on TV for minutes at a time. You could say the exact same thing about a court case. Every one of us on the outside is seeing snippets from different angles. None of us has the perspective of a juror who sat there and listened to every single thing.

The asymmetry of public opinion is, in your author’s opinion, the really unhinged part of this whole affair. Millions of people watched a minute at a time, and the comments sections are filled with certainty. Twelve jurors watched everything and couldn’t agree. My readers want to know what the experts think. Some of those experts, and their frank thoughts, follow over the next four articles.

The Most Obvious Bias in the Room: Expert Witnesses

An attorney isn’t going to pay an expert to review thousands of pages of documents if that expert’s opinion is not in keeping with the attorney’s preferred theory of the case. And if the first expert doesn’t say what they want, the attorney will find somebody else who does. This is not a system that helps jurors arrive at understanding, as Clancy’s case makes stark.

On Adversarial Experts

Chris: It never made sense to me, because it seems like they all say what they’re paid to say. Perhaps the money should go into a central pool — both parties pay in, and the judge chooses the expert witnesses, so they’re not clearly biased.

Owen: We have a whole jury selection process to keep bias out of the jury pool, to the degree possible. We don’t have that at all with experts.

Chris: It’s embarrassing, I think. I just don’t pay them any attention, because it’s the most obvious form of bias in our profession.

I’ve been an expert witness in two cases. I’m not going to get into the details of those cases. I can say that I provided my likely opinion up front, and, given it was in keeping with what the attorneys wanted, I was hired. I assume the experts in this case did the same. The system hires experts whose least-informed preliminary opinion best matches the payor’s wishes. The most objective experts are not hired. There is no mechanism to hire objective experts. They would have to be retained by the court, and this isn’t what happens.

Don’t Trust The Lawyers

Will: I got hauled into court when I was an intern, and it was very clear the lawyer was looking to make me into a fool to help their client. I got that education very young: this is not a case presentation in front of my colleagues at morning report. There’s a lawyer, and they are one hundred percent out to burn me down.

Owen: They’re only giving you rope to hang you with.

Will: That’s the idea.

The treating clinicians in this case were fact witnesses. They were, in theory, the most objective. They had had to address the concerns of the patient when she was a patient to whom they had an obligation. They were cross-examined by professionals paid to make them look incompetent. This adversarial cross-examination does not make the case that the care was good, or bad; it exists only to make it controversial for the jury.

In the next articles, we’ll get into perspectives that get at what I hope is an objective truth. This series attempts to do the opposite of what cross-examination does. We’re looking to find ambiguity and come to terms with it — not manufacture more of it, the way cross-examiners are paid to.

We’re going to try to help you make sense of this case, and more importantly, answer some of the questions it raises with hope, not fearmongering.

Next week, in Part 2, I ask the question the jury couldn’t answer: was Lindsay Clancy psychotic?

I put the same public record in front of a reproductive psychiatrist who treated nine women with postpartum psychosis at the same time last summer, and a treatment-resistant-depression specialist who read the medical timeline closer than anyone I know. They came back with opposite answers.

The Question the Jury Couldn’t Answer

“Society couldn’t handle believing that moms could have mental illness and kill their kids.” – Catherine Birndorf, M.D.

Catherine Birndorf is an evangelist among physicians. She created The Motherhood Center in New York City, and she leads it as someone who believes women deserve help for medical conditions. This includes when they’ve had children. More on her program below.

David Carreon, M.D. is both an evangelist among physicians and a devout Christian. He routinely wrestles with matters of ethics and morality, and treats some of the most unwell people on earth with lifesaving accelerated transcranial magnetic stimulation, guided by functional MRI, among other groundbreaking treatments. His center is Acacia Clinics.

Chris Aiken, M.D. , Will Sauve, M.D ., and Carlene MacMillan, M.D. also weighed in. ( Full disclosures for every guest are in Part 1. )

The mistrial in Lindsay Clancy’s murder case left us wrestling with the question the jury, conclusively, couldn’t answer with one voice.

The ground rules of this article are simple: none of the physicians quoted evaluated the patient. None of us were expert witnesses in the trial. And the experts I interviewed disagreed about whether the question the jury was asked can be answered at all.

Was Lindsay Clancy psychotic, definitively and knowably, in the 45-minute window in which she killed her children? Or was she, as the state was tasked with proving, criminally responsible at the time, based on her demonstrable sanity?

In keeping with the Goldwater rule, my expert guests are describing what postpartum psychosis is, from their clinical experience. They’re commenting on a public record that includes both sides’ forensic experts, and that has already been litigated, inconclusively, once. No one is making a diagnosis or rendering a verdict.

We are seeking to characterize an illness, not diagnose a person. All that out of the way, let’s get into the meat of it.

This is Part 2 in a multi-part series. Read the first part here [LINK].

Psychosis That Says Please and Thank You

Catherine Birndorf, M.D. is a reproductive psychiatrist and founder of The Motherhood Center, a program for women that works. Many of my former teammates now work with her, and they report back modern medical miracles: a partial hospitalization program with an on-site nursery, so new mothers are not separated from their children, for example. Intensive psychotherapy that doesn’t shy away from the bone-deep challenges of new parenthood, including postpartum depression and even psychosis. Her center has treated twenty to thirty cases of postpartum psychosis a year. Every year, for a decade now. She understands the struggles women face not from theory or one-off cases, but from clinical experience at scale.

NINE AT THE SAME TIME

Catherine: Look, I’m an unusual player on this field. At The Motherhood Center last summer, we had nine women at the same time with postpartum psychosis in some form of recovery. Nine at the same time. Right now, we have about five. We probably see between 20 and 30 cases a year, over ten years.

Owen: So even with it being theoretically rare, you have the services for the problem, so the problem finds you?

Catherine: They come to us. They step down from inpatient. They step up from outpatient when they’re not getting better. And you may not be sharing the delusional thinking — because you haven’t been asked, or because you know it’s going to be received poorly, or because you feel crazy saying it, or because you think your kid’s going to be taken away if you share it. But we’re going to do our damnedest to find it if we see you every day.

Owen: And what does it look like when you do find it?

Catherine: Here’s an important point: we believe postpartum psychosis to be an affective illness, not a primary psychotic illness. These are not people with schizophrenia or a primary psychotic disorder.

Owen: And how is that important here?

Catherine: Because postpartum psychosis does not always look floridly out of control. People want to see somebody looking — you know. She showed up at a party, drink in hand, blank and tired and obviously unwell, but you just keep showing up. It’s the motherhood myth: suck it up, push through, you look fine, you can do it all.

Owen: How possible is that? A woman who is psychotic — she can still go to a party?

Catherine: Oh, yeah.

THE MASK STAYS ON

Chris: Most of these psychoses related to mood disorder come with some preserved insight, and — I forget what you call it — people want to appear socially well.

Owen: Right. It’s awkward to say, “I’m feeling really psychotic today.”

Chris: We’re all taught this in dementia: only in the last stages do you lose your social propriety. A person with dementia will still say please and thank you. And people with psychosis do too. So they don’t let you know. They hide it. Instead, they come in and say:

“I’m anxious, I’m scattered, I can’t think straight, I can’t sleep—”

Owen: “…I have a problem with my attention.”

Chris: — all the things that are socially acceptable. Exactly. That’s what Lindsay Clancy would have said, and I would have treated her the same.

Chris Aiken, M.D. is not a novice. He’s among the most prominent physician-educators of our time, and he feels he might have made the same decisions as the clinicians who treated Lindsay Clancy. Even expert clinicians who knew what to look for might have faced a second hurdle. The phenomenon is shifting, waxing, waning, and doing anything but staying perfectly still so we can accurately diagnose it.

TWO-HOUR EPISODES

Chris: Now, this postpartum psychosis — which I believe Lindsay Clancy could have had; I can’t prove that, and the whole forensic thing is beyond me —

Owen: Luckily, you haven’t been called upon to do so.

Chris: That’s right. Postpartum psychosis is even more episodic than bipolar disorder. In bipolar, we think of episodes lasting at least two weeks. In postpartum psychosis, these so-called episodes might last two hours. It almost looks like a delirium picture — they come in and out of the psychotic state depending on the hour of the day. It can be very fragmented to capture that mental status.

Chris: And it’s devastating that the DSM removed postpartum psychosis in 1980. In doing so, they left clinicians with no guidance on how to recognize it, other than a specifier buried in the major depression label.

The incidence of postpartum psychotic symptoms is on the order of 1–2 cases per 1,000 births. At 3.6 million births per year, that is on the order of 3,600–7,200 cases in the US alone. The postpartum month carries roughly 23 times the risk of first-onset psychosis versus any other time in a woman’s life. Most of these episodes are attributable to bipolar illness, thus “affective” psychosis, and 43.5% of these cases remain isolated to the postpartum episode only. Most of the rest go on to develop a longitudinal course of bipolar disorder. However, postpartum psychosis, on its own, despite existing? It’s not in the DSM-5-TR. It was removed from the canonical manual in 1980. Of note, our guest Dr. Birndorf is on the task force advocating for its reinstatement when DSM-6 comes to pass.

“Rare” Is Doing a Lot of Work in That Sentence

Rare sounds like a description. In my mind, when I use the word, it means “1% prevalence or less.” It is more than that, too. “Rare” ends up being a permission slip to ignore a potentially catastrophic condition. Mental health conditions are the leading cause of pregnancy-related death in America, accounting for 23% of pregnancy-related deaths according to the CDC. So, while rare, these brain disorders are also the most common thing that will kill a new mother. Ahead of hemorrhage. Ahead of cardiomyopathy — which is also “rare” by my definition and ignored by approximately zero physicians who care for pregnant women. I used the word the way that made sense to me — but Dr. Birndorf put it in the appropriate context.

DON’T CALL IT RARE

Catherine: I want to go back for a second. I hate the word rare as it relates to postpartum psychosis.

Owen: I meant less than one percent of the population.

Catherine: Totally. And I’m taking issue with it anyway — I’m allowed to, because survivors don’t like it either. Rare makes it untouchable. No one is going to fund research on something that’s super rare. I’ll take uncommon. But if eight thousand women have it a year, how uncommon is it?

Owen: There’s a number for how uncommon it is — but it’s common enough that we should think about it all the time. Pulmonary embolism after a plane ride is also uncommon. And yet it’s part of the differential.

Catherine: Everything is part of the differential. Everything is screened for — gestational diabetes, hypertension, we worry about everything. It is still not mandated to screen during pregnancy for depression and anxiety. Twenty percent of people are going to have a perinatal mood or anxiety disorder — far more common than the things we screen for — and this is the one we leave to chance.

Catherine: And then go to afterwards. The leading cause of maternal mortality — pregnancy through the first year — is mental health. Roughly twice as deadly as the cardiovascular issues we treat as public health crises. Why is that not a public health crisis?

Owen: Well — then we couldn’t be putting women on trial.

Catherine: We wouldn’t put women on trial, because we would understand that this is a potential consequence of untreated medical illness. Mental illness is medical illness. Mind-body. They’re all one.

Owen: I think there’s a neck that connects them.

Catherine: Apparently. But not according to most.

The Case That She Was Psychotic

THE ILLNESS BELIEVES IT’S RIGHT

Owen: So some portion of people are so unwell that they hear command hallucinations. And a further subset are so additionally unwell that they believe what those voices say, and act on them without the scruples most of us would bring.

Catherine: It’s not even scruples. Scruples [imply] morality, or choice. You believe you’re right. It’s not up for debate. Go back to the Yates case — she believed what she was doing was right. This is a belief, held absolutely, that killing your children is the right thing to do — altruistic filicide. If you need to die because you’re a bad person, and you don’t want your kids to suffer a life without you, you might come to believe that taking them with you is mercy.

Owen: My analog for this recently is movement disorders. Nobody judges someone with a tremor for moving their hand. If all hand tremors were illegal and you had a movement disorder, you still couldn’t be held liable for that crime — you literally don’t control it.

STICKY THOUGHTS

Catherine: People are parsing intrusive thoughts right now. Everybody has them — intrusive violent thoughts, even. They come, they go, they’re gone, and you’re scared. Whoa. And then it moves on.

Owen: I’ve quit this job in my head more times than —

Catherine: Right. And then some people have them and they get stuck. They get very sticky, in an OCD way. People have OCD that is about self-harm, or harm to others. It’s part of the illness. And almost invariably, they don’t act. But since starting the Motherhood Center — I had never seen OCD verging into psychosis the way I have in the past ten years.

THREE DAYS WITHOUT SLEEP, ON SERTRALINE

Chris: When I start to see — I’m not sleeping for three days, on sertraline — then I start to suspect a manic psychotic process. I lump the two together, because most manias have psychotic features. And then I start to involve the family more, and dig and dig and dig, and see if this other stuff is going on. There was a lack of digging.

Owen: And I was struck that nobody at trial seized on that. I thought the lawyer would pounce — three days without sleep on sertraline — and say: ha, they missed mania.

Chris: I was personally surprised too. Something there — or he’s more strategic than I am. I don’t know.

Chris: And it can persist. Postpartum psychosis can still be present eight months after the pregnancy — about half of cases persist intermittently that way.

A young mother with ample protective factors develops escalating insomnia. She’s prescribed serotonergic drugs. She presents as socially competent to evaluator after evaluator — because this illness lets you. Then, in a single hour, her children are dead and she has attempted suicide violently enough to leave her paralyzed. And yet — a careful psychiatrist read the same record and… flinched.

45 Minutes, Once, and Never Again

Of my interviews, Dr. David Carreon spent the most time digging through the timeline and evidence presented in the case. He does not claim she was well. He was, however, forced into an uncomfortable corner. One claim made by the defense didn’t sit well.

THE VOICE CAME…ONCE

David: She reported that she heard a voice — something along the lines of: this is your last chance; you need to kill the children so you can kill yourself. She heard that, and then she followed it. The voice came sometime after her husband left — he left around 5:15 and was back by 6:09 — and it never came back once the killings were complete.

Owen: And if I’m hearing you right — if you’re the defense attorney, you have to make the case that psychiatry did it wrong. Because it gives the jury something else to blame.

David: Correct. So: did she flip into psychosis for literally 45 minutes, once in her whole lifetime, and then never again? Once in her whole life — had voices? That’s a little suspicious. And by a little suspicious, I mean that is unheard of. That just does not happen — in schizophrenia, in depression with psychosis, in any of it.

Owen: But a defense attorney isn’t actually arguing for the truth. You’re arguing for something that’s truthy, in Stephen Colbert’s terms. And the argument that’s probably closest to the truth — she was really sick; look how sick she was, she couldn’t even follow a doctor’s recommendation — is an unsatisfying case for a jury. It gives them no one to blame except the existential fear that any of us could get that sick.

David: Yep. And rumor has it, it was eleven to one. So did he make his point? Apparently. It was a very effective rhetorical argument.

David’s next point is about command auditory hallucinations as a category, not about this case. The category is worth understanding on its own terms: whether hearing a command compels obeying it is precisely the question a second jury would be asked to weigh. Per the ground rules I set out in Part 1, I cut his views on Lindsay Clancy’s case specifically — as I did for every guest in this series.

TWELVE PEOPLE WHO HAVE NEVER BEEN PSYCHOTIC

Owen: The jurors had to opine on the reasoning in her head — in a room full of people who’ve probably never been psychotic. The chance that anyone on that jury had experienced psychosis is low. Twelve never-psychotic people, opining on whether someone they’d just met in this horrific circumstance had something they were told was psychosis, with no expertise in it.

David: I do feel for the jury. And I’ve got plenty of patients — people I’ve treated and continue to treat with psychosis — who don’t kill people. The jury standard is really about human responsibility, a layperson’s understanding of insanity, not a technical one. Just because you hear a voice telling you to kill your kids doesn’t mean you have to kill your kids. And — command auditory hallucinations are scary. People who have them are far more likely to do the thing the voice says.

NOBODY SAW THAT COMING

David: The thing that was emotionally shocking to me, going visit by visit through the record: amitriptyline ten, amitriptyline twenty — and she kills her three kids. Even though I knew the ending, it was one of those moments reminding us, as psychiatrists and as a system, just how bad we are at predicting. In a sense, nobody saw that coming.

David: Was she in crisis? Yes — December was terrible. She wasn’t sleeping. Everybody was saying higher level of care. And maybe she was coming out of it. Or maybe this was a first-onset bipolar disorder triggered by the SSRI. Maybe the psychosis was bubbling. Maybe this was a mania that everybody missed.

David: She was not well, and she was not adequately treated.

The timeline in evidence has her husband leaving around 5:15 p.m., returning at 6:09, and dialing 911 two minutes later. “The voice” entered the legal record almost two weeks after the killings, on a February 6 call to her husband that she placed from her doctor’s cell phone with a defense-retained psychologist present.

Eleven to One, Everywhere You Look

Both Dr. David Carreon and Dr. Catherine Birndorf have engaged meaningfully with the facts presented in Lindsay Clancy’s case. Both doctors agree that the outcome was horrific, and that the standard of care needs to be better. Dr. Birndorf reads the record and sees a recognizable illness: one that explains the history, hides behind social competence, and ends with a woman going out a second-story window headfirst. Due to what I understand as the peculiar nature of the not criminally responsible standard in the Commonwealth of Massachusetts, a standard that rewards emphasizing command hallucinations, the defense leaned harder on the voice than the clinical picture alone would have required. Dr. Carreon is understandably dubious of that voice: one that arrived once, and left, forever. And yet it was Dr. Carreon himself who named the diagnostic entity in which both doctors could be right.

In the very same week the Clancy trial deadlocked, a Bronx judge found another defendant, Dimone Fleming, not responsible for killing her two sons. In that case, evaluators for both the defense and the prosecution, including the prosecution’s own psychiatrist, agreed the patient was grossly psychotic. To make a pedantic point, this is a different outcome than we had in the Lindsay Clancy case. Why? There was an observable difference: Fleming was found at the scene in the midst of a florid psychosis and evaluated while the illness was still visible. Clancy, to the detriment of her defense, went out a window and into an unconscious state, where the facts of her underlying mental state were unresolvable in the immediate aftermath of her children’s deaths and her suicide attempt. Fleming is Black, and was living in a shelter. Clancy is white, from the affluent town of Duxbury, Massachusetts. Fleming wasn’t taking her medication either. And yet, somehow, it was still agreeable to both sides to find her not criminally responsible. One final point: Fleming’s case drew a fraction of the attention Clancy’s did. Make of that what you will.

Eleven jurors read the record in the Lindsay Clancy case one way, one completely another. My experts didn’t reach consensus either. And none of those jurors are likely to have suffered a psychotic illness, or to have known anyone with postpartum psychosis before this case.

I’m uncomfortable living in a world where our legal machinery obligates one side to argue that throwing yourself out a window was a performance. It’s possible our legal standards need to evolve, in addition to our standard of care. Had someone from the care system been observing Lindsay Clancy at the hour in question, the legal question would have been answerable. Had someone from the care system been with her at that hour, there might have been no legal question at all. That is not a courtroom problem. That is a care problem, and it’s where this series goes next.

I graduated college in 2001. I remember that day. Many other people remember that day. It has become an anchor, but maybe not the kind we expected. It was the end of peace, and the modern, at least the US military is concerned. The global war on terror created an operational tempo that was not the same as before that day. That operational tempo has continued for many of our special operations forces. Special operations warriors are asked to do things that no one else can do, and they do them routinely because that's their job.

Most of these warriors enlisted after September 11, 2001. Now, service members with 25 years of service enlisted after they knew they were signing up. This was the end of “summer” and the beginning of a protracted winter for service members compared to the rest of America. Most of us will remember Covid-19 as something that changed the world. For the military, it was September 11.

There are new injuries from this conflict. There are far fewer deaths, making it less evident that a war is going on. There is a saying about war: “The only winner is medicine.”

We have learned lessons of the past and learned more about saving people from gunshots, improvised explosives, and other massive and life-altering injuries than we were ever able to before. The casualties are incredibly high in these conflicts. The casualties are also extremely high due to the operational training required for these remarkable missions against asymmetrical adversaries.

I had the pleasure of being welcomed at Homebase in Boston on 9/11/24. Homebase is a collaboration between Massachusetts General Hospital and the Boston Red Sox Foundation and provides neuropsychiatric services to special operations forces, and their families. It's not part of the VA, it's not paid by insurance— it's all grant-funded. Multimodal care, research, and treatment aim to create the most excellent care our Warfighters could want.

Share

I'm going to write about one of those hidden wounds. This will not be about Post-traumatic stress disorder. That's obvious. We all get the veterans get PTSD.

By the way, I'm saying Warrior and warfighter because I am following the custom—soldier refers to somebody who is in the army, marine refers to a marine, airman relates to someone in the air force, and sailor—or sometimes just SEAL—refers to somebody in the Navy. These services contribute warriors to our special operations command, but once you're a special operator, you are the weapon system . There's less boundary between services—the deployments can consist of service members from across branches of the military times, so it's easier to stay warrior to encompass everyone.

These remarkable humans have learned how to fight in a way that maintains their ability to be incomprehensibly lethal. They are not always ordered to kill, they have rules engagement that are much more robust than our domestic police forces. They have lawyers dictating what they can do on the battlefield. But sometimes, warriors kill.

While doing their job, they get blown up a lot. There's a specific kind of injury from being exposed to blasts. Common parlance includes the term “chronic traumatic encephalopathy,” which is the kind of brain injury that people in the NFL have been reported to get from repeated concussion. That's a different kind of injury, pathophysiologically, than what is present in warfighters. They get concussion injuries as well, but a lot of the wounds to their brain have to do with sound waves.

These sound waves are because they're standing next to mortars and tanks, holding rifles, blowing open doors, getting hit with improvised explosive devices, and crucially, they experience all of this in training missions as well.

In training, they jump out of helicopters, ride on fast boats, shoot live ammunition, and practice. They get good at their job. They get ready to fight a war well before they fight the war. To be able to get up in the middle of the night and ship out in under three hours, to accomplish the impossible anywhere in the world, it turns out that it takes a lot of practice. And that practice exposed them to blast trauma to their brain and body as well.

We have a specific name for this. It’s called repeated blast exposure. Ironically, it's similar to the shell shock that we named in World War I, which we assumed wasn't that. The scholarship has advanced , and the medical understanding is coming along with it:

Thanks to the scholarship, we now know there are differences between the injuries sustained from getting hit in the head and having the concussive force of sound pressure waves shaking the brain inside your head. Helmets that were supposed to protect you from shrapnel, thanks to reverb within the helmet, might also make you more likely to sustain this sort of injury. There's a lot we need to learn. Mitigating risk needs to include maintaining lethality.

The risk of suicide in warriors is high. Some of these suicides are from psychiatric illnesses, but in the special operations community, these lesions in their brain have distinct pathophysiology, and like all physical illnesses, they predispose individuals to suicide. Yes, that's right, all physical disease makes you more likely to die by suicide. It's not just mental illness, it's physical illness too. Brain injuries are more likely to die by suicide, also.

Our Warriors have undertaken dangerous careers. That's well-known to anybody who signs up to be a serious warrior. It's our job, as a society, to fund the science and medicine that will protect them from harm to the degree possible and heal them when they hurt. Most importantly, we need to understand their culture and the sacrifices they've made to understand their injuries appropriately in their context.

The world changed on 9/11, but for some of us, the changes were more profound than others.
