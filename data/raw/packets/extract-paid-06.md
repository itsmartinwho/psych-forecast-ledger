Dear readers, what follows is a piece of personal narrative medicine. It is for the purpose of helping people learn biology and how it interacts with stigma, perception, employment law, and the stories we tell ourselves.

In August and September of 2015, I began my child psychiatry fellowship at the NYU School of Medicine. My first rotation was on the child psychiatry unit at Bellevue Hospital which specializes in young children ages 3 to 11. It is a unit called 21 South.

One of the things that pediatricians learn in their Internship is that kids are sick…often. We learn by also being infected in their presence.

In my second month of fellowship, I got strep throat. I was out for a couple of days. When I returned, one of the kids was complaining of a sore throat. I promptly ordered a group A streptococcus rapid test. I administered it with a gag-inducing swab. Patient Zero! I found him! He was promptly treated with antibiotics. His “psychiatric” symptoms—severe irritability, among other things—also resolved rapidly. Could there have been a connection?

How is that for clumsy foreshadowing?

Streptococcus is a common bacteria. It is more pernicious—sometimes— than one can imagine. After a situation like mine, it’s hard to forget:

Streptococcus is an autoimmune disease waiting to happen.

Answers Footnoted1

And once you get strep throat once, you're likely to get strep throat again. That is what happened to me. Group A streptococcus is common. Group C streptococcus also exists. We regularly test for Group A streptococcus with those rapid strep tests you're familiar with. One can only determine Group C on bacterial culture.

The next time I got sick, it was actually an employment issue that brought it to light. I was very, very tired. I was also moonlighting because resident doctors don't make very much money. I had graduated adult psychiatry residency so I could work as an Adult Psychiatry Attending, which pays a heck of a lot better than the $15 an hour you make as a child psychiatry fellow. So I had worked 2 x 12-hour shifts, back to back, in the emergency room of another hospital system in New York City many weekends.

One of my colleagues noted I was quite irritable on one of those shifts, and assuming it was my bipolar disorder acting up, made a report to senior management.

The morning of that second shift, I was exhausted. More tired than I have ever remembered. I did not start drinking coffee until my psychiatric residency began, and even then not much. But this morning, I had a cold brew. Next, I had a nitro cold brew. This second drink was augmented with an espresso shot. And then I had another cold brew. Then I got to work.

See notes for answers!2

It is worth noting I've never had a recreational drug in my life, and I've never been drunk, ever. I’ve never even had a drink. “Being intoxicated” is not something that is obvious to me from related prior experience. Let me report: caffeine intoxication exists! Was it obvious that this article was going to turn into continuing medical education?

Here is information about caffeine:

Caffeine is a stimulant that has been utilized globally for centuries secondary to its ability to improve mental alertness. As many as 85% of Americans consume caffeine daily with the average cup of coffee containing anywhere from 40 to 150 mg of caffeine.

Here are the diagnostic criteria for caffeine intoxication:

Firstly, the patient must have recently consumed a high dose of caffeine - well above 250mg. They must then display five or more of the following symptoms either during or shortly after consumption:

• Nervousness

• Restlessness

• Excitement

• Facial flushing

• Digestive complaints

• Increased or irregular heart rate

• Diuresis

• Insomnia

• Muscle twitching

• Nervous energy

• Periods of tirelessness

The symptoms identified must cause the patient significant distress or impair them in some way, such as in their social interactions, at work, or in some other important area of functioning.

Then there is boilerplate about how it's not due to something else (like everything else in the DSM-5), and then:

In some cases, individuals may experience some mild sensory disturbance like ringing in the ears after a high dose of caffeine, and while large doses can make the heart beat faster, smaller amounts of caffeine can reduce the heart rate.

The text goes on to indicate that usually, individuals can identify that they've had a lot of caffeine. In this exciting story about the interplay of biology, subjective experience, phenomenology, physiology, and employment law, I was not yet able to figure out that I’d had too much coffee.

A systematic assessment of my drinks is as follows:

The USDA reports that a single shot of espresso (1 oz/29.6ml) contains about 63mg of caffeine, while a double shot (2 oz/59.2ml) has 126mg.

So that double shot went into a nitro cold brew:

A 2017 study found that a 12-oz cup of cold brew coffee can contain between 153–238 mg of caffeine.

Cold brew coffee is made without heat. To make it, a person needs to steep coffee grounds in water at room temperature for 8–24 hours.

This is a different process than the way to make iced coffee, which simply involves serving brewed coffee over ice.

So we're already at 238mg + 126 mg on the first cup. But what about that nitro cold brew?

It is a little bit more, around 280mg, and of note, Starbucks blocks people from buying the large version:

given this is a love story of chemistry and biology in the training of physicians, follow along!

So by the time I walked into the emergency room for my second shift in two days, feeling very, very tired, I had already ingested 644 mg of caffeine. A later examination of my heart rate (thanks to my Apple Watch) revealed tachycardia to the 130s throughout most of that shift.

I also, and this is embarrassing, swore at a cop. The police should not have brought their guns loaded into a psychiatric emergency room.

These are issues, I maintain, about which any attending physician could reasonably take issue. Part of my point is that this defensive stance isn’t what it’s about! What can our behavior tell us about the biology at play at any given moment? Let’s find out!

I was the “charge attending,” thus running Psych ER at that time. I could've been more polite about it, but, crucially, I wasn’t.

The words:

“Don’t ever bring a loaded fucking gun into my ER”

...came out of my mouth. I subsequently had to speak to management about it.3 A physician using such uncouth language with law enforcement? I'm not proud, and I'm not defending it. It was not in character.

And it was obvious to me, upon discussion with Dr. J. a remarkably compassionate manager that this was off.4 I started to get curious. Why was I so off? How much coffee had I had?

And, as it turns out, this is what it means when a diagnostic manual says “impairment in a work setting.”

I was caffeine intoxicated!

They were worried I had a manic episode. That is obvious. I have bipolar disorder, and this suspicion is the “go-to” worry.

My maxim about this worry is a simple one, and it explains my longevity in the field of medicine without concerns about my ability to practice medicine5.

“When someone asks if you might be manic, the answer is yes, until proven otherwise.”

Irritability is a common symptom of mania. People with bipolar disorder can have a loss of insight into their symptoms. This is called anosognosia. It’s not a helpful name for a problem. I don’t love this video. I don’t love coercive care. But it is clear:

Not knowing you have a problem is a problem. It can wax and wane in bipolar disorder. Here is my summary recommendation:

If the question is “Might you be having symptoms of mania”?

“Yes” is a great default. It allows more appropriate and aggressive management of even maybe symptoms!

Another answer, “NO, WHAT THE HELL IS WRONG WITH YOU?”—is common.

It is a worse default. It is off-putting. The fact is, bipolar mania or depression can sneak up on you. By placing more behavior into the “default yes” bucket, people with bipolar will do better than those with a default of “no.”

It is the mental illness equivalent of “Is it a good idea to check if the jet plane has all safety systems working before take off?” Yes, and LET US CHECK is a safe answer. NO can lead to disaster. “NO, I’M FINE” risks missing opportunities for wellness before a disaster.

In this case, however, the “until proven otherwise” part of my maxim proved important as well! I thought back: I noticed the following:

Excessive Tiredness and somnolence lead to drinking more coffee. These are no mania symptoms. Reduced sleep drive is. Hmmm…

Tachycardia is not a mania symptom. The sustained nature was more in keeping with caffeine intoxication than transient anger.

Exposure to (a remarkable all-time high dose, not that I knew it at the time) caffeine, could explain:

Nervousness

Flushing

Irritability

“periods of tirelessness” aka finishing the shift.

Oh…Caffeine intoxication. However, a person who has never been intoxicated in any way? They will have less insight into it—anosognosia for way too much coffee6! The differential diagnosis of my own behavior…supported this answer. The practice of medicine, and in particular psychiatry, is about more than just any one behavior. However, I believe the differential diagnosis of my irritability strongly supported caffeine intoxication. The question as to why I was drinking so much coffee in the first place turned out to be... more important.

I went to the doctor. I had strep throat all over again. I also had the Epstein-Barr virus. This had not been picked up on my initial evaluation because we didn't look for it. The rapid strep test was considered enough of an answer for why I felt bad. But I had mononucleosis for the first time in my life. And thus, I was exhausted.

Three weeks later my body broke out in a rash. This turned out to be the first concrete symptom of psoriasis, which was kicked off by the streptococcal infection. The psoriatic rash was actually psoriatic arthritis. A condition I would deal with for the rest of my life.

I was very tired for a really complex reason. And that's why I had so much coffee. Going beyond the first “why” is where answers might lie.

Keep going; much like cold brew coffee, taking your time answer any question is worth it.

—Owen Scott Muir, M.D.

Hey, the audience of people who read my things. I will share a little bit about my process.

Writing is hard. It's really easy to write something, anything. In that, you can plug whatever you want to be written into ChatGPT, it will be. And it will suck.

But writing something novel, compelling, interesting, concise, funny, or all of those things is not automatic. Not yet. Maybe someday. But it's not revelatory writing that I am able to get done for you today.

Some of the things I want to say are more simple than others. Some of the things I want to write about are complicated and require references.

It requires editing. It requires thought. And some days, I just spent a lot of time on the things that are not going to get there.

Today is one of those days. So I'm writing this throwaway piece to disclose that it took too long to edit other things to make them better.

I write because it matters. I imagine that the people who read the things I write might have their outlook changed. And some of those things can be silly, but some of them need to be written well.

The stakes are too high to write poorly.

So I'm just writing this piece today to let you know that I spend a long time on some pieces of writing. And it is because I value your opinion. And I value the time you spend reading the things that I write.

Out of respect, you're not gonna get to read any of them yet. That is my “it’s not good enough” promise to you, dear readers.

The proposed rule, which has received almost 30k comments including one from yours truly, still needs to have all of those public comments, including ones filed on paper with physical mail, read!

Why the act requires an in-person visit is not obvious so I will explain it. And it's about making somebody's day easier!

This law was an administrative workaround. It made the DEA have an easier time dealing with online pill mills. The requirement for an in-person visit is categorical: either a visit happened, or it didn't. This requirement does not exist because it is clinically necessary. The DEA are not clinicians. However, it does mean that if a categorical variable, visit/no visit, landed on the no visit side of the coin, they save themselves the trouble of an administrative review when revoking a license. The requirement existed to save time for the DEA. That's it. Absent this categorical reason to pull somebody's DEA license, they must thoroughly review whether the prescribing was appropriate, and every case is tiresome. It's easier to say they broke a rule about a categorical variable.

Notably, the likelihood that in-office visits will immediately return for everybody everywhere is low.

I'm not saying that because I have a Palantir (“of Isengard” not “of the Bay Area”) or other off-brand crystal ball. But, I am confident about that statement because I understand that somebody would probably lose their job if that's what happened.

To let the public health emergency expire with no action from the DEA as it relates to the millions of people who are now getting their controlled substances prescribed via telehealth? That would be a public health disaster; somebody would look like a huge dope, there would be congressional hearings, somebody would get fired, and other people would be embarrassed.

If you work at the DEA, you are, by definition, a Narc. I don't mean that pejoratively. I suggest that you probably care about things being done in an appropriate and regulatory-compliant way. Not looking like you created a tidal wave of death and misery? That's a good look for Narcs!

I love writing about compliance. And today, in a moment of sadness and pride, I brought up a relevant compliance issue. It related to my role on a planning committee for CME. Next, I promptly resigned.

I've been working with my colleagues at AACAP for the past six or more years to help build the Clinical Essentials Task Force into an official committee. We make continuing medical education content. And, for that content to count, it needs to be planned by those who don’t have conflicts of interest with Ineligible Companies.

And, importantly, even the committees planning those educational products not only must disclose conflicts of interest but, if they exist, we can not be involved in planning. At all.

Today, I tendered my resignation after raising the compliance concern about my participation. I think this is not a brilliant standard…because it essentially penalizes compliance with disclosure, but also because I don’t believe it meaningfully protects anyone from actual conflict or bias. My feeling about that, however, isn't what is essential. I DO NOT GET TO MAKE THAT RULE. I have to follow it. Because it’s a privilege to be a doctor and to make medical education content, that privilege is born of years, decades, and centuries of lessons learned, revised, updated, and complied with.

Professional AI development in a role with the FDA means no CME planning for me. Pictured discussions between myself and an AI duck on Bluesky.

It’s not my right. It’s an honor that I ennoble with my identification of a problem and resignation. It’s the system working.

Here was the problem:

Companies that are ineligible to be accredited in the ACCME System (ineligible companies) are those whose primary business is producing, marketing, selling, re-selling, or distributing healthcare products used by or on patients.

Examples include:

• Bio-medical startups that have begun a governmental regulatory approval process

And a company I work with just got an NIH SBIR Grant to do such a thing. And others to whom I advise have products in the market. And my wife works for another. And I have a few shares of this or that.

Here are the standards that AACAP, as an accredited provider, has to comply with:

The accredited provider must ensure that all decisions related to the planning, faculty selection, delivery, and evaluation of accredited education are made without any influence or involvement from the owners and employees of an ineligible company.

And the language is unambiguous: WITHOUT ANY INFLUENCE OR INVOLVEMENT FROM OWNERS AND EMPLOYEES.

I’m both. So, no matter how earnest my intentions are or how bias free I Imagine myself, I can’t be in the room when they plan.

So I immediately resigned. Didn’t finish the call. Didn’t stay for any planning.

And this article is itself documentation of an active compliance process:

4. Mitigate relevant financial relationships: Take steps to prevent all those with relevant financial relationships from inserting commercial bias into the content.

a. Mitigate relationships prior to the individuals assuming their roles. Take steps appropriate to the role of the individual. For example, steps for planners will likely be different than for faculty and would occur before planning begins.
b. Document the steps taken to mitigate relevant financial relationships.

Which this article serves to do!

Experts will have industry involvement and conflicts of interest in the AI-driven healthcare age. In addition, current regulations prevent these experts from generating continuing medical education content for colleagues, hindering knowledge-sharing.

A refined understanding of conflicts of interest and effective risk mitigation is necessary. We need a system that meaningfully reduces bias while allowing experts to educate doctors on using advanced tools. The current approach, excluding experts due to affiliations, restricts the dissemination of cutting-edge science among physicians.

This system is limited. It's limited by design. But it's not limited with an eye toward the future. To use 's brilliant construct, the rationale for excluding industry-involved experts from CME creation is a Weak Link solution for a Strong Link problem:

Weak-link problems are problems where the overall quality depends on how good the worst stuff is. You fix weak-link problems by making the weakest links stronger, or by eliminating them entirely.

And uses music as a counter-example:

Some problems are strong-link problems: overall quality depends on how good the best stuff is, and the bad stuff barely matters. Like music, for instance. You listen to the stuff you like the most and ignore the rest.

And he goes on to argue that ignorable sciences are most of it, but we don't need to focus on reducing the risk that will be exposed to low Fidelity science; we need to focus on enhancing the possibility the groundbreaking will come to the fore.

Similarly, I posit it for medical education:

What we're solving for, historically, is reducing the bias that crappy interventions will be promoted by hucksters, which is what these policies solve for. But, unfortunately, we simultaneously exclude the most motivated and innovative people drawn away from academia into the world of bringing those innovative products to market.

In a world with only lame medical interventions, it makes sense to keep the snake oil salesman out of the room when planning who should get what education related to snake oil. In a world with life-changing and breakthrough science, getting excellent education about that science is probably better for society. We might care less about ensuring nobody covertly promotes their own snake oil.

Psychiatric hospital care and general medical hospital care are paid for in completely different ways. In fact, on the financial backend, there are even two different types of payment models for psychiatric hospitals.

In every other medical condition, the payment model follows the same basic principles: (the patient) may get a hospital bill, and it's got a bunch of line items, and it's for a phenomenal amount of money, and then it gets knocked down to this or that.

This is Live Action Role Playing on the part of Big Health.

The truth? This that's not how any of the actual bills from Medicare or other payers are paid. The bill you see is a legal truth and practical fiction… if you have insurance. The song and dance and $400 Tylenol are all there to obscure the fact that everything is paid differently:

• Diagnosis-Related Group (DRG): Under the DRG system, patients are classified into groups based on primary ICD-10 diagnostic code, surgical procedures, age, etc. Each DRG group has a fixed reimbursement rate. Hospitals receive a single payment per patient per DRG. This system “encourages efficiency and cost containment,” but at least it is internally consistent. It’s the prix fix menu approach to hospital payments.

So for medical hospitalizations, they're getting a lump sum. Examples (estimates, not exact numbers):

• Heart failure and shock (DRG 291): This is a relatively common condition, but it can be serious. It might have a weight of about 0.7.

• Major joint replacement or reattachment of lower extremity without major complications or comorbidities (DRG 470): This is a surgical procedure that might have a weight of about 2.0.

• Tracheostomy with mechanical ventilation 96+ hours with major operating room procedure (DRG 207): This represents a very severe condition that requires significant resources. It might have a weight of about 8.0.

Below is the 2023 from is the AHA. The 2024 CMS data is here.

Each DRG's weight is then multiplied by a base payment rate, which is about $5000+ ish from Medicare. This can get multiplied by a LOT MORE in major payer contracting1.

The hospital has every incentive theoretical incentive to take care of you in the most cost-efficient manner.

All of the negotiations is around the multiplier the hospital gets for the management of any given DRG in their setting. These multipliers for any DRG payment are considered proprietary by third-party payers.

Psychiatric hospitals don't work this way. They don't work one way. There are two different models with two different fee structures:

• Per Diem: Under the per diem system, hospitals receive a daily rate for each day a patient is in the facility. This rate is meant to cover the costs of care, including room and board, nursing care, and other services.

• Inpatient Psychiatric Facility Prospective Payment System (IPF PPS): The IPF PPS is a Medicare payment system specifically designed for inpatient psychiatric facilities. It determines payment rates based on a patient's diagnosis, age, and other factors, similar to the DRG system.

Similar to the DRG System Isn’t the Same As The DRG System.

There was no financial mechanism to pay for any innovative treatment in psychiatric hospitals…(yes, this is foreshadowing!)

General medical hospitals have a bunch of payments factored into the care of a cardiac patient. That care can involve MRI scanners, surgical interventions, Extracorporeal Membrane Oxygenation, and associated wizardry of modern medicine. If you come into the hospital with a heart attack, they factor in the cost of the cardiac catheterization lab that will save your life.

The separate billing schemes for psychiatry, until the 2024 CMS update that inspired this article, never imagined any technology being brought to inpatient psychiatric care. When I previously complained about the archaic nature of inpatient psychiatry, I did not understand this. Even the admittedly very nerdy healthcare economics enthusiast author of this column did not know these were different systems from a payment standpoint, much less two separate systems.

The practical upshot? The whole game for hospitals is about getting the most financially viable care per patient. The healthcare payers had the incentive to “deny days.”

There was no incentive on the part of inpatient psychiatry to get the person out the door quickly, efficiently, and well.

And that is on the verge of changing…Follow for More!

—O. Scott Muir, M.D.
