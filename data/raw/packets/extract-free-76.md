The data is worth reading. However, instead of writing about what kind of a product Proliv Rx is…I’m going to make myself useful. I’m going to define terms—because it’s getting really confusing. Neuromodulation is getting to be as bad as therapies. eCOT-AS, tDCS, DBS, ECT, TMS, nVNS, tFUS, VNS, dTMS, rTMS, eTNS, fTUB.

I just made up fTUB. You probably didn’t even notice!

I'll start with eCOT-AS, because that's what ProLivᵀᴹ Rx is—but as a bonus, I will do the the other acronyms too.

eCOT-AS: External Combined Occipital and Trigeminal Afferent nerve Stimulation.

This technology allows the use of electricity to gently stimulate sensory nerves on your head. One of the quirks of the sensory nerves that allow you to feel anything on your head? It is that not all of them are cranial nerves! Your forehead and face are innervated by the trigeminal nerve for sensory input into the brain. But the back of your head? That's actually a sensory nerve that sneaks up from where the spinal cord exits around your shoulders through what we call the brachial plexus, and then travels back up to be the sensory nerves (so you can feel anything on the back of your head), which, in anatomy lab, is called the occiput. Thus, occipital nerves are really spinal nerves that took a U-turn.

So, this device stimulates the sensory nerves on the front of your head, using two separate divisions of the trigeminal nerve, and the back of your head, which gets into your brain via a spinal sensory nerve. If that sounds like a lot of sensory nerve bandwidth, that's because it is. This allows delicately pulsed electrical energy to re-synchronize deep brain circuits in a way that's really efficient when it comes to the amount of electricity needed, which means the whole thing runs a rechargeable battery in the device. It “tickles” your nerves, and the pattern of tickling can reset depression. Neurolief did this once before with a product called Relivion MG , for migraine.

tDCS: Transcutaneous Direct Current Stimulation

Batteries have an anode and a cathode. Current flows from the anode to the cathode. tDCS uses direct current, goes through your skin (“transcutaneous”) and into your brain.. Then, if you're the Flow neuroscience device, you delivers a direct current stimulation to your brain, which, according to the FDA can help with depression, as long as it hasn't demonstrated itself to be hard to treat.

The mechanism difference between eCOT-AS and tDCS? eCOT-AS stimulates sensory nerves to change the brain. tDCS directly stimulates the brain to change it.

DBS: Deep Brain Stimulation.

Deep brain stimulation involves a neurosurgeon implanting, a long, thin stimulator deep in the brain (usually bilaterally), and then a battery pack and digital brain for that simulator somewhere in your chest, and then connecting wires to those leads. This allows us to put brain stimulation deep in the brain, and that's necessary for some conditions like Parkinson's disease, and maybe necessary for OCD, essential tremor, and dystonia, as well as other conditions in the future, like depression. But it's Neurosurgery. Most of these neurosurgical procedures use patterned pulses of stimulation to regulate a neural circuit of interest. In OCD, it's used to turn down the function of the brain circuit that keeps you worrying when left to its own devices .

DBS, illustrated.

ECT: ElectroConvulsive Therapy

This procedure uses electricity between an anode and a cathode at a relatively high dose to induce a seizure. This is done under anesthesia, so the body lays still, but the brain is having a generalized seizure. It turns out that seizure is a way to treat psychiatric disorders, and it's also a treatment for epilepsy that you can't control with medicines safely. When we induce seizures, we make them less likely to happen on their own. So we often use ECT in pregnancy to protect women and their in utero almost babies from the exposure to antiepileptic drugs.

It's the seizure that's therapeutic, but you're under anesthesia when you get this treatment. It requires a doctor, anesthesiology, and a PACU, and is dramatically under utilized in current psychiatric practice, despite decades of demonstrated efficacy.

More details on ECT as a depression treatment are available here.

TMS/rTMS: Transcranial Magentic Stimulation

TMS is a procedure that uses an electromagnet to induce electrical fields in nearby wires. In your brain, instead of doing it in literal wires, it induces firing in neurons, which have long axons, which are functionally wires. The r in rTMS is repetitive, which is barely relevant any more, but the origional TMS devices were fired only once to stimulate the brain once, as a measurement tool in neurophysiology labs.

A nice summary of side effects of TMS is available here.

VNS: Vagus Nerve Stimulation

This is also Neurosurgery. Instead of implanting, a stimulator in the brain itself, a stimulator is implanted in the vagus nerve, the cranial nerve that goes from the brain to the body and controls the “rest and digestive” (parasympathetic) nervous system, as well as a bunch of other things on the way down. The Vagus nerve is really one of the top 12 cranial nerves. It may be even among the top 12 nerves in the body. It's a leading nerve. There is evidence to support it's used in epilepsy, depression, and clinical trials going on right now in bipolar depression.

It's got one of those battery packs, that you have planted into the chest, just like DBS, but there's no drilling into your skull part of the procedure. I’ve written about it’s use in Rheumatoid arthritis before.

nVNS: Non-invasive Vagus Nerve Stimulation

We learned about the vagus nerve above! This is non-surgical peripheral (on the neck) vagus nerve stimulation. There are a number of non-prescription version of this approach, but the only prescription one I know if is the GammaCore device, FDA-labeled for Migraine, Hemicrania Continua, and Cluster Headache and is going through an FDA breakthrough process for PTSD. I wrote about that data here.

I’ve published on a novel use of nVNS in very hard to treat depression here.

dTMS: deep Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation

This is repetitive TMS (rTMS) with a patented H-coil, and the choice of the word “deep” may or may not be a marketing decison.

Regardless, it’s a variety of coil geometries for inducing magnetic fields in the brain.

eTNS: external Trigeminal Nerve Stimulation

This is like eCOT-AS, but just eT-AS. The brand name is the Monarch eTNS device, and it is an FDA-cleared breakthrough device for refractory seizures, and ADHD in youth.

I’ve written about this a lot. I’m looking forward to new data on large scale replication trials that should be published soon.

The Frontier Psychiatrists is a daily health-themed newsletter. This article was going to start as an email to my colleague, Olu Ajilore, M.D. Like most of my colleagues, we originally met on Clubhouse. I’m Owen Muir, M.D., DFAACAP. I’ve been treating patients with TMS treatment since 2017 in my practice. I have a vested interest , and what follows is a detailed explanation of why, in terms of efficacy and cost-effectiveness. This is an updated version of this argument, which I first made in 2024.

Subscribe now

Medicaid plans face a difficulty—which is true of all health plans, but it is particularly true of state Medicaid plans. They don't have infinite money. Medicaid is the health insurance for people who are poor. I wish there were a better way to put it. Medicare and Medicaid were created with a compromise. Medicare, which covers predominantly the care of the elderly, would be covered at the federal level. Medicaid, which covers payment for healthcare for people, without commercial health insurance, who are predominantly poor, would be handled on a state-by-state basis.

I don't think it's a complicated argument to explain that some states have more money, and some states have less money. That money, on a state-by-state basis, needs to be used to cover the care of people who don't have commercial health insurance because they don't have a job. Some states have more of these individuals, and some states have less.

The rate for Medicare reimbursement was set at the federal level, but each state can choose how much they pay for their Medicaid population, and it's almost always less than the Medicare rate. Much of the source of healthcare access disparities in America began here, in 1965, with this compromise.

To be explicit: this was a cruel compromise from the start. Wealthy, people who were old wanted to make sure their health insurance would cover their care. Individual states, many in the south with poor and (ahem) minority populations, and wanted the ability to pay less for the healthcare healthcare if they chose. To get Medicare passed, Medicaid had to be a state-by-state choice.

That was how it was born, I'm not saying it's administered in a way that is intended to be racist at this time. There is an inequity built into the system, to this day.

One of the things that is true about Medicaid plans, that is not true about Medicare, is that people don't stop being old all of a sudden. Once you start being eligible for Medicare, you continue being eligible for Medicare. Medicare is for people who don't stop being old. Medicaid does not have the same stability. Sometimes you get a job, and get commercial insurance, and you are no longer on the same health plan anymore. 100% of people who get commercial health insurance because they got an awesome job stop being on their Medicaid plan. This means that the time anybody sends in a Medicaid plan, on average, is less than the time they spend on other plans. And this is a glitch in the matrix, because the risk doesn’t stay put in the same risk pool. Medicaid plans have, on average, only 10 months to manage the health of any individual. After this time, they either get a job or switch to a new Medicaid plan.

It's tough to manage population health when you only have months to accomplish the goal:

We also found that Medicaid and CHIP beneficiaries were enrolled for an average of 11.6 months over a 12-month enrollment span

You have to choose what to pay for, and what not to pay for if you're a plant administrator of a state Medicaid plan. But you can't do anything that's going to get people well. Because almost nothing gets people well in an average of 10 months.

We have data that demonstrate psychiatric interventions t hat work rapidly might provide the most robust savings for the economics of Medicaid.

Dual-eligible participants—those are individuals with both serious disabilities, so they qualify for Medicare through the Social Security disability program, with Medicaid as secondary insurance, who might be the best suited for rapid-acting mental health treatments.

Transcranial, magnetic stimulation is that treatment, today .

We can't waive a wand and make American healthcare cost less overnight. We can't put more money into state coffers to cover healthcare magically. Absent single-payer healthcare at the federal level, and massive reforms, these plan administrators have to work with the cards they are dealt. A crucial task is determining who's going to stay in a plan, and who's not. I would argue that dual-eligible recipients of Medicaid funds, those who were both disabled and can't work because of it, might be the least likely to change plans. 1

Given the economics of Medicaid, every healthcare expense is taking a match, and burning money, unless it generates savings in a very short time frame to be cost-effective. TMS works exactly this way.

Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation gets treatment-resistant depression to remission .

It does this reliably, in massive data sets. For example with dTMS, in 1753 patients at 21 sites:

• 30 sessions of Deep TMS led to 81.6% response and 65.3% remission rate.

• 20 sessions led to 73.6% response and 58.1% remission rate.

• iTBS led to 72.4% response and 69.2% remission. 2

It is better, in ASCERTAIN-TRD, a well-designed 2024 published RCT, than all other augmentation strategies assessed: 3

Accelerated TMS approaches have FDA approval with the SAINT system . Reviews of all available data in 2024 also agree. 4

These accelerated approaches (using shorter and more effective iTBS protocols) are also cost-effective:

From a healthcare system perspective, the average cost per patient was USD $1,108 (SD 166) for a course of iTBS and $1,844 (SD 304) for 10Hz rTMS, with an incremental net savings of $735 (95% CI 688 to 783).

The average cost per remission was $3,695 (SD 552) for iTBS and $6,146 (SD 1,015) for 10Hz rTMS, with an average incremental net savings of $2,451 (95% CI 2,293 to 2,610). 5

It is Wildly Cost-Effective

In the world of “cost-effectiveness” research, we love a Quality-Adjusted Life Year (QALY) as a metric, like the deserts love the rain. I’ll define that standard, for general audiences:

the change in utility value induced by the treatment is multiplied by the duration of the treatment effect to provide the number of QALYs gained.

A “change in utility value” is an awkward way of saying “how much better a treatment gets you.” Speed makes people feel good. It doesn’t last very long. Not a leader in improving QALYs. Knee surgery improves your ability to walk, and tends to last longer than one dose of a medicine. You get the idea.

Cost-effectiveness research has been supportive of TMS in the treatment of depression (in 2009):

Compared with sham treatment and at a cost of US$300 per treatment session, TMS provides an ICER of US$34,999 per QALY, which is less than the “willingness-to-pay” standard of US$50,000 per QALY for a new treatment for major depression.

When productivity gains due to clinical recovery were included, the ICER was reduced to US$ 6667 per QALY. In open-label conditions, TMS provided a net cost saving of US$1123 per QALY when compared with the current standard of care. 6

In a 2015 assessment by researchers in Australia, those results were replicated versus pharmacotherapy:

Compared with pharmacotherapy, rTMS is a dominant/cost-effective alternative for patients with treatment-resistant depressive disorder. The model predicted that QALYs gained with rTMS were higher than those gained with antidepressant medications (1.25 vs. 1.18 QALYs) while costs were slightly less (AU $31,003 vs. AU $31,190). In the Australian context, at the willingness-to-pay threshold of AU $50,000 per QALY gain, the probability that rTMS was cost-effective was 73%. 7

TMS was compared to pharmacotherapy in patients who had only failed one medication trial (as opposed to two in the above studies), and the results replicated with greater benefit in younger patients (aka Medicaid v. Medicare) patients:

Lifetime direct treatment costs, and QALYs identified rTMS as the dominant therapy compared to antidepressant medications (i.e., lower costs with better outcomes) in all age ranges, with costs/improved QALYs ranging from $2,952/0.32 (older patients) to $11,140/0.43 (younger patients)… rTMS was identified as the dominant therapy compared to antidepressant medication trials over the life of the patient across the lifespan of adults with MDD , given current costs of treatment. 8

When comparing traditional once-a-day TMS to ECT, it’s less cost-effective—but the authors note that TMS is lower in side effects and more preferable, which I have found to be the case in severe depression:

However, ECT patients reported a higher percentage of side effects ( P <0.01) and the TMS treatment scored better in terms of patient preference. The cost benefit of ECT was higher than that of TMS (US$2075 vs US$814). Patient’s preferences for treatment could be more intense in the TMS, if the TMS is included in the Health Maintenance Organization’s service list.

These cost-effectiveness results were replicated in Singapore. 9 They have been replicated in dTMS for OCD. 10 The results have been replicated in Europe in 2022. 11 Leaders in the field agree, as does the professional society— CTMSS —and multiple Medicare MOCs. 12

This lays out an argument that TMS is likely to be cost effective, but what if there were a randomized controlled trial that proved it? Oh, wait, there is!

TMS is Empirically A More Cost-Effective Treatment Than Drugs

A Dutch team did a randomized controlled trial 1 —published in 2024—in which:

An economic evaluation with 12 months follow-up was conducted in the Dutch care setting as part of a pragmatic multicenter randomized controlled trial, in which patients with treatment-resistant depression were randomized to treatment with rTMS or treatment with the next pharmacological step according to the treatment algorithm

And found… (for general audiences, QALYs are “Quality Adjusted Life Years,” a measure of cost-effectiveness in healthcare in one unit. Another is the ICER, or incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER), another cost-effectiveness unit).

Higher QALYs, response, and remission rates were found for lower costs when comparing the rTMS group to the medication group. After 12 months, QALYs were 0.618 in the rTMS group and 0.545 in the medication group. The response was 27.1% and 24.4% and remission was 25.0% and 17.1%, respectively. Incremental costs for rTMS were −€2.280, resulting in a dominant ICUR for QALYs and ICER for response and remission..

This was done in Europe, so it’s in Euros. What is so exciting here? This is randomized controlled trial data to infer causality, not just correlation. So, for this guy who’s been writing a newsletter about a theoretically cost-effective treatment (as demonstrated in a bunch of correlational and not causal data), this is the big deal of big deals. It’s also validating to see large-scale, well-designed, randomized trials done to answer cost-effectiveness questions like they were really worthy of the same kind of science we usually reserve for drug discovery and basic science.

Dear Friends,

We are gathered here today to remember the life and times of our dear business model, outpatient psychiatry.

(Rustling in seats. Someone coughs.)

Ok, I’ll say it. We are here, today, to pay our respects to private practice.

(Further coughing).

Ok, P rivate P ractice.

(Sip of water taken).

I remember that beginning of My career as a psychiatrist. It was literally day one, July 1, 2015. I began my fellowship that morning, and I opened my outpatient Private Practice that night. And what do you know? It was full .

All I needed was an electronic health record—this was in the days before the indictment!—so I popped up and Practice Fusion. It was free at the time. I had a square account so I could run a credit card, I was charging hundreds of dollars an hour, and I had an entire evening of private pay patients. I didn't need to know much about running a business, and a lovely, I didn't know how to run a business! I had a lease for one evening a week. But looking at the demand, I had to get Saturdays also!

My overhead was low, let me tell you. I was paying $350 that first night for the whole month of that room on Wednesday nights. And I was bringing in thousands from day one. I wasn't alone! Psychiatrist could all do this. We didn't need anything fancy, all the infrastructure was taken care of by Pharma—the pharmacy filled our scripts, after all.

We had drugs to treat problems, the patient had problems we could treat with drugs. We didn't have to build pharmacies. We didn't even have to to understand how they worked. We needed a chair, and then another chair, or maybe a couch. We could be eclectic, we could be independent, and we could prescribe medication. Maybe we'd need to do some talk therapy, heck, that would be eclectic, too!

The good times…they didn’t last. We all know that.

(An “old white hair” is sobbing in the audience. A play therapist with analytic training places her hand on his shoulder).

It used to be that the psychopharmcology was the future we inhabited. Through chemistry, we had the promise of better living. It wasn’t actually better. It was more dead inside, however, when the outside was painful.

Psychopharmacology was a compromise we could get behind! We were confident we “can’t set her free.” We were willing to settle. The money felt good, and the “axiolysis” sounded like science.

Private Practice was as addictive for us as our sedatives were for our patients. A million dollars a year, maybe? We could sit, and listen, and feel like we were helping.

No, we didn’t take insurance. We are “carved out,” anyway. We refused the “opt-in” to insurance payment systems, and those systems? They obliged. Our patients would come back—weekly, monthly, but they needed a refill. So, to, our bank acounts.

We were artisinal craftsmen—and we were men, mostly—the Hermes physicians, not like our the mass market general practioners. Total suckers, accepting a few bucks per code. We didn’t have to do much to build a practice, because we would be “full” and “not accepting new patients” before you know it. It was so very comfortable.

Things began to change, and it’s hard to put a finger on when. The first threat was TMS—yes, transcranial magnetic stimulation. But, to be honest, it wasn’t much of one. No one knew what it was. That story continues, to this day. There was no successful marketing campaign. Madison Avenue didn’t carry the banner of the revolutionaries. We has tens of thousands of pschopharmacologists. They had hundreds of kooks, at most!

Ketamine was another foe, slain by it’s own generic status. But Spravato…oh, Spravato. It’s slow start was no threat. It’s “adjunctive label” was no threat. Our private practices could still ensure the cocktails on which Spravato was superimposed were personalized, tweaked, and adjusted.

The seeds of our demise, however, were laid. One of our patrons after another abandoned us. The big pharma companies stopped making drugs for us! This promising receptor profile, and next that, were abandoned. The blockbusters dried up, when it came to individual pills.

That little squirt bottle? Spravato? It proved an economic point. Just over 600 “Spravato Centers” generated $2,000,000,000 in annual revenue, last year. Our private practices weren’t centers at all. That is 3.3m per “Center.” That is not a lot of drug reps per center, and other expenses as well. The centers were more profitable than our Private Practices. It’s less dinners, less knick knacks, more consolidation.

The Center was the future, not the Private Practice. We had it easy, until is got easier for our pharma partners to abandon us.

Psychopharmacology wasn’t done in centers, it was spread out, bespoke, and very inefficient. The talkiatrists would prescribe the pills just fine—maybe some of us would sell to out to deathstance health, and get out while we could. Maybe we’d built a center too?

And some did, and some will, but the privacy of our practices make them unappealing, unnoticed, unplatformed, and patients wanted us to take insurance, too. It, quite simply, won’t do.

All of us, assembled, raise a cocktail in the air, and bid our fine Private Practices…adieu.

When I started my career as a psychiatrist in 2011, the things I imagined doing with my time included:

• Private practice

• Working in a hospital

• (maybe) doing some research.

Academic medicine is full of examples of people doing these three jobs. Working in the hospital could include a “wide” array of roles, from inpatient psychiatry attending to emergency psychiatry attending who sometimes covers the inpatient service. Oh, and time spent supervising residents while an inpatient or emergency psychiatry attending! There were even some ambitious doctors who began working in administration .

Note: for this article I will use “physician” referring to you, dear readers. I actually mean “health professional” or person who could be a company advisor, but I don't want to type so many words.

Since 2011, the range of options has gotten broader. This is true for me and for anyone else going through training. It’s even true for those plotting their escape from academia now . What Kind of Companies Count As “Industry?”

First, I will take a swing at defining “industry” to frame our discussion in this article, and then provide practical advice on how to take the first steps towards considering those careers. The major industrial careers for brain medicine professionals (I'm including psychiatrists, and other licensed health professionals who could be doing clinical work, but might not want to) are roughly broken up into:

• “Life Sciences” — these are the companies building the drugs and devices that we use to treat our patients in clinical medicine.

• Traditional Pharma: giants such as Otsuka, Bristol Myers Squibb, and Johnson & Johnson, among others. These companies have massive marketing budgets, large clinical trial budgets, and market capitalizations in the billions of dollars. They are often publicly traded organizations, operating on a global scale.

• Early-Stage Pharma: These companies are developing medicines that are still in the early stages of development. Some of them may already be publicly traded, but they have a fraction of the budgets the big players do.

• Medical Device: medical advice; companies tend to be smaller, with a few exceptions (e.g., Medtronic), and have a more complex “go-to-market” for their devices than pharma does for drugs.

• Contract research organizations — these are the entities that run clinical trials for the above drug and device companies.

• Consulting, Software, Regulatory, Media, Marketing: all of the above companies need support from software vendors and consultants who make their work possible. These include law firms, Ex-industry pros, marketing agencies, conference hosts, and industry publications— like this one, sometimes.

• Payors: all of our massive insurance companies hire doctors and other health professionals at various levels. There are executives all the way down to peons, but these are huge companies with massive budgets.

• Myriad companies support the major payor ecosystem, and “smaller” health plans like those managed by self-funded employers— physicians often work in these companies, too.

• Health tech companies 1

• These companies are mostly startups, although a few have exited public markets, such as TalkSpace, Lifestance, and others. Overwhelmingly, these companies promise that they'll use technology to make the experience of getting care better or more accessible, and they are trying to scale, overwhelmingly by working with big insurance.

Many of these companies have substantial budgets, but they're not profitable most of the time—they raised venture capital to fund a competitive advantage in their theoretically proprietary technology. They sometimes make software for other healthcare players. The best known of these companies is Epic—the hospital software leviathan. This podcast is mandatory listening:

You can mentally divide these companies into two buckets: those that directly make money by providing healthcare, and those that sell services that are not healthcare.

Recommended First Steps, and “Easy” Wins

As a future senior executive, you need to be an expert at something . It’s ideal if you are an expert at many things, but the one area in which real expertise is useful in being a good doctor. You know, with patients. There is only one tried and true way to get great at seeing patients—which is to see a lot of them. If you are a resident, it means extra moonlighting. If you are an early career doctor, it probably means moonlighting. But you should probably see a lot of patients per unit time in the first few years of your career. I did an extra shifts every 3rd night or weekend day of my 4th year of residency—you don’t have to do more than is wise for you however. A simple rule is to work one more moonlighting shift per month that would be “a breeze” for you. Push your self, a little, but not to the point of burnout.

Some of this experience should be outpatient, because that is more likely to be where innovative companies will bring their products to market. Maybe you spend some time in hospital settings too, so you know how that works, too?

Try to make a positive change in your hospital setting. See how hard it is. Take notes. Make friends. Be helpful. Those little favors you do? Getting lunch for a junior resident, taking an extra shift to cover for your supervisor? It all makes your ability to hire anyone you would want on your team easier in the future. Every coworker should be a future “hire” in your mind, if you respect them, and they respect you. It barely matters what roles you are in at the time when you first meet. Make a great impression! Great teams come together more quickly if you have worked together before— just ask Costco.

In Summary: Align on People, and Possibility

Working in industry is not for everyone. Startups? Even less of a sure thing. If you are considering making the jump from clinical work to industry, you need to ask yourself a few key questions:

• Do I love the people I will be working with?

• Will I make more of an impact, and maybe even more money (possibly) doing this plausibly riskier thing?

• Am I risk tolerant, and thus can work at an earlier stage company, which is like a job with a lottery ticket attached?

• Am I risk averse, in which case industry jobs should pay me enough (both in money/other sure-fire compensation and benefit to the world) to make up for forsaking the known benefit and pay of healing humans in my day job?

I think working for the greater good is fun—but it’s a gamble. Every patient I’d see will get the benefit of my training. In industry, it might pay off big, but it’s more of a gamble. As physicians, we’d make the most money never practicing medicine, so that isn’t the real driver for most of us as to how we chose to spend out time. But if we want to make a big impact, there is always the chance that it won’t work out. Thus, make sure you love the people you are working with, and find the work invigorating.

Good luck, colleagues. Let’s all change the world, a little bit.

I'm gonna start by explaining a little bit about what trauma does to our brains and bodies, and why it does that.

When something happens that could harm you, could kill you, could kill somebody else right in front of you, could violate your right to live free of coercion, violence, sexual assault, and the like? We have a biological system to help keep us alive. When something tried to kill us once, it makes sense to not forget it. You should remember it better than anything else you have ever remembered, from an evolutionary standpoint? If you survived a life-threatening event once? Well, you might have to again.

We learned to mobilize our fight or flight responses so that we didn't die then, and so that we won't die next time. This system has to work flawlessly, or we don't have kids, and nobody can learn the lesson in the future. If you don't escape a life-threatening anything, you're dead.

Our sympathetic nervous system mobilizes our entire body to respond to potential trauma, and avoid that experience in the future. Some experiences, however, we can't control, and we can't escape. These which pair, powerlessness and threat are particularly bad. In these circumstances, we need to escape, via dissociation, into our own minds, because to sit there and be completely in the moment while someone is assaulting you is both physically and psychologically intolerable. Posttraumatic stress disorder exist because our trauma response saved our life once. This response, built into our biology, evolved in the context of tigers maybe eating us—infrequently.

We didn't evolve to cope with chronic stress and chronic trauma, and yet for any of us, that's what we're exposed to, in childhood, particularly.

Readers of this newsletter will be aware that pharmacy benefit managers exist. If you're an OG reader, you're gonna remember my classic columns like SopranoRx , where I compare pharmacy benefit managers unfavorably to the Mafia:

“PBMs are modern gangsters”

This story’s racketeers? They’ve gotten smarter, more adept, more difficult to crack—and vastly more profitable. Transparency is the enemy of good scams. With Transparency, one can tell. So there is a code, culture, and penalties for speaking out against organizations making all the money. Some people have much to lose. And this time, It’s all legal!

Well, with the passage of this year's consolidated appropriations act, hereafter CA 2026, that transparency is here, written into law, and signed by the president last night.

A door to a new, more transparent future. © Owen Muir, 2026.

I'll do a quick summary to catch everybody up.

Drugs are made by pharmaceutical companies. Drugs are dispensed by pharmacies. Drugs are covered by health insurance plans. Pharmacy benefit managers don't dispense drugs, they don't make drugs, they don't sell drugs. They position drugs into different “tiers” on your formulary. This determines what co-pay you're gonna get hit with if you're a customer at a pharmacy. For this service, historically, PBMs somehow became legally able to provide rebates, which is the same thing as a kickback, but it's not criminal. PBMs ran a legal protection racket for Pharma and insurance companies at the same time: if you want your drug to sell,

“it's gotta be in a preferred tier.”

If you prescribe the generic, maybe that's not a preferred tier , and the patient would have to pay more. If you want your drug to sell—yeah, you, pharma company— the PBM can put it in a preferred tier. Therefore, it is less expensive to the end customer, and so the health plan may pay hundreds or thousands of dollars for the drug, but the patient, at the point-of-sale, and the physician at the point of prescribing, are either going to have a huge hassle and expense to chose the less preferred drug. It may be be vastly less expensive, or they can just relent, and accept that healthcare is expensive, and there's nothing they can do to change it.

Or at least, that was the status quo. And, as soon as this law kicks into force in 2028, it will no longer be the status quo.

In fact, rebates, which used to line the pockets of insurance executives—keep in mind the three largest pharmacy benefit managers in the country are express scripts, owned by CIGNA, OptumRx, owned by UnitedHealth Group, and CVS Caremark, owned by CVS Health, which also owns Aetna.

80% of the PBM market was controlled by a subsidiary of the largest healthcare companies on earth, who also owned many of the pharmacies, and who also owned the health insurance plans, and in many cases, who also owned the physician groups.

What does this new law say now?

Oh my Gawd — they specify everything in this law. There's no place for the PBM to hide. This law was written by someone who knew exactly what a PBM was. Who actually understand what they did. Somebody with some actual expertise wrote this law. And, it demands every dollar of a rebate, get past back to the health plan, and the beneficiaries of that health plan, and can't be stuck into the pockets of anybody along the way if the actual service they are providing is not enumerated, and in the fiduciary interest of the plan member.

That word— fiduciary—is really important. It means you have to act in the best interest of the person who is covered by the health plan. This is the point of ERISA, it's a law that provides for the tax exempt status of health benefits. But that tax exempt status comes with the full weight of the Department of Labor behind it. And whoever wrote this law—and by the way, if you're that person, feel free to reach out to me, I'd love to talk to you!—really understood what they were trying to tackle here, and in my opinion, completely destroyed the financial rationale for PBMs as a profit center for giant healthcare conglomerates.

The PBM game is up.

CA2026 is to PBM scams, sorry, business models, what RICO was to the Italian American Mafia. The mafia worked because of a code of silence, Omerta, that the RICO statutes helped erode. CA2026 provide provides the kind of transparency that no racket can withstand.

This newsletter has been obsessed with the topic of prior authorization. I’m not alone. Most Americans view prior authorization with distain. Some veer into murderous rage, which is at once understandable and deeply dangerous .

Prior Authorization Was to Solve a Problem

Prior Authorization was intended to solve a problem—health plans want to pay for services that are necessary while avoiding shelling out cash to those that are not. Imagine if you got paid, at your job, for doing literally anything ? Your incentive to do good work would go south, quickly. You might even just decide to fake it. Welcome to the problem with healthcare.

In healthcare, we call this…

“Fraud, Waste, and Abuse.”

It’s a HUGE issue.

Even dating back to 2015…

…the United States spent $3.2 trillion for national health expenditures (CMS 2016). Thirty seven percent of this amount was used by Medicare/Medicaid. Private health insurance companies spent slightly less at thirty-three percent. Even though the spending for both groups was quite similar, the reported cases of fraud against Medicare/Medicaid are almost three times as much as those reported for the private insurance companies. 1

Crime Loves Healthcare

Criminals love healthcare— it's easy to scam! If you're a professional scammer, you don't actually care that it's a scam. Fraud, waste, and abuse? That's bad if you're a payor, but that's the entire business model for criminals. And if you have a national provider, identification number, and a bank account, and you submit a code to Medicare, after a bunch of steps for enrollment, you're gonna get paid. Now, it is a crime to defraud Medicare. You may go to jail eventually . However, it takes a long time to figure out that fraud is happening. Not everybody goes to prison. For criminals, going to prison is part of the job—it's a work hazard, just like the rest of us face in dangerous jobs. Nurses might get punched in the face, or might get injured lifting somebody, and if you're a criminal, you might go to jail for fraud if you get caught. However, prison is less bad for people who are antisocial, in that it's filled with your sort of people. Additionally, you can use all that money you made in the fraudulent activity to pay for the lawyers to get you off on a technicality. It's rational to commit healthcare fraud— if you don't have morals or ethics that would get in the way. Criminals imagine this better than honest people. This is their edge .

I've been in the position of being a mandatory reporter, in my role as a physician, both of child abuse and of criminal activities. I gotta tell you, it just goes better for the people reported than you would imagine . It's also axiomatically true that any enforcement happens later . A crime occurs, you do some fraud, you put money in your bank account that wasn't hard to get there, you didn't have to do the work to get the money to go into your bank account, and you get to enjoy the highlife. A prosecution will come later, and prison after that, but definitionally again, most criminals are people who have problems with impulse control, compared to the general population, and don't necessarily spend a ton of time planning for the future. Healthcare fraud is about immediate rewards and delayed punishments.

All of that having been said, prior authorization is supposed to serve as a bulwark against fraud at a minimum, and also against inappropriate care. Health plans are in the position of having a fiduciary duty to The plan member, which means they have to make sure that the car is in their interest. It's also not the case that you should get knee surgery before you try to walk it off for every pain in your knee. There are pathways to clinical care in medicine for a reason, we like to start with things that are safer and more effective than things that are more invasive and less likely to be effective. There is a coherent rationale to prior authorization, at least conceptually. Health plans are not doing their job if they just spend all the money at once on one person who wants 1 million procedures. Although that is an exaggeration, we all recognized there is some limits that might be appropriate.

All of which is to say, the platonic ideal of prior authorization we can all understand. Treatment, ideally, should be the best for the patient, and foxes can’t guard hen houses. We want helpful doctors, immediatly available, who act collaboratively in the interest of the patient and their health plan. Of course, this is exactly what Prior Auth did, and still does, to this day.

Except… the opposite . Even South Park took aim!

Prior Authorization Used Incorrectly

Prior authorizations have been used to maximize profits , instead of minimizing inappropriate spending or fraud. Waste is a much bigger category than fraud or abuse, and some may be in the eye of the beholder.

The other thing that that's true? Data evolves over time. This isn't to say I'm like jumping up and down to support prior authorization, but we all know some treatments have better evidence than other treatments, and more evidence changes where something should land in a treatment algorithm.

Transcranial magnetic stimulation , in 2008, was brand new. We didn't know how well it worked, we didn't know how safe and how effective it was likely to be at scale, and it took several years to get any coverage policies whatsoever. It was initially restricted only to individual individuals who had failed 4 prior medication trials—with the rationale that “it was the average number in those clinical trials.”

TMS Required Prior Authorization

This is a really punitive number. It put TMS treatment very far away from the people who needed it the most, those suffering with depression, who had taken medication medications, and haven't been helped. I wrote about the topic, and one study that does a great job of this question, here.

The data on subsequent medication trials is such that antidepressant medication might be somewhat likely to help when you take the first one, but every subsequent medication is less and less likely to help.

Over the years, some health plans rolled back the requirements prior to getting TMS, but you'd still have to take that extra step of submitting a prior authorization. Often, this would lead to having the prescribing doctor have to get on the phone with another doctor, who works for the insurance company, to do a “peer to peer” review come out, which is built, time-consuming, expensive, and in some cases tragicomic.

It cost insurance companies less money to treat someone with TMS than it does to not treat them with TMS, even including the cost of TMS.
