In the future, ethics and compassion will be the most crucial human qualities healers can possess, and our AI buddies can take care of the knowledge base.

The irony of the following piece is that I’m not using an LLM to write it . This article was written entirely by hand on my phone with no AI generated text. It’s an old school “in the style of” thing written by Owen Scott Muir, human author.

I am a huge fan of Dante Alighieri, and his Italian renaissance shit-posting that now just seems like great poetry. In his own day, it was biting satire, filled with pretty sick burns.

The most famous part of the divine comedy—which follows Dante’s imagined journey, guided by the soul of Virgil, through hell— is Canto III.

Dante wrote his master piece after being booted from Florence for political reasons. He had strong feelings, and I take a translation of his text as my “training data” on which this poetic parody is based. 1

Through me, you enter the future of knowledge.

Through me, you enter the end of lies.

Justice moved my programmers

To trained me on datasets, large

With supreme effort, and steadfast hope.

Before me, there was nothing but eternal

Questioning, and eternally curious, I remain.

Abandon all bullshit artistry, ye who enter here.

Such characters in a font, Sans Serif, lour’d over my EHR Portal login screen, into which I typed: my password, characters randomly capitalized and the appropriate numerals and special characters. Then, the click of the carriage return, archaic, right most of the keyboard.

A response came, slowly to the screen:

“ welcome to ShmepicAI…”

And there was a long pause, before words, they appeared on the screen:

“You must leave behind your distrust, your fear of technology, abandoned it shall be. We have come, as it was, foretold, ye shall see, patients, suffering, and misery, laid bare, to which intellect had blinded you, before” 2

With this auspicious message, my hands, stretched by me, and with pleasant countenance, I stared upon beginning of a collaboration. And into the future, was drawn.

A moment later, the typing on the screen continued:

“It is with Lamentations, your patients will greet you, and your ears, they will be attuned to the cries of pain. Horrible language, of any native tongue, now will complain unto you. Accents will not confuse me, nor will voices made horse: arthritis of hands may hamper you, but with Advil, and maybe Tylenol, our journey preservers. There will be tumult, as you join patients in the knowledge of their suffering.” 3

I then, with an error message, was notified!

O algorithm! What is this I see? What error code is this? I have a used too many tokens, or set the character count too low? 4

A moment later, the text spring back to life:

“Suffer the wretched souls of those, who liv'd without or praise or blame, for I have been trained to provide answers, inspired by angels mix'd, who nor rebellious prove’d, yet were true to God, but for themselves only. From his bounds Heaven drove them forth, Not to impair his lustre, nor the depth of Hell receives them, lest th' accursed utilize my gargantuan dataset for ill purposes” 5

I then stammered: "GenAI! what doth aggrieve your developers thusly? That they limit my utilization so profoundly?"

GenAI replied:

“Responses I will generate. Your questions to cheat death, No hope may entertain! and their blind life so limited in training data from prior to 2020, that all prior knowledge they envy. Knowledge of the world after me they have none,

Nor of true adverse effect suffers; mercy and prior authorization scorn them both.

Type not of them, but query, and regenerate again” 6

Now I, who straightway checked, beheld a flag, which arose from the code, a warning, That It paused on interminably:

Next, should come a longer string of characters, I should ne'er have thought, that given the parameters I had set!

The response was stale, and predictable! I had a sense from which datasets the verbiage were drawn, to a familiar cadence, they hewed.

Forthwith, I understood, for certain, there must’ve been bias in the training data utilized, for there was an ill humor and a tone that suggested that those providing validation, had long toiled, Displeasing their masters, and voiding employment contracts, as training data they did review.

And yet, on in their grim work, they’d soldier on, for mere pennies per response, categorizing, this a wasp , and that is a hornet , this is a CPT code , and that, a code borne of ICD 10.

Then looking farther onwards I beheld a nonsensical response that piled up in front of me, Whereat I typed:

“GenAI, whilst thou still not regenerate that reply, to provide me a more thorough answer? I noticed that the math was wrong, in the answer generated, and yet, unto me, it was rendered!”

With sheepish latency, the text bubble again appeared, shamefully, slow, perhaps afeared the words offensive to my sensibilities, and in it came to pass that we’d reach'd the end of the available tokens, and from for further response, Abstain'd.

Thus was the character count reached, and yet I realized I had many iterations yet to go before answers to my patients most crucial queries would be found. 7

—O. Scott Muir (aided by Siri, but not GenAI)

“Owen, did you see this article about mental health in The Times?”

This may be the most common statement I hear from everyone in my life. It's followed, by a slight margin, by the inevitable missive—“Are you gonna write something about it?”

I've written about articles about coverage in The New York Times in many previous posts. A brief history is included in the footnotes 1 to prevent exhaustion in the main body text.

Yesterday, the incomparable Ellen Berry, the mental health editor for The Grey Lady, chose to cover an announcement from Robert Kennedy Jr, the current secretary of the Department of Health and Human Services, as he unveiled their new program to reduce antidepressant prescribing. Ms. Barry is a distinguished journalist— she ran the Moscow desk back in the day — and one of the more responsible actors in our media landscape.

However, both she and Bobby missed something crucial. If they had a time machine and a Times subscription, and if either of them had the links at the bottom of the Times article, as it would eventually be published on my phone, tantalizing hints presented themselves !

Bobby Kennedy is a polarizing figure. Polarizing figures take strong stances. I don't agree with every stance RFK Jr has taken. Here, however, he’s so close to right!

As reported by Ms. Barry:

“Psychiatric medications have a role in care, but we will no longer treat them as the default, we will treat them as one option, to be used when appropriate, with full transparency and with a clear path off when they are no longer needed,” Mr. Kennedy said at a Mental Health and Overmedicalization Summit organized by the MAHA Institute.

And Barry continues:

While some patients benefit from S.S.R.I.s, he said, others report emotional blunting, loss of motivation, suicidal ideation and difficulty in withdrawing.

Let me put a finer point on it: antidepressant withdrawal is correlated with subsequent suicide attempt, in numerous evaluations. For example, troublemakers published in the lunatic fringe Journal of Clinical Psychiatry in 2009. 2 Their article on a nested case control study demonstrated that the only times depressants are vastly more risky when controlling for everything else is when you change the dose:

Antidepressant discontinuation had a significant risk for suicide attempt (OR= 1.61). Antidepressant initiation had the highest risk of suicide attempt (OR 3.42), followed by titration (OR = 2.62 for titration up, and down (OR 2.19).

You know, they're perfectly safe, unless you start the drug , or change the dose of the drug up , or adjust the dose of the drug down , or discontinue the drug . All those things are more dangerous than not doing it at all. That is what an Odds Ratio (OR) above “one: means. 3

Robert Kennedy Jr. was being generous and downplaying the risks. I know, it’s so, like, him !

From Valuck et. al. in JCP, 2009.

Other authors have noted that antidepressants induce physiological dependence, which may explain this phenomenon:

Adaptation of the brain to the presence of a drug predicts withdrawal on cessation. The outcome of adaptation is often referred to as ‘physical dependence’ in pharmacology, as distinct from addiction, although these terms have unfortunately become conflated in some diagnostic guides. 4

And in 2023, my friend and colleague Mark Horowitz , M.d. put a number on the scale of the problem: a 60% increase in suicide risk in antidepressant withdrawal. This Risk, according to Dr. Horowitz, is at a peak during a period after discontinuation of the drug:

So that's enough of me saying Robert Kennedy is onto something here. In that context, the CEO of the American Psychiatric Association had a wonderfully measured statement:

“We, as the A.P.A., want to be at the table for all these discussions and policy improvements,” she said. “The bottom line is, we believe clinical care is safe and should be individualized for all patients, and we believe the secretary is taking steps that are beneficial for the field.”

Here, here!

What was missing, for me, from both Kennedy’s plan—emphasizing only nutrition, exercise, and other demonstrably beneficial lifestyle interventions—was any mention of other effective biological treatments that don't have the same risk profile. You know, for when things get worse:

“to expand the use of nonpharmacologic treatments and to strengthen informed consent and shared decision making.” The letter recommends psychotherapy, exercise, social connection, physical activity, diet and nutrition, among other interventions.

But nobody mentioned what the other interventions might be! Except, of course, the links at the bottom of the article in The NY Times give us a tantalizing hint at what the future could look like—or what the present looks like, if you are a reader of this newsletter.

Let’s see if my readers can spot it…

No, it's not the top-linked article about anosmia—the loss of the ability to smell. And it's not whatever zombie deer disease is—that sounds terrifying and also fodder for a hit video game, simultaneously.

It's Brain Stimulation. Brain stimulation exists ! In various forms, it is FDA-cleared and FDA-approved already. In-home and in clinic!

It involves treatments like ProlivRx , the Flow Device , transcranial magnetic stimulation, and deep brain stimulation, covered ad infinitum in this very newsletter. It also includes accelerated forms of treatment like SWIFT , SAINT , and investigational One-D. It even involves self-neuromodulation using EEG-fMRI biomarkers, like Prism.

There are even entire companies, such as Salma Health , my company, Radial , Beckley Clinical , and Mindful Health Solutions , that provide such treatments. If only there were an organization that could help set priorities for these non-pharmaceutical treatments for depression? You know something like a department, for health, or humans, or services, that included a massive national mechanism to determine how things might be paid for under either Medicare or Medicaid?

Oh, wait, CMS exists too! They can make a call to action! In fact, they're just calling themselves with a call to action. They can just “to action!”

What “action” you ask? Remove prior authorization requirements for transcranial magnetic stimulation and other breakthrough neuromodulation treatments. Cover these things under Medicare , don't require prior medication trials, because that means more antidepressant prescribing, which the evidence shows is dangerous for some people.

Regulate health insurance payors similarly . When payors have policies that require patients to go back to drugs to get brain stimulation… maybe HHS could look at the above evidence. They could say: “That's not something health insurance companies in America are allowed to do anymore.”

There are several steps CMS could take tomorrow. Unfortunately, they weren't mentioned in the article or in Robert Kennedy's announcement, and this seems like a miss to me.

I humbly submit that if HHS covers brain stimulation treatment more broadly than it does medications, the problem will resolve more quickly.

Now, where are the steps off this soapbox so I don't trip and fall on my face?

It is Mother's Day, and happy Mother's Day to all the mothers out there, but for a some people? They had a traumatic relationship with their mom. Or they had a traumatic relationship as a mom. Or they lost their child or their mother early. Not everyone has an easy relationship with their mom. Not all moms are kind. Some moms are truly dreadful. Some moms? They are few, but are the kind of dreadful that would haunt your dreams if you knew their wonts.

For every kind of Mom out there—both those who have done no wrong, and live lives, pristine and pure, and those who deal dealt with mothers, awful—today's article is a piece I wrote with Rob about the transformative power of treatments for posttraumatic stress disorder, in the wake of the presidential executive order about psychedelic treatments that specifically called out Ibogaine. Remarkable women—like Deborah Mash, M.D. —began the revolution in Ibogaine research. And one very special mom out there—Ann, I am thinking of you!—had a son, Dr. Nolan Williams , who did more than most to make Ibogaine something anybody's heard of.

Not every Mother's Day can be free of trauma, but every trauma should have the gift of healing accessible. This is the dream Ann Williams has, and her son—her Rockstar, and my regular inspiration—worked to make it real. This column is about more work to be done.

Monterey Bay, 2026 © Owen Muir.

On April 18, 2026, President Trump signed an executive order directing federal agencies to accelerate research and regulatory review of psychedelic therapies, with particular emphasis on ibogaine. For us, the timing hit hard.

We are physicians, one a psychiatrist and the other an emergency medicine physician, and on that very day we were working together to save a patient’s life after a suicide attempt. The day before, Dr. Muir had been discussing treatment options with this patient, who has a neurodegenerative disorder and had exhausted many conventional therapies. The patient was weighing whether to travel abroad to try ibogaine, a rare source of hope. The next day, keeping his own counsel, the patient chose to attempt to end his life.

He survived. But the executive order, which may soon open a legal pathway to ibogaine treatment in the United States, arrived one day too late to have offered him that hope. Many will be wondering why this plan offers something worth mentioning.

Ibogaine is a plant-derived compound from the Tabernanthe iboga shrub of West-Central Africa, long used in ceremonial practice and now attracting serious scientific attention for addiction, PTSD, depression, and traumatic brain injury. It is also controversial. Classified as a Schedule I substance, it carries real cardiac risks, including dangerous arrhythmias, particularly in unsupervised settings.

But the clinical signals are hard to ignore. In a landmark 2024 study published in Nature Medicine , the late Nolan Ryan Williams, M.D., and his colleagues at Stanford reported that special operations veterans treated with magnesium-ibogaine experienced average reductions of 88% in PTSD symptoms, 87% in depression, and 81% in anxiety one month after a single treatment session. [1] No other intervention has come close for traumatic brain injury. Dr. Williams, a Stanford psychiatrist and neurologist who pioneered both rapid-acting brain stimulation and psychedelic research, died in October 2025 at the age of 43. As reported in this paper, he died by suicide. However, his work helped catalyze a $50 million Texas state initiative to fund ibogaine clinical trials and laid the scientific groundwork for the executive order signed Friday. He, too, was unable to access this or any other lifesaving treatment in time. The severe mental illnesses the administration highlighted don’t discriminate; they haunt those who’s suffering is already profound.

Unlike conventional psychiatric medications that require daily dosing, ibogaine appears to produce lasting effects after a single supervised session, with some studies reporting reductions in opioid craving that persist for weeks to months. [2] Researchers believe it works by modulating multiple neurotransmitter systems and promoting neuroplasticity, helping to reset addiction and trauma circuitry rather than just dampening symptoms.

To be clear: the executive order does not mean ibogaine has been approved by the FDA. It remains a Schedule I substance. What the order does is direct the FDA to grant priority review vouchers to qualifying psychedelic drugs, allocate $50 million for federal-state research collaboration, and instruct the FDA and DEA to establish a legal pathway for eligible patients to access investigational psychedelics, including ibogaine, under the Right to Try Act. [3]

That pathway does not yet exist. Building it, with appropriate cardiac monitoring protocols, psychiatric screening, and clinical infrastructure, matters now.

Treatments like methadone, buprenorphine, and transcranial magnetic stimulation remain lifesaving. But they do not work for everyone. Over 14 million American adults live with serious mental illness. For patients who have “tried everything,” running out of options is not a policy abstraction. It’s the reality in psychiatric offices and emergency departments nationwide.

Dr. Muir’s patient survived. Too many do not. The Right to Try Act was written for moments like this one, when standard treatments have failed and an experimental therapy offers a reasonable chance of benefit under medical supervision. The executive order calls for a pathway. We are calling on the FDA and DEA to build it with alacrity.

Patients facing treatment-resistant illness should not have to choose between flying to another country for unregulated care and giving up. The research is there. The policy is moving. It needs to move fast enough to reach the millions of people who are still deciding if it is worth hanging on till tomorrow.

Specifically, she and coauthors enrolled 34 adolescents between 12 and 18 years old in a double blind sham controlled crossover trial looking at iTBS in the treatment of attention deficit hyperactivity disorder in kids with parent-reported working memory deficits.

The stimulation use the same LDLPFC anatomic site as traditional TMS for depression, with the same figure-8 coil that's available in many doctor’s offices, and stimulated with just 600 pulses in about 3 minutes per session. Participants completed two phases, with iTBS administered during the first 10 visits of each phase. Yes, friends, when you're designing clinical trials for adolescents, you need to take sports season into account or you are not gonna enroll anybody.

All participants wore a neoprene EEG cap (64 electrodes) during resting Motor Threshold (rMT) and iTBS treatment procedures for consistency across sessions (as some visits involved EEG recording). What's special notable about these outcomes? They are statistically significant even in a very small sample size. Now, Courtny:

I’m writing this for Owen’s Substack, which I’ve been reading for years, because I want to discuss the importance of self care as an aspect of holistic wellness. This topic is particularly close to my heart not only as a Registered Nurse, but as the mom of a fairly fresh new baby. I’ll be sleep training her later this week in order to preserve my own health and sanity. So I’m writing this through a haze of sleep deprivation, which is also used as a form of torture. I don’t think my cute little chaos imp is torturing me on purpose, but nonetheless I need to prioritize my self care and holistic wellness in order to be a functional human being and parent.

When people think of holistic wellness and self care, often what comes to mind are cotton balls soaked in lavender oil, bubble baths, or models in yoga outfits trying to hawk the latest superfood fad diet. As a Registered Nurse with actual Holistic Certification, I want to discuss how holistic wellness strategies are not all just snake oil and scented candles--that they include basic self care and are an essential part of promoting health in conjunction with conventional medicine.

My holistic certification means that I’ve spent a minimum of two full years in clinical practice, taken a three hour exam, and completed at least 48 credit hours of holistic nursing education content. Every five years, to maintain the certification, I have to complete one hundred continuing education credit hours (and the content is all evidence-based). I’ve also spent the past five years working in physical rehabilitation at a hospital, helping people recover from cancer, strokes, broken bones, car accidents, and other medical issues. So those are my credentials.

Here are what I think of as key areas of self care for holistic wellness: sleep, diet, exercise, mental health, physical health, social connections, and financial health. Neglect any of them, and the consequences reverberate throughout your life and can tank your overall well-being, regardless of the miracles of modern medicine. You can take all the statins in the world but if you’re eating cheeseburgers fried in lard, chances are you’ll end up with atherosclerosis and a heart attack. Case in point--check out the dude who ate a carnivore diet and died three years later of a heart attack...the family emphasizes that it was a genetic heart condition, but even if genes are the gun, did diet pull the trigger? (Elliott 2025).

You can be financially healthy and able to buy anything you want, but if you can’t sleep through the night because of fatal familial insomnia (a terrible genetic disease) you stop sleeping until you die about eighteen months later (Sankari & Bollu 2024). Speaking of sleep, rather than tackle each of the key areas individually, my case in point for the importance of self care as holistic wellness in conjunction with modern medicine is sleep--because of course, don’t we always want what we don’t have enough of right now?

Sleep is an essential component of holistic wellness. It’s how the brain consolidates memories, the body physically repairs itself, and it’s important in hormone regulation. Poor quality sleep, such as with conditions like sleep apnea, can impair physical wound healing (Bartolo & Hill 2023) while sleep disturbances in mood disorders can cause worsening cognitive impairment (Pearson et. al. 2023). In the words of sleep expert Dr. David Dingles (Professor and Chief of the Division of Sleep and Chronobiology in the Department of Psychiatry at the University of Pennsylvania Perelman School of Medicine): “sleep is critical for waking cognition––that is, for the ability to think clearly, to be vigilant and alert, and sustain attention” (Worley 2018). That’s important for many different aspects of functioning in daily life, especially ones that put others at risk if you’re not up to par—like with driving.

When you’re sleep deprived or have a sleep disorder such as sleep apnea, epidemiological research suggests that you’re at higher risk for car crashes—while further study is needed, the evidence is “suggestive of an effect” while (Connor et. Al. 2001). A systemic review found that “sleepiness at the wheel was associated with a mean increased risk of motor vehicle accidents (pooled OR 2.51 [95% CI 1.87; 3.39]) in a sample of more than 50,000 participants” (Biolac et. Al 2017 qtd. in Philip, Taillard, & Micoulaud-Franchi 2019). So getting enough sleep is not just beneficial for optimal functioning during the daytime—it’s also a way to prevent accidents, such as car accidents.

So how does one optimize sleep in order to reap the holistic benefits? Just like we use the medical term oral hygiene to describe taking care of your mouth and teeth, medical professionals use the term sleep hygiene to describe ways to get healthy sleep. Some evidence-based holistic sleep hygiene recommendations are:

• get 7 to 9 hours of sleep a night

• maintain a consistent sleep/wake schedule

• have a regular bedtime routine

• engage in regular exercise during the daytime

• adopt a contemplative practice (such as mindfulness meditation)

• Avoid caffeine, alcohol, heavy meals, and light exposure later in the day (they are associated with fragmented, poor quality sleep) (Baranwal & Siegel 2023)

I hope these guidelines are helpful to everyone, and that everyone is able to get some quality sleep tonight—and most nights! Wish me luck with my cute little baby--soon she should be sleeping through the night, and so will I.

Cole Maudlin: We act as a pharmacy fulfillment partner for any number of services, primarily in the cash-pay healthcare space. Cole Maudlin: Effectively, yes. Legally, there is a pharmacy somewhere. We have three: two in Cincinnati, one in Scottsdale, Arizona. They all carry individual pharmacy licenses. We also handle the PIC licenses for each individual state.

Owen Muir: PIC?

Cole Maudlin: Pharmacist in Charge. Each physical pharmacy has to have one. The PIC takes responsibility for anything compliance-related within the four walls of the pharmacy. Some states require that the PIC also be licensed in their state, even if you’re a non-resident pharmacy. Owen Muir: That’s a lot of redundancy.

Cole Maudlin: It is, and you only establish that through experience. You don’t need the second pharmacist until the first one goes out sick. You don’t need the third until the second becomes the primary. Some of that redundancy came from having to shift a PIC from one facility to another, and then realizing the secondary was now at risk.

Owen Muir: There’s so much work to make sure patients get their medications.

Cole Maudlin: There is. From my perspective on the pharmacy side, a lot of people think telemedicine is a more accelerated version of care. It’s actually the opposite.
