Dear Ones,

It has been a year since my friend Craig died, and I reconnected with the love of my life, Zach. It has been a year and a week since my father’s death. It has been nearly a year since my mother’s death.

I have not yet opened the box(es) with her ashes.

I seem to write in seasons, in eras, in epochs, and I have not written for about four months. I was hibernating, nesting, recentering myself in my yoga practice, considering teaching again. I was going to grief counseling, and trying to heal my tummy, forgetting and remembering to date my sadness, and spending time with my love, loving.

My words are not as accessible now, but they will return. They always have. They always do.

What I have done is prepared and rehearsed and polished my Big Speech, the one that compiles my theories about intimacy so far. The one that shares all the stuff that I have made . The most vulnerable one of all.

My hope is that this synthesis of 20 years of research & reading & interviewing & conversing & workshop-leading & teaching on intimacy will usher in a new era of my career.

In January I gave a truncated version of this speech to a warm crowd of 500 smarty pants at Nerd Nite St. Pete. It was my biggest audience to date, and an absolute delight. The perfect forerunner to giving a keynote speech at Adam’s Connection & Relationships edition of his Lifelong Learners speaker series in February.

If you are in the Tampa Bay area, and you are a nerdy sort, like us, I’m pretty certain that you will enjoy this series. And you have, I believe, at least 6 more opportunities to attend. Go! Learn stuff!

I imagine you are a person who longs to connect. I hope this talk means something to you. I hope it brings more joy, more nourishment, and more intimacy into your life.

The more things get AI, the more I want to human. I want to human with you.

I want to bring this speech, this work, and workshops based on my work to conferences, festivals, and companies near you. If you feel inspired to bring me, please share this with whomsoever you know makes decisions about these sorts of things.

If you have thoughts on my theories, if they move you to action, if they remind you of a story you’d like to tell someone, tell someone. Or feel free to reply to this missive, and tell me. I’m someone too.

Big Love,

Lila

OWEN : She said, I feel like I blew my chance with you. And I said, well, you get infinite chances. Forgiveness for family. That’s how this works. Those were my last words.

VITA : When she went to La Varenne, she was twenty, and I told her this story. And she came back to me and said, I would like to be adopted before I fly to Paris. Can we do that? So we did.

OWEN: What does the process of adoption look like for a twenty-year-old?

VITA: Same as anybody else. You go to a lawyer, you tell them what you want to do. He does the paperwork, and that’s that. Any adult can choose to be adopted by anybody. You didn’t have to deal with their biological mother.

She was very happy to be adopted. Born Art Muir’s daughter...

OWEN: ...died your daughter.

VITA: She died two weeks after he did. And the only thing I could think was, thank God he didn’t live to see it.

OWEN: We buried them both in the same hole in the same day.

OWEN: What did you learn from Alison’s life, and her death?

VITA: Never waste perfectly good shrimp shells is a great metaphor for a life. The waste of it. Because she was so rejected as a child. And when a mother rejects a child, I think that’s the worst of all. And it’s almost, at least in my experience, very difficult, if not impossible, to undo that damage. Before you’re adopted, you’re abandoned. You can adopt a child, but you can’t un-abandon them.

OWEN: I can’t un-abandon them.

VITA: Which is really the ceremony she needed.

OWEN: Like an exorcism. We should develop an un-abandonment ritual.

VITA: That could work.

This is part one of a conversation with Jordan Sloshower, M.D., psychiatrist in private practice in New Haven, assistant clinical professor at Yale, and Chief Medical Officer of Beckley Clinical . He will be joining us for the next edition of the Rapid Acting Mental Health Treatment live event on May 16th, in San Francisco, California. It’s open to all! I’m a big fan of Beckley Clinical and a friend of their CEO, Josh Stevens. They are also kind enough to sponsor RAMHT, as a disclosure. It has been a pleasure getting to know even more of the team, and none more than their CMO, Jordan. He’s gracious, measured, and compassionate in a way that is at once old-fashioned and so very needed in this moment. He has the wisdom to take his time and the urgency to know that the future needs to be carefully designed to work as we hope it will. To that end, we sat down yesterday for the first of several conversations.

Jordan Sloshower, M.D.

Owen Muir: Tell our “The Frontier Psychiatrists” audience who you are and what you do.

Jordan Sloshower: My name is Jordan Sloshower. I’m a board-certified psychiatrist in private practice in New Haven, Connecticut. I’m an assistant clinical professor at Yale University, where I did my training, and I’m the Chief Medical Officer of Beckley Clinical.

Owen : Tell me a little bit about how you decided to become a psychiatrist, and then we’ll get into what you specialize in. Five-minute version, and I’ll dig in if there’s something spicy.

Jordan : When I entered medical school, I really thought I’d become an infectious disease doctor focused on global health. I was inspired by Paul Farmer, who argued that even in Haiti, patients deserve the best drugs, full stop. He critiqued not just the medical models of care but the social environment in which healthcare gets delivered.

But I found through my training that what I really enjoyed most was talking to patients, and deeply understanding their struggles. I was less interested in microbes and the nuts and bolts of internal medicine, and more interested in experiences of suffering, and the realm of mental health.

Owen : What I’m hearing, just to check, is that you decided you liked talking to people, and didn’t really like the microbes. I didn’t hear any drop in your passion for public health.

Jordan : That is true. So initially I was trying to marry those, and was focused on global mental health. I was doing some work in Peru and Nepal, looking at how to improve systems of care in Andean communities and Nepalese mountain communities. That was work I was doing at the end of medical school and applying into residency.

At that point I had decided to apply into psychiatry, but with a focus on global health and public health approaches. I’d completed a master’s in medical anthropology before my medical training, so I was interested in cross-cultural approaches and being a bridge between conventional medicine and community-based, even traditional, approaches.

Owen : What’s an example of something you saw in Peru or Nepal that would be applicable here? I know people who’ve done work in Peru who told me there are basically ten drugs they can use. Give us an example of something that made sense in Peru that maybe makes even more sense here.

Jordan : In some of those communities, especially in South America, there was historically traditional use of psychedelic plants.

Owen : So they had extra drugs that you didn’t bring in the formulary.

Jordan : That was one piece. I was exposed to a real appreciation of ethnobotany, both in my training and in these communities. Only a small percentage of plant medicines are psychoactive, but those were gaining traction. I was aware of the historical use of psilocybin mushrooms and ayahuasca in Middle, Central, and South America. In Cusco, the Coca Museum showed me that what we’re told about coca is not the full story. There’s additional medical benefit there.

Right around when I was applying into residency, the initial studies from Hopkins and NYU had come out about psilocybin in cancer patients. I’d just been down in South America, and I was like, this is an area, as an anthropologist physician, that is of great interest to me. It requires bridging biomedical knowledge, the clinical trial context, and an understanding of how these things have been used historically. As well as a little bit of, well, let’s challenge the conventional norms around these medicines and see if we’re missing something.

That led me, even as a first-year resident, to design a trial of psilocybin for depression at Yale.

I like your question more broadly, though. There were other things, and you hinted at one of them: yes, they use a small number of drugs, but they use community health workers, and there’s a huge community outreach piece we don’t take advantage of in the U.S. They have this network that’s much more an extension of primary care right into the communities, going to people’s homes, checking on them, doing more home visits. Being interested in people’s living situations, their family, their relationships. To some extent, that actually inspired my private practice to be situated in the community where I live.

Owen : You saw this vast array of community support that we don’t do. And there’s some paucity of pharmacology. Do you feel like they were getting good outcomes? Was there something we can learn from what they did really well?

Jordan : There’s certainly no outcome tracking. In Nepal, there’s just an utter dearth of real healthcare infrastructure. What I was doing there was tracking what I called pathways to care: how do people who have mental health challenges even get any amount of help? The reality in a lot of places is that the first stop is some form of traditional healer. Some of the work was actually establishing relationships with the traditional healers to recognize, okay, what’s in your scope, and when is it outside your scope and this person needs a referral into the medical system.

Owen : Who are our traditional healers in the U.S.? In your community in New Haven, who would the analogous people be?

Jordan : Interesting question. I don’t know that we have them here. I think we’ve lost that.

Owen : My hunch is, it’s barbers. Bartenders. People you talk to about your problems.

Jordan : Yeah. The only difference is that the traditional healers in those communities embody the role. They know the gig. It’s their gig.

Owen : It’s not accidental.

Jordan : It should be the primary care doctors, right? Or the primary care workforce. But even that, as someone who grew up in Canada, access to primary care here is going to be pretty limited.

Owen : How’d you end up in the U.S.?

Jordan : After my medical anthropology master’s at the University of Edinburgh, I applied widely, both in Canada and the U.S. I got into Yale and thought that was a pretty good offer.

Owen : Yale was willing to take a flyer on an outlier. How many other anthropologists were in your class?

Jordan : None. There were quite a few global health-oriented people throughout the Yale Med School, which was a draw for me. I think they also saw I’d be a good fit in that burgeoning area where they had a foothold.

Owen : You fulfilled a mission for them, not just them for you.

Jordan : I think so. I think that’s how you want to position yourself.

Owen : So, you go from this interest in global health and anthropology, you realize psychiatry has the most to talk to patients about suffering, and you choose that. In your first year of residency, you designed a psilocybin trial. Tell me about that. That’s a level of ambition for the audience that is rare.

Jordan : Yeah, and possibly unwise. Yale had protected research time. The trial wouldn’t have been possible at most places.

As I was learning about the so-called “renaissance” of psychedelics, I don’t actually love that term.

Owen : Why not?

Jordan : It has a very Western connotation, for one.

Owen : What makes that bad to you?

Jordan : It prioritizes that lens over the fact that psychedelic plants and drugs had been used without a break in other parts of the world, in Indigenous communities. It centers a more Western narrative around them.

Owen : What would you call it?

Jordan : I think it’s a resurgence of psychedelic interest and research in the West. Even in the U.S., the war on drugs and the scheduling of these compounds as Schedule I, and the interruption in research that was sort of happening, was centered here. So it’s a U.S.-centric term, which is fine. There definitely was a break, and then a resurgence of interest in these compounds.

“A single dose of any medication isn’t going to reverse twenty years of depression.”

Owen : And so you found yourself at an organization, Beckley Clinical. Tell us about that.

Jordan: Just to bridge from the last part: the trial I designed at Yale was a trial of psilocybin-assisted psychotherapy in the treatment of depression, with a mechanistic arm looking at EEG and neuroplasticity. My interest was really in understanding how to deliver these interventions to patients in the best, most humane, and beneficial therapeutic way. I was quite interested in research, but really in treatment delivery models with psychedelics. How do you do this in this medical context?

There was the model developed in the 60s, and it was like, well, what’s the 2.0 version? How do we integrate knowledge from traditional uses of psychedelics with clinical approaches, and do it in a scientifically rigorous and ethical manner?

The field had been focused on treatment-refractory patients, just like ketamine had been initially. My sense was that for these patients, who had been struggling with depression and trauma for, on average, about 20 years in our sample, it just didn’t make a lot of sense to think a single dose of any medication would reverse that full course of illness. That tied into this idea of integration: what kind of psychotherapeutic lens might lend itself to ongoing work from the drug experience, which is limited in number?

Owen : When you say a single dose of medication wouldn’t do it, it’s not that you’re advocating for necessarily more doses of medication. If I’m hearing you right, you’re advocating for basically a post-surgical model? You get a surgery, which is a big thing, and then you get physical therapy to learn to walk again if it was your knee, because you have to learn to use your leg. Walking is a movement output of the brain that needs to relearn how to use the now-working knee. Question mark. Is that the right understanding?

Jordan : I think that’s a great analogy. We need research to determine optimal dosing, and I probably think more than one dose, on a repeat cycle. We have to understand the dosing frequency. In general, yes. You have these bigger experiences, certainly with higher doses of medicines like psilocybin, and then you do the ongoing work, whether you call it integration or post-work, to get to: okay, now what? You had a big insight, you had a therapeutic experience, maybe you have heightened neuroplasticity in your brain, which of course doesn’t last forever. How do we take advantage of that?

We used acceptance and commitment therapy in that particular study. I’m very open to different approaches, but essentially the bridge into Beckley Clinical was that I had done this work in combined drug and therapy interventions. That was resonant with the Fielding family, who runs the Beckley Foundation and the Beckley companies. The direction they wanted to take with Beckley Clinical was to raise the standard of care in behavioral health using these combined drug-and-therapy approaches. Luckily, they knocked on my door and asked if I’d be interested in that journey.

Owen : Pretty good fit. A guy who’s deeply interested in how to combine drugs and therapy should probably be running the medical operations for a drug-and-therapy company. So what’s the mission Beckley Clinical is trying to accomplish, using your skill set?

Jordan : To raise the standard of care in behavioral health using novel approaches, including drug-assisted and psychedelic-assisted therapies. Starting with ketamine and Spravato, and combining those with what I call wraparound psychotherapeutic support, which includes psychotherapy and lifestyle medicine interventions. With an eye towards incorporating other novel psychedelics as they become FDA-approved.

Owen : Amazing. I’m fascinated and want to do a follow-up. Consider this the prologue. Next time, we get into the meat of it.
