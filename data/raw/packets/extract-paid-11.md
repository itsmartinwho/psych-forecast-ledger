I have turned 45 today. I hate my birthday. I'm not sure that it's so much the day, but the date. May 6 is a perfectly good day, unless, of course, you would like to do something nice on your birthday. As a dual board-certified child and adult psychiatrist, I've done a lot of years of school. May is a beautiful month. Spring is a lovely time. It is also the end of the academic year when you've had as many academic years as your author; what is almost surely the case is that what happens on your birthday is an exam or obligation. I took no pre-medical classes in college because I was trying to prevent myself from medical school matriculation. In my Amherst College era, hubris, I thought I could reduce the risk of becoming a physician to zero simply by not taking the courses necessary to apply to medical school. This proved quixotic. Post-baccalaureate programs exist. You could do college after college to knock out those prerequisites, and I did. I did six years of college, including two additional years of preparatory classes.

Share

I have not celebrated my birthday for more than one out of the past ten years at least. I've seen people who have birthdays. They routinely have birthday parties. I made a cognitive error. I assumed that birthday parties were an emergent phenomenon of individuals with friends and family who cared about them. This may be the case, but I think it's a lot more likely that people who have birthday parties solicit them. They proactively let other people know they're having a birthday. They may even plan the party for themselves. I know this because I eventually asked someone who had a birthday party, and I thought it was great. And she—thanks —told me the secret. You have to plan it for yourself. It doesn't happen by magic. You might have it planned on your behalf, but you need a friend who is really into planning parties and, additionally, knows it's your birthday. Silently sulking about the fact that you haven't had a birthday party is an inferior way to have a birthday party. Your hunger for your birthday to be a party is non-obvious to others— what’s worse, it is ineffectual.

Oh, my God. The world isn't thinking about my birthday? I'm sorry to break it to you, self, but that's the case. Birthday parties require knowledge of the birthday itself, a fortunate date, friends, access to cake, and a variety of other factors. Consider those unfortunate souls born on Christmas or the New Year. For me, it is a paper, or a meeting of the American Psychiatric Association at which one finds one’s self doing some presenting of academic content about self-disclosure.

Birthdays change in quality and character as we age in ways that are almost impossible to imagine before we've had enough of them. After a certain threshold, which varies for everybody—but becomes inevitable for all—each new year brings more infirmity, sickness, and limitations. The “ability to smoke,” “legal drinking age,” or “ability to rent a car?” Even the most gentle of time’s ravages—a snake biting a bit too much of its own tail—become colon cancer screening and looking “great for one’s age.”

For some of us, the relentlessness of chronology finds a way to let us know things will never be better. Psychiatric illness can feel catastrophic when you're young. You can feel like it will rule your whole life. And I have bad news for you: worse is coming. The death rate is holding constant at 100%. Death is a categorical outcome—you are alive or dead, with the exception of vampires. But something, for almost everybody, leads to death. There are the lucky few who died abruptly from something no one could've seen coming. The rest of us get sick. Perhaps years earlier than we are going to die.

There is a point in time when each person realizes that they won't ever be more attractive. It's only going to get worse. It's possible you're wrong, and a person might get even hotter for a while. Medicine exists, and it's getting better, and you could be one of those lucky adults who looks better older than they did younger. But that snake, too, will turn. We fetishize youth. We adore it. It speaks to things deep inside our brains, and even if we didn't have societal pressures, our hearts skip beats for beautiful, youthful people more frequently than beautiful older adults. I have to like it. I don't have to condone it. I do have to notice that, at 90 or at 20, we all are going downhill at some point.

Although the same is not entirely the case for intelligence and cognitive abilities, it's pretty close to true in that regard, also. We all fade. You gain insights, wisdom, and more in the process of aging, as well, and then that, too, is taken away from you at the end.

Heartbreak is the price we pay for love. It's the ticket to the movie, and you pay it at the end. As a psychiatrist, who also has done a fair bit of therapy, I'm in the process of people in a state of hopelessness about their lives. One of the ways I tend to do that is counterintuitive. I say the kind of thing I wrote above. I say outrageously depressing things like, "I guarantee your life will get worse.” I try to put a little smile on my face because I attempted to provide some sort of cognitive tripwire so that they could challenge their hopelessness. Most are expecting someone to tell them it will “get better.” So telling someone it's going to get worse?

Unexpected Magic!

Life gets worse until you die. This is either grim or a call to action to live. Damnit, live. We have now and maybe tomorrow, but it is not unlimited. Love, connect, live, now, tomorrow, but soon. A dream deferred? Over a long enough time scale, it is dust.

In any individual moment, it can be great. And we all get to choose, for ourselves, if this moment will be one of those moments.

Time and timelessness are connected. This moment and eternity are struggling within us, and that is the source of all of our contradictions.

—Arvo Part

I've come to a horrifying realization. First, I will set it up for you. I never liked the Grateful Dead. I listen to Phish— despite having matriculated for high school to the Taft school in Watertown Connecticut, the alma mater of Trey, their singer. I don't use recreational drugs. I never have. I've never had a drink. I looked down on my high school classmates who like to get high. I didn't really hang out with people in college who were getting high. I was also, and I'm not proud of this, judgmental of the behavior. Why do you want to get high all the time?!

Share

Maybe, maybe, the reason my classmates were using so many recreational substances—prominently psychedelic compounds—is that they may be potent. We use the term “self-medicating” quite frequently in medicine. We mean it as an insult. To denigrate the practice. I'm not advocating “taking just anything” to treat medical conditions.

However, one of the guiding principles in my life is, at this point, assuming people do things for reasons that makes sense to them. My job is to try to understand why it makes sense.

Today, compass pathways published more top-line data on the use of their psilocybin analog, COMP360, in the treatment of post traumatic stress disorder. It is remarkable.

This is phase II data. As a reminder for my readers, in the FDA approval process, phase 2 trials are enrolling individuals who have the condition we are interested in treating, but they are powered to determine dosage and safety, not designed to enroll enough individuals to demonstrate efficacy. These are smaller studies. We are not expecting big differences in small sample sizes, generally.

Post-traumatic stress disorder sucks. By its very nature, it is a biology by environment interaction. We have to experience something horrible, often life-threatening, and our nervous system learns the lesson: there is a threat, and we need to be ready.

“Ready for horror” equals a series of unpleasant experiences and reactions that are maladaptive in a context other than horror. This starts with disruptions in memory, having either flashbacks of difficulty remembering what happened during the event. There is also a whole spectrum of hyper-vigilance: exaggerated startle responses to loud noises or reminders, feeling on edge, quick to anger, disrupted sleep— you're always ready to wake up to save your life from the horror that you encountered. You can have nightmares. Even in your sleep you're reminded to be ready, the horror could come again, you need to be prepared in every single moment.

A pervasive sense of dread is common, and the inability to experience joy or pleasure, all the time, is also common. Suffering with PTSD can make you feel like you're wrong, the world is wrong, and no one is to be trusted.

Avoidance of reminders of the traumatic events are also common. Sufferers avoid places, things, they don't want to be reminded. They learn to be afraid and live in fear. Often, they build their lives around that avoidance.

PTSD can become so severe that individuals will experience psychosis, visual or auditory hallucinations. This is rare.

Even if you know you have post-traumatic stress disorder, your nervous system will react faster than your conscious mind can regulate. There is a combination of genetic vulnerability to developing PTSD, and of course exposure to something horrible. It is more common in individuals to do something horrible was done on purpose, with intention:

I don't like being on edge all the time. I don't like not having easy access to feelings that are positive. It's different than depression, which I had become familiar with since my childhood. About 25% of Psychiatrists are in my position, having experienced stalking. Workplace violence is also common for mental health professionals, including nurses, staff and physicians. We understand that these risks occur, but honestly, most of us would probably choose to be punched in the face in the morning if it meant we didn't have to experience post traumatic stress disorder symptoms.

It's an extremely common problem for veterans, police officers, firefighters, end, and other individuals who serve us all.

There's more hope today than before.

We don't have the published studies yet, we're just getting results at 12 weeks after the conclusion of the phase 2 trial for COMP360. However, here is what it looks like, and I really look forward to the results in subsequent phase 3 trials.

At baseline, participants scored an average of 47.5 on the CAPS-5 symptom scale, indicating they had severe PTSD. Compass tracked a mean 29.9-point reduction after four weeks. The reduction was almost identical, 29.5 points, after 12 weeks. More than half of participants had a score of 20 or less, meaning they were in remission, at Week 12. Two adverse events of suicidal ideation resolved during the study.

These are huge reductions in symptoms! Further:

81.8% response (reduction of ≥ 15 points in CAPS-5 score), 63.6% remission (total CAPS-5 ≤ 20) rates at week 4 with 77.3% response and 54.5% remission at week 12

This is after a single dose of treatment, and subsequent psychological support.

More than half of people didn't have PTSD anymore 12 weeks later.

For many individuals, PTSD remit. But it often takes a long time:

lifetime remission rate was 92% and median time to remission was 14 years. Those who experienced childhood trauma, interpersonal violence, severe symptoms or a secondary anxiety or affective disorder were less likely to remit from PTSD and reported longer median times to remission compared to those with other trauma experiences, less severe symptoms or no co-morbidity.1

For many of my high school classmates, recreational drug use was a vice. However, for some, and for millennia before our modern era, it may be that compound like psilocybin were the way we were recovered from the stress we encountered as humans.

If we lost our way, I hope this may be rediscovering a road back to healing. There's a science left to do: we need to understand for whom this treatment will be the most appropriate. I'm not encouraging people to go out and use drugs recreationally— medical contexts are different. Aside from opining on the medical benefits of steel cut oats or walking, it's not really my place to address, as a physician, what people choose to do recreationally or in a ceremonial context.

I am allowed to wonder, however. Was some of the “ self-medicating” actually healing, meaningfully?

I suspect I was more judgmental of peers-who may have been suffering—than I could've been. I don't think I'm willing to start listening to the Grateful Dead.

Even a curmudgeon like myself? We can find ourselves traumatized, and there may be more paths to heal than we imagined.

---- text ----
The trend towards using a medical model for legalization of formally illegal substances has been undeniably successful. Cannabis was just rescheduled as a schedule III compound, which means it has legitimate medical use according to the DEA. One of the ways in which cannabis has been promoted, is as a treatment for post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD). This seems strange to me, as a psychiatrist, because the data I had seen was unconvincing. I've seen a lot of people smoke it, and… It didn't seem like it was doing much that was useful. The plural of anecdote? It is not evidence. So, in today's column, I'm going to review the data on the role of cannabis in PTSD.

The cannabis plant has myriad different compounds. Generally, the psychoactive compounds are considered to be primarily THC and to a much lesser extent, CBD:

It contains cannabinoids and several other classes of chemical compounds acting on the cannabinoid receptors. Specifically, Δ-9-tetrahydrocannabinol (THC) is a highly lipophilic alkaloid and is the primary psychoactive ingredient in marijuana, varying from 0.2% to 30% potency per plant and strain; whilst cannabidiol (CBD) is a non-psychotomimetic cannabinoid, with neuroprotective, analgesic, sedative antiemetic, antispasmodic, anti-inflammatory, and anxiolytic properties.1

When's the last time you saw the word “whilst?” I knew there was a reason to write this newsletter on Substack—it’s a place for writers! Ugh. Ok, whilst I got distracted by that less-than-usual word choice, we will pull our act together and move forward with the evidence-assessing process.

It turns out that most pharmacologic treatments for PTSD also are modestly helpful at best, according to the highest level evidence reviews we have, done by our friends at the Cochrane group:

We include 66 RCTs in the review (range: 13 days to 28 weeks; 7442 participants; age range 18 to 85 years) and 54 in the meta-analysis.

It’s a very large sample!

For the primary outcome of treatment response, we found evidence of beneficial effect for selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) compared with placebo (risk ratio (RR) 0.66, 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.59 to 0.74; 8 studies, 1078 participants), which improved PTSD symptoms in 58% of SSRI participants compared with 35% of placebo participants, based on moderate-certainty evidence.

Now, unfortunately, because the Cochrane review uses a relative risk and not effect size, we have to do some math to compare to anything else I discuss in this newsletter, so I hope you'll bear with me as I walk you through that process.

First, we need to convert the relative risk to an effect size (Cohen's d):

So, a relative risk of 0.66 corresponds to an effect size (Cohen's d) of approximately -0.229. This indicates a small to medium effect, where the negative sign shows that the outcome (PTSD symptoms) are less likely in the treatment group compared to the control group. This is to say, SSRIs are modestly helpful. However, using the Muir-Skee Lo Emotionally Corrective Equation to convert that effect size to height, we multiply the effect size (in units of standard deviations) by size of the standard deviation in the height of the average American male human (2.5”), and get the following…

Share

“If SSRI were a drug for increased height, how much height would they add (in individuals for this indication)?”

SSRIs' benefit equals only an extra 0.5725” taller. Which is not…baller.

It's also worth noting that cannabis had a withdrawal phenomenon as an Adverse Effect in individuals treated for PTSD:

For the outcome of treatment withdrawal, we found evidence of a harm for the individual SSRI agents compared with placebo (RR 1.41, 95% CI 1.07 to 1.87; 14 studies, 2399 participants). Withdrawals were also higher for the separate SSRI paroxetine group compared to the placebo group (RR 1.55, 95% CI 1.05 to 2.29; 5 studies, 1101 participants).

Using the same math above, it's a relatively small effect size for harm in this cohort, 0.190. This would shave 0.45 inches from height, using the M-SLo EQE. It's just about as likely to hurt you in withdrawal as it is to help you in reducing your symptoms. For those who remember my myriad reviews of antipsychotic medications, it's worth noting that none of them panned out as helpful in PTSD:

There was however no evidence of beneficial effect for the number of participants who improved with the antipsychotics (RR 0.51, 95% CI 0.16 to 1.67; 2 studies, 43 participants) compared to placebo, based on very low-certainty evidence.

Now that we have a baseline, we can move on to taking a look at the evidence for cannabis.

We know that traditional pharmacologic agents are a pretty low bar. We have some data that, particularly in veterans, cannabis might be harmful—but keep in mind, we can't address causality from observational longitudinal studies:

a longitudinal, observational study evaluating the association between cannabis use and PTSD symptom severity recruited 2,276 PTSD participants admitted to specialized veterans’ treatment programs, classified into four groups according to their cannabis use: those without cannabis use at admission or after discharge (“never used”); those who used cannabis at admission but not after discharge (“stoppers”); those who used at admission and after discharge (“continuing users”) and those using cannabis after discharge but not at admission (“starters”) [38],

So, here we have a little bit of a natural experiment, we have individuals with PTSD, who used cannabis, who didn't use cannabis, and who started and who stopped. If medical cannabis were an effective intervention for PTSD, we'd probably expect to see a reduction in problems in the lives of those who started or continued. We wouldn’t expect it to get worse…oops:

…interestingly, cannabis use was significantly associated with worse outcomes in PTSD symptom severity, violent behavior, and measures of alcohol and drug use. At follow-up, stoppers and never users had the lowest levels of PTSD symptomatology whilst starters showed the highest levels of violent behavior.2

There's that whilst word again! But we can't let it distract us because it pointed out that the people who started treatment with medicinal cannabis for their PTSD showed the highest levels of violent behavior.

Now, it's certainly possible that people who chose to start cannabis were those who had the highest risk for violent behavior. Or they were refractory to other treatments already, and people were using cannabis as a last-ditch effort. The one thing it's really hard to assume is that cannabis is broadly helpful in post-traumatic stress disorder. This is similar to my argument regarding psychiatric hospitalization, which I've made on prior occasions, in which we see only worse outcomes in a hard-to-randomize cohort. We can argue whether it's causally harmful, but it's very hard to argue that it's causally helpful, given the evidence only suggests harm.

It's also worth noting that that paper, from when the data came, was done by a veteran researcher in veterans trauma, Dr. Robert A. Rosenheck. He's the author of many serious papers on the treatment of veterans—so we may be tempted to believe his findings slightly more than that of others.

When it comes to CBD, the non-psychoactive component of cannabis, we have one retrospective study that indicates it was well tolerated:

A retrospective open-label study examining CBD oral administration in flexible doses on PTSD symptomatology for 8 weeks reported a significant reduction in PTSD severity and intensity. CBD was well tolerated, and no patients discontinued treatment due to side-effects.3

But if we learned anything from this newsletter, one retrospective open-label study does not, an evidence-base, make.

What are the issues in cannabis research in PTSD has been the high risk of bias. A 2021 paper looked at the risk of bias across all PTSD studies and found that the risk of bias was high across many of them.

Which, of course, means that when we plop all that data into a meta-analysis, we get “bias in, bias out” as a plausible risk in the data.

Evidence from the included studies was mainly based on non-randomized studies with no comparators. Results from unpooled, high [Risk of Bias] studies showed that cannabis was associated with a reduction in overall PTSD symptoms and improved QOL. Dry mouth, headaches, and psychoactive effects such as agitation and euphoria were the commonly reported adverse effects. In most studies, cannabis was well tolerated, but small proportions of patients experienced a worsening of PTSD symptoms.4

Post-traumatic stress disorder has been a hard nut to crack, with predisposition and exposure to trauma combining to make a particularly refractory set of problems. Oral medication and cannabis both have modest effect sizes (in highly biased samples in the case of cannabis).

Additionally, individuals treated with cannabis for their PTSD, they were also being treated with a variety of other pharmacologic agents:

In most included studies, cannabis was administered in combination with other pharmacological agents, in varying potencies and through different routes such as tablets, oral sprays, or in powder form. The duration of response to cannabis was also variable among the studies.5

This it makes it even harder to know what was helpful!

Our veterans deserve better. The vast majority of people with PTSD are, of course, women who suffered sexual assault in the general population. But, for a reason I'm not gonna get into, we tend to focus on veterans. OK, fine. I mean, not fine. We routinely ignore women's health in research for decades, and it's unconscionable. But at least, in the case of cannabis as a treatment for PTSD, by legislative Fiat, we've also ignored the needs of our veterans.

Everybody deserves better. Everybody. Veterans, physicians who've been stalked, which is also very common, and people who have been in the ICU setting, which is also very common, everybody. Everybody deserves better than they're getting right now. We need better treatments for trauma, and when we have them, we need to pay for them. We need better than current oral medications, and we need better than cannabis.

It doesn't escape anybody's notice that MDMA is going through the FDA-approval process with Lykos Therapeutics, and other hopeful publications are coming out. These include psilocybin analogs for PTSD, and magnesium-ibogaine for PTSD. There are FDA-Breakthrough treatments like the Nightwear device for PTSD-related nightmares. There is the advent of PRISM EEG-Neurofeedback:

During Prism therapy, a computer simulation and EEG headset create an immersive environment to train individuals to gain control over amygdala-based biomarker activity associated with PTSD.

There is a course talk therapy for PTSD, which is an entirely separate article! In closing, here is Ben Everett, Ph.D., from Lykos at our recent RAMHT event who speaks about the FDA-Breakthrough process:

Cannabis doesn't work that well. It may be useful for other things, and I can say the same thing about oral medications, but if you're looking for relief from your PTSD, I don't know that cannabis is the first stop. I think scientists, regulators, and people who pay for treatment need to get extremely serious about the needs of individuals with PTSD and not keep us all waiting. More Breakthroughs, please.

The Frontier Psychiatrists is a daily-enough health health-themed publication. Today, I’m reviewing the data on GLP-1 drugs (short for Glucagon-like Peptide-1) as they apply to substance use disorders. I’ve written about my journey with weight loss and DM II, which, thanks to these drugs, I no longer have.

To set the stage, addiction sucks. We also have limited treatments available to relieve the suffering of addictive disorders. Here is a chart1:

We don't have that much. We have no biological treatments approved for the treatment of cocaine or stimulant use disorders. We have a few studies on transcranial magnetic stimulation23, one of which I wrote 4!

Reward circuitry is hard to overcome because it's crucial to our existence. I've written before about the role of the nucleus accumbens (NAc), a structure deep in the brain where dopamine binds to receptors that give us the sense of feeling good. Without the NAc, we wouldn't have goal-directed behavior, and we would all die. Nothing would be worth it. Dopamine release in this small structure deep in the brain is what we mean by “worth it.”

Thus, we can understand that biological treatments intervening in this crucial structure will have a hard time getting it to accomplish anything other than what it's biologically attuned to. The problem with addictive drugs is that they jack into this reward system and have an extremely hard time being pried out.

Addictions are sticky because staying alive is sticky, and feeling good is sticky because of course it is. We do have a recent example of a series of compounds that altered the underlying biology in the direction of health. That's glucagon-like peptide 1 drugs. This includes Semeglutide (aka Ozempic and Wegovy) and related compounds.

These medications address satiety signals in the brain—the sense of having had “enough” which extra-sugar does a good job of breaking. A nice review in BMJ5 recently addressed the evidence, which is mostly pre-clinical (we've done science in animals, and we have trials enrolling in humans, but we don't have evidence from those trials published yet). Here are some of the proposed mechanisms:

We know in animal models, we get reduced alcohol consumption when we have GLP one pre-treatment. We also know that we get reduced cocaine self-administration, again in animal models, with GLP -1 administration6. Humans and animals are different. However, our reward systems are ancient and preserved throughout evolution because if something works, keep it around!

The human clinical data we have is limited, but it comes from a study on individuals treated for diabetes mellitus, which looked at how much alcohol they drank and noticed it was less7:

Thus, patients treated with liraglutide reported reduced alcohol intake compared to patients who received other diabetic treatments (Kalra, 2011). This tentative interaction was explored in a recently conducted randomized clinical trial of patients with AUD treated with the GLP-1R agonist exenatide (similar to Ex4). In this study, exenatide does not alter alcohol intake in AUD patients who were of regular weight (Klausen et al., 2022). However, in this initial report, exenatide modestly reduced alcohol intake in AUD patients who were overweight (Klausen et al., 2022).

Science is about noticing. And then, after you've noticed, check to see if the thing you've noticed holds up in subsequent evaluations. We have a signal—that's what we call noticing in science—that GLP-1s might be helpful for addictions.

The SEMALCO Study is currently still recruiting. now, the evidence doesn't support treating people with primary diction with these difficult-to-pay-for, difficult-source medications. It is a call to action to do further research and for those who are not clinical trialists, to consider participating in that research as subjects if we're eligible.

Yesterday was clinical trials day. I consider every day “clinical trials day” on this newsletter, but it's worth noting that for ethical research to proceed, people have to volunteer. We're all relying on each other to step up and be experimented on. The thing that makes that OK is consent. I've been enrolled in several research studies as a subject in my life, and so have my children.

Participating in clinical trials is an excellent way to get back to the universe, and I will note, for people who are worried, there's a lot of oversight and clinical trials that isn't the case in clinical practice. I'm not saying it is safe; I am saying the risks will be carefully documented if there is an adverse outcome. And for some people, particularly those with cancer, or other severe illnesses, of which I would include addiction at this point, peoples lives are on the line, when it comes to clinical trials. What Did The FDA Advisory Committee Hear?

Many of those sharing their stories with the FDA highlighted the least recognized domain of PTSD symptoms—a pervasive change in thinking about the world and one’s self toward the negative. Mistrust, fear, dread, and anger become the compass points of one's experience after another exposure to trauma. In the DSM-5-TR, these are in “Cluster C” when it comes to PTSD—which, by the way, the MAPS MDMA-AT therapy manual only does a scant job of describing and, frankly, is off from how the DSM-5-TR handles the disorder in an avoidable confusing way:

1.1 MDMA for PTSD

PTSD is a serious public health problem that causes significant suffering and contributes substantially to healthcare costs. A complex biopsychosocial condition, PTSD is characterized by a combination of three types of symptoms:

1. Hyper-arousal symptoms, such as hypervigilance, anxiety, and sleep disturbance.

2. Intrusive re-experiencing of traumatic experiences, such as intrusive memories, nightmares, or flashbacks.
3. Avoidance symptoms, including emotional numbing and withdrawal.

For my readers who read yesterday, you will note that the criteria for PTSD are quite a bit more extensive than what is above. There are four domains of symptoms:

A: Intrusive memories

B: Avoidance

C: Negative changes in thinking and mood

D: Changes in physical and emotional reactions

What the MAPS manual lists as “avoidance?” These are classified as “negative changes in thinking and mood.” I think it's a little strange that the manual for therapy to treat PTSD doesn't include the official DSM-5-TR diagnostic criteria for PTSD and miscategorizes others.

I will take a moment to say a little bit more about PTSD, as I experienced it as someone who happens to be a psychiatrist— with the disorder. On Functional Unblinding…Measurement Matters

Not every clinical trial uses an inert placebo. Randomization in placebo control is a hallmark of clinical trial design, but I would argue that preserving the double-blind is vastly more important than the placebo's inertness.

In prior trials of ketamine, blinding was (attempted) to be preserved by psychoactive drugs that did not create the intended treatment effect. Often, midazolam is the comparator — the issues with blinding and expectancy bias matter. Placebo is a remarkable treatment. When the folks at MAPS (per STAT News) were fond of saying:

“We already know MDMA works; we just need to prove it in some way,”

It’s worth remembering the same can be said, accurately, about placebo (the following is from a paper comparing placebo to any treatment. Not just in psychiatry!):

In trials with continuous outcomes (n = 115) we found no difference between treatment and placebo effects (MD = −0.29, 95% CI −0.62 to 0.05, P = 0.10).

The measurement tools we use matter. Using change in rating scales and measuring changes in those outcomes, even with blinded raters, is fraught because placebo is just as effective as active treatments in those trials. This is not because a placebo doesn’t work. It’s because it DOES work. It’s potent. The question should not be, at a pharma company running clinical trials, whether a drug “works.”

OF COURSE, THEY WORK—JUST LIKE PLACEBO.

Being enrolled in a clinical trial—in either group— is an effective way to get most depression or trauma better. The relevant question is: does the active agent work better than the placebo? One way of addressing that is using a categorical, binary endpoint:

In trials with binary outcomes (n = 37) treatments were significantly more effective than placebos (RRR = 0.72, 95%CI = 0.61 to 0.86, P = 0.0003). Treatment and placebo effects were not different in 22 out of 28 predefined subgroup analyses. Of the six subgroups with differences treatments were more effective than placebos in five. However when all criteria for reducing bias were ruled out (continuous outcomes) placebos were more effective than treatments (MD = 1.59, 95% CI = 0.40 to 2.77, P = 0.009).1

When we're trying to prove that a difference exists between two powerful tools— one of those things is a placebo? We should bias our clinical trial science towards categorical outcomes as a primary endpoint.

This is why I write so much about remission! It's not just that remission is better; remission versus not remission is a binary endpoint. Thus, they are less likely to have the erroneous outcome of a categorical variable. This may be counterintuitive—that the measure we look at can change the ability of a clinical trial to show a difference. However, the people in my readership who generate evidence: use a binary primary endpoint.

When you combine binary endpoints with active comparators, you improve the chance of preserving the blinding. The best placebo for this purpose will be one that has identical psychoactive effects but no treatment effects. That might not exist. But we all know that street drugs are adulterated all the time, and people don't necessarily know what they're taking. I would argue. It's a really good idea for psychedelic research, large, to identify a very psychoactive, but very ineffective compound to use as a comparator across trials. We need a psychoactive control that doesn't do anything useful thereafter. Drug development for a psychoactive placebo, please!

Know the Placebo Response Rates in the Disorder You Are Studying

The following data is from a met an analysis of just the placebo response in various kinds of clinical trials for psychiatric illness using SSRIs. We're not looking at the drugs; we're just looking at the placebo group and how much better they got, depending on the condition being studied. You're going to notice—there's a difference! Depression, in a clinical trial setting, is much more responsive to a placebo than obsessive-compulsive disorder, for example.2

This is just the placebo group!

The kinds of people in clinical trials who might have been given antidepressant medications—but instead received a placebo? IT IS A BLOCKBUSTER TREATMENT!!! You all noticed that the effect size in depression and the effects and anxiety was larger than that reported by MAPS/Lykos. These are double blind placebo control trials. They didn't have the problem with functioning unblinding, and yet the placebo group still did remarkably well. That's (perhaps) because the blind was preserved. Any anti-depressant medication evaluated in the studies had to do better than placebo. My argument is that it's a high bar.

The other thing to note in the above graph is that it's a lot less true in OCD. In the condition you're studying, some conditions are more responsive to placebo than others. And some cohorts for disorder are more responsive than others. For example, after four failed medication trials, the placebo response in major depressive disorder drops off precipitously. Thus, it's a lot easier to show a difference between treatment and placebo-highly treatment-resistant depression than depression, which is not so refractory.

The people you enroll in the study matter. The 40% of individuals who had previously been exposed to MDMA in a study about MDMA biases the results in favor of MDMA. This means 100% of people who had a terrible experience will not enroll in the trial. No one's going to risk getting a drug they responded to poorly previously, even combined with a theoretically novel psychotherapy protocol. It biases the results of the study to show fewer adverse effects because everyone who tried the drug previously is at low risk of adverse effects compared to a drug-naive population.

This brings me to my third recommendation: the best populations in which two studies these compounds are the least likely to have a robust response. It makes sense to start with depression, but treatment-resistant depression trials need to enroll highly treatment-resistant patients to be more sure that the difference will be because of the agent. The same is likely true of PTSD trials.

Psychedelic medicine studies, particularly for MDMA, need to enroll MDMA naïve patients with highly treatment-refractory conditions. In layman's terms, the next MDMA trial should study patients who've never used recreational drugs and tried every traditional treatment that could be expected to work for trauma.

The NY Times featured— yesterday—a lukewarm op-ed by Vikek Murthy, M.D., MBA, the Surgeon General of the United States. My wife, is of the opinion that a subscription to The NY Times is the kind of thing you just need to have as an adult. It’s like life insurance or buckling your seat belt. In that august pulication, Dr. Murthy is advocating for panicked responses to the mental health crisis… as public policy:

One of the most important lessons I learned in medical school was that in an emergency, you don’t have the luxury to wait for perfect information. You assess the available facts, you use your best judgment, and you act quickly.

As I have previously opined…how to put this delicately? Ok…I’ll go with this is a Bad Idea (Owen Muir, M.D., in “The Mental Health Crisis…Crisis”):

We do not have a mental health crisis. We have a mental health crisis…crisis. We have become so panicked by the relentless onslaught of early, preventable death, loss, and sorrow that we cannot address it. The crisis frame creates panicked responses. Those responses have served up more death, suffering, and isolation.

Dr. Murthy is not a child psychiatrist or pediatric subspecialist. He’s an MD/MBA with training in internal medicine. He, however, promises us he has found the villain in the children’s mental health crisis:

The mental health crisis among young people is an emergency — and social media has emerged as an important contributor.

He goes on to remind us all that correlation and causation are not that important to disambiguate:

Adolescents who spend more than three hours a day on social media face double the risk of anxiety and depression symptoms, and the average daily use in this age group, as of the summer of 2023, was 4.8 hours. Additionally, nearly half of adolescents say social media makes them feel worse about their bodies.

These data are important but not causal, randomized, or a solid basis for public policy—according to me. According to the doctor who actually gets to set public health policy, they are a call to action to do…something. In this case, he takes to the Grey Lady to call for a Warning Label—a hammer in his belt as a Surgeon General:

It is time to require a surgeon general’s warning label on social media platforms, stating that social media is associated with significant mental health harms for adolescents. A surgeon general’s warning label, which requires congressional action, would regularly remind parents and adolescents that social media has not been proved safe.

As a child psychiatrist, I will take a moment to remind Dr. Murthy: This is a time-tested strategy! If we want less of a behavior in teens, we regularly remind them — that fun thing your friends are doing? STOP IT. It isn’t isn’t safe. Of course all adolescents stop doing it. They act in keeping with the stern rules of their par—wait a minute.

This has been considered before. One parent, Mr. Capulet— Juliet’s father—tried a similar technique. In Romeo and Juliet, he takes a firm stand in favor of marriage to a man who’s not Romeo:

“to go with Paris to Saint Peter’s Church, or I will drag thee on a hurdle thither” (III.V.154)

Juliet, famously, was not having it:

“he shall not make me there a joyful bride” (III.V.117)

Reminding adolescents that something—Murthy’s proposed plan— isn’t safe? This is an awesome strategy to encourage more of that behavior. Adolescent brains don’t work exactly like adult brains, and they are particularly sensitive to social context, a concept called approach sensitization”

That is, neural responses to positively valenced socioemotional stimuli—in this case, responses that do not even reach the level of conscious awareness—may sensitize approach responding to unrelated incentive cues.1

That is to say, the social approval of your friends makes you more likely to do it, even if you aren’t aware that is what is happening. I’ve previously noted that even walking isn’t proven safe. Maybe we can get a warning label on that? How about treatment for depression with oral medication in adolescents? That has a black box warning for suicidal ideation also. After that warning label went on, deaths among youth increased2:

The warnings about suicidal ideation on antidepressant meds was followed by more overdose suicide attempts in adolescents.

Or what about antipsychotic medicines in youth on Medicaid? Oh, that also kills 127/100,000 more youth a year as well— 500% more deaths in that age group than fentanyl.

It’s also important to note that the data on how adolescents interpret warning labels indicates they are often more confused about what they mean than the label-makers might expect:3

Teens don’t understand warnings labels the way the label makers always think.

He makes the assertion that warning labels about cigarettes are an effective public health intervention. There is some data to back this up, but it’s not unambiguous. Graphics on cigarettes that have low emotional content outperform text only in randomized trials (from Nicotine and Tobacco Research, October 2017), in which they were randomized to three groups: text warnings, low emotion pictures, and high emotion pictures:

Participants in the high-emotion condition reported greater emotional reaction than text-only participants (bAdult = 0.21; bTeen = 0.27, p’s < .004);4

While, on the contrary:

Those in the low-emotion condition reported lower emotional reaction than text-only participants (bAdult = −0.18; bTeen = −0.22, p’s < .018).

That is, some graphics underperformed text only warnings. Comparing teens and adults, again, the content of the warnings mattered:

Stronger emotional reaction was associated with increased risk perceptions in both samples (bAdult= 0.66; bTeen = 0.85, p’s < .001) and greater quit intentions among adults (bAdult = 1.00, p < .001).

It is worth noting that the intention to quit was only higher in adults, not youth. Low emotion warnings led to lower quit intentions in that tobacco warning study:

Compared to text-only warnings, low-emotion warnings were associated with reduced risk perceptions and quit intentions whereas high-emotion warnings were associated with increased risk perceptions and quit intentions.

In the world of tobacco warnings science, it’s not hard to imagine what kind of pictures one could use to warn—horrible cancers, blighted lungs, disgusting teeth, and more. What does Dr. Murthy imagine we will use as the content of the warning labels? Frightening pictures of posts with no likes? A TikTok video of your Gen X mother using Facebook to respond with an emoji she doesn’t understand? Cringe!

Perhaps we need a warning label on warning labels from regulators and surgeons general?
