About a year or two ago, I started working on a book pitch. This format, to be honest, is loathsome. However, to sell a book, you write a pitch. It’s a lot about market sizing and generating the impression that the eventual book will sell a lot of copies with minimal work from the publisher. I got two “bites,” but they ended up being nibbles , not bids. Rather than let this rot, I figure I’ll share an unsuccessful book pitch with you, dear readers. It was so close! Now you, my dear readers, can imagine how great this book might have been, but live in the dreadful shadow world knowing it’s not going to be in your hands any time soon, unless you are personally in control of a publishing house.

Not everything we do will be a success. That is ok. I have written a lot since then. I’m a better writer for it. Here is the pitch for a book that wasn’t. The format is solid, however, so if you are planning on pitching a book, feel free to reference it.

Overview

“Do you wanna have a milkshake?”

Maggie was wearing a polka-dot dress with a bright purple polka-dot scrunchie in her hair. She stood awkwardly, unsteadily, in her patent leather shoes, on top of the couch in my office. The awkwardness in her balance was exacerbated by the fact that she was trying to push herself upward to grasp the other end of the electrical cable that was wrapped around her neck as she flung it up over an exposed pipe.

Maggie was trying to hang herself.

“Wait, what?” said Maggie, with her eyes locked onto mine, momentarily distracted from the effort she had been making to end her life in my doctor’s office.

“Do… you… want… to have a milkshake?”

“Like, a milkshake milkshake?”

“Yeah, there’s a milkshake place that makes milkshakes with cold brew, it’s just a couple of blocks away. Oddfellows. Ice cream. Cold brew. Coffee milkshake?”

Maggie slowly let the electrical cord that was wrapped around her neck fall away. Her heels dropped down onto the pillow of the couch. Much less precarious. Good.

“I have a question. Do they have other ice cream flavors? Other than coffee?” Maggie was 25, but she looked and talked like someone much younger.

“They have a variety of ice cream flavors. It’s an ice cream shop. It’s right down the street, do you want to go?”

“You mean and not call the police because I’m trying to kill myself again?”

“That is my plan, yes.”

“You know, I have to go see Hamilton with my dad tonight. And I was going to do it after I kill myself here, but I guess I could just go to the play. I really don’t want to go to the hospital.”

“I know you don’t want to go to the hospital. Besides, I have a headache. The coffee will help. Come on.”

Maggie stepped down from the sofa, tossed the electrical-cable-turned-noose to the side. There, it resumed its electrical cable identity, and she followed me out the door of the office and onto the Brooklyn sidewalk.

Yes, I’m a psychiatrist. Yes, some version of that actually happened.

What I did there, convincing someone not to end their life by offering them an alternative that they didn’t expect—that’s my superpower in action. It’s all about understanding what emotional state a person’s in and helping them shift out of that state to something that’s more beneficial to them and you. You do this first by gaining their trust and then getting them curious about something. No matter how upset they are, no matter how certain about the path they’re on, you engage their curiosity. This superpower is what I’m going to teach you in this book. You don’t have to be a doctor to use it. You don’t have to be in serious self-endangerment for it to help you. You just have to understand some really basic information, get a little bit of practice, and you can practice it on yourself. You can be the Jedi master of your own life.

It’s called mentalizing .

Cool, right?

Like, ice cream headache cool. It’s so cool, it’ll make your head hurt. That’s how cool we’re talking.

I did not invent mentalizing, although I’ve written a book on the subject for professionals and teach it at places like the Mayo Clinic, The Menninger Clinic, and conferences worldwide. I worked with Spotify to help its employees in the aftermath of the George Floyd protests, so that workers felt seen and heard. The theory came about in the 1960s, but wasn’t an actual treatment until the 1990s in England in the hands of Hungarian psychologist Peter Fonagy and British psychiatrist Anthony Bateman. The simplest definition: The process by which we understand our own—or someone else’s—mental state and how that state is connected to behavior . Up until now, only therapists and scientists have had access to these awesome tools. This will change once people read this book.

I use mentalizing to treat people whose brains are doing things that most people’s brains aren’t doing. Sometimes that means treating people in extreme situations like Maggie. Sometimes it means helping patients who suffer from crippling depression or OCD (which, surprisingly, contributes to more suicides than depression). But it almost always revolves around people who are worried and stressed out. And right now we have a lot to worry about. Journalists have written about it as a second pandemic, a pandemic of loneliness and stress. They aren’t wrong. We can’t connect. We’re more short-tempered and not trusting of others. We humans are social creatures, but we’ve become more isolated from our friends and family. We’re misfiring on our cylinders, and that’s leading to a lot of stress. At the root of this is a lack of communication and understanding.

In the beginning of the Internet, computer scientists had a similar problem. Computers can’t assume that they are speaking the same language, or they won’t connect. The computer’s solution to this is to negotiate, in the beginning of every conversation, to ensure that both computers are speaking the same digital language. This goes something like: “1 means 1, and 0 means 0, right buddy?” On the internet, they call this TCP/IP protocol, and for those of us who are old enough, we remember the sounds of our modems, working this out with other computers. We would hear:

Computer 1: “Ccchhhhkkk sphhh chuuuuck spppphhhh chuk chuk beep beep beeeeeeep.”

Computer 2: “Ccchhhhkkk sphhh chuuuuck spppphhhh chuk sphleewwwww beep beep beeeeeeep”.

Computer 1: [ Thank god. ]

Computer 2: [ I know, right? ]

This was the sound of computers establishing their connection. In humans, there are similar prerequisites to forming connections. If we try communicating without them, we usually fail. The emotional WiFi goes down. We’re pissed off and hurt and can’t figure out how to get it back up and running. We’re maybe also resentful that we have to work so hard to reestablish the connection again. So we call a therapist, just like we have to call Verizon to get online again. It’s a connection we take for granted—until it’s not there anymore. No one taught us the building blocks of connection, or the emotional code that produces human WiFi for feelings. We think of it as a mysterious vibe that seems to arise from the ether. But we’ve gotten worse at it because we’ve become so detached from one another, physically and emotionally, over the last ten years.

Connection should be anything but mysterious. There are tangible, learnable skills to get connections going between humans, just like there are to get WiFi going between computers. In this book, we’ll learn them.

These methods are for everybody. You don’t have to be in the midst of a life-threatening crisis. You can do this to prevent a crisis from occurring. You can use it to help a loved one in need. If you’re having a serious problem, these lessons are potentially very helpful (but you should still find someone to talk to, especially with those waitlists). The ability to look at things in a way that is just a little bit different than you’re used to, or to get your loved one to look at things a little bit different—this breaks the chain of worrying and opens the door of possibility. You can get what you want out of your life. This is what I’ve spent years learning how to do, and it’s going to be a much quicker journey for you. Because I already made the mistakes, but that’s for later in the book.

But now back to a sidewalk in Brooklyn, New York, on a late fall morning, and a walk to an ice cream shop:

“That wasn’t very therapeutic,” Maggie said matter-of-factly as we stepped out of my office.

“I mean, you’re not wrong. It would be hard to argue that anything in that session was therapeutic except maybe this part. Ice cream doesn’t have to be therapy. Ice cream just has to be good. I was trying to ignore you because, you’re right, I didn’t want to reinforce your behavior, when your behavior was to kill yourself again, or at least try.”

“You know that never works, Dr. Muir.”

Maggie made a really good point. When she was as upset as she was in my office, and didn’t feel connected, nothing I said had… relevance. Like, I may have had good ideas, but it didn’t matter; she didn’t want to hear them. It wasn’t going to change anything. This is true for so many of us so much of the time. How often as a parent or a spouse do we realize that no matter what we say, we know we’re not going to break through to our loved one?

We know the right answer. We’re in the right. The other person’s point of view can be completely and obviously wrong. But we can’t convince them. And sometimes we try to push and control, but it never works. At this point, people usually go searching in the self-help section, and maybe they find a very useful book like Atomic Habits or The Subtle Art of Not Giving a F*** . And they’ll try not giving a fuck over the things they can’t control. Because zero fucks is sometimes the right answer. But it’s not always the right answer. And when it’s not the right answer, it can go catastrophically wrong like what happened in my office with Maggie.

Not giving a single fuck, no fucks at all, that’s a useful strategy only some of the time. The real win in life is knowing how many fucks to give . You need to give the right amount of fucks. When it’s zero, the subtle art of not giving a fuck comes in quite handy. But so does giving enough fucks, some, not too much, not too little.

It’s like Goldilocks, except in this case it’s Goldif*cks , that subtle zone of caring just enough, giving just enough of a fuck, to really be interested in the outcome, but not trying to push things too far, and not giving up on caring entirely. It’s in this golden zone that we do our most remarkable work. This is the work known as mentalizing.

Caring the right amount is where we win. Because that’s where we stay engaged and figure out what’s really going on in our own minds or the minds of others, this mind-radar is different from the Jedi ability to simply suggest and have it be the case. When Obi-Wan Kenobi does his little hand wave, and then the stormtrooper lets him go because these aren’t the droids they’re looking for, it’s kind of a cheap trick. He basically could’ve gotten the same result by punching them in the face hard enough and knocking them out for a hot minute.

Obi-Wan Kenobi would have been a shitty therapist. He’d wave his hand and tell you what to think, and you’d believe it in the moment. Maybe it would last forever. But the cooler version of that trick is helping someone do their own mind tricks, come to their own realizations, and not have to keep doing it all the time. Give that stormtrooper a sense of his own agency so that instead of asking if these are the droids he’s looking for, he’s asking himself if this is the identity he wants to have, as one of a million clone soldiers, toiling away on a desert planet.

That’s what the mentalizing does. Obi-Wan Kenobi offered certainty. I just offer the possibility, to myself and to others, that we’re not quite sure, and in that uncertainty comes curiosity for what you can be more certain about. And this pursuit frees you up from all sorts of awfulness.

When you’re done with this book, you won’t be so worried. (I’m required to remind you that this is not medical advice—and then further, remind myself—the really useful things I say aren’t remotely medical or, for that matter, advice.) The hand-wringing that gets done about the mental health crisis is not unlike a weatherman dutifully standing in the middle of a hurricane. He’s yelling about how it’s raining. He’s not wrong…but he’s not helping. We get it. He’s wet, and so are we. Has anyone seen my umbrella?

This book is an umbrella. It’s not here to get rid of the rain; it’s here to help keep you dry whenever things come down unexpectedly on you or someone you love. If that sounds good to you, then you’re reading the right book. If it sounds bad to you, then I’m sorry, but you spent $30 on the wrong book. But you’re probably not gonna return it. Because people don’t do a very good job of that, but that’s a different problem.

Target Audience

If A. A. Milne worked as a child psychiatrist who wanted to get the good word out on how all the lost Christopher Robins were delivered from despair, he would have written this book.

We foresee two large audiences for Don’t Panic. The first is people suffering or worrying about their family members. For this cohort, who have been begging Dr. Muir to write this book for years, he brings the basic skill set he teaches his Mayo Clinic Trainees. The audience in this first group is huge. It includes about 20% of the US population at any given time. This book has the potential to attain a global audience in subsequent translations, serving this same target audience worldwide.

The second cohort is the already enthusiastic purchasers of self-help books (18.6M copies sold annually according to the NPD Group). People want to be effective communicators. Everybody wants to learn some Jedi mind-trick to convince people (including themselves) to do what they want. Many people suffer from a crisis of self-direction—they feel lost. This book is for people who have been looking for a map. But what it does, instead, is teach them how to chart their own course.

I have worked with patients in terrible need, but I learned much from them, and so will the readers. But as with everything in my practice, I don’t want to sugarcoat the real problems that exist out there. My candor has been one of the things my patients and students appreciate about me.

Suicide is a leading cause of death among young people in America. People have to deal with someone else being in despair often, even if it’s not them. And even if they don’t, they’ll appreciate honest guidance about what to say when their own life gets heavy. It’s also likely the most humorous book intended to help deep despair you are likely to have land on your desk, with, I imagine, limited direct competitors.

Introduction

Who’s Crazy?

A few years ago, I had a 14-year-old patient named Jeremiah who had a bad habit of swallowing sharp objects. Repeatedly. The young man had single-handedly trained an entire generation of local gastroenterology fellows. Every other day, a sharp object – and God knows where these came from – would go down Jeremiah’s throat. And every other day, Jeremiah would be sent to the operating room under sedation while doctors tried to retrieve the sharp object before it punctured his intestines or stomach.

I tried to talk to Jeremiah directly, but he ignored me or pretended I wasn’t there. The only person he’d talk to was a member of the recreation staff who took the kids out for basketball. This staff member was pretty good with kids and would tell me things about Jeremiah, but he and I knew this was no way to treat a patient. So I tried a different approach. I had recently recorded a podcast with a colleague discussing her struggle with depression. Instead of trying to get Jeremiah to talk, I simply played the podcast on my phone, and then afterwards, I had a one-sided conversation out loud.

“Lara didn’t tell anybody about her depression,” I said aloud. “She was suicidal for years. She was really struggling. When she finally told me about it – she hadn’t really told anyone before because she had been afraid of what doctors would think. We’re both doctors, so maybe that’s why she finally told me, I don’t know.” I paused to look over at him. I could tell he was listening even though he wasn’t looking at me.

“I wonder if this is the situation you’re in, Jeremiah? I wonder if you’re worried about telling me what’s going on that’s really getting to you. I worry about you swallowing that stuff. I don’t know why it’s happening. It’s really hard for me to not know.”

Jeremiah gave me a little smile, then spoke to me for the first time: “Thanks. I’ll see you tomorrow.”

Bingo! I was thrilled to have broken through that first wall. But then the next day, when we were supposed to meet, he was nowhere to be found. That’s because he was in surgery again for swallowing another sharp object. Two days later, I did see him after I was called to the unit to help. When I arrived, Jeremiah was on the ground screaming. His limbs were flailing, and he was clawing at the walls. A staff member was trying to hold his hands from pulling something, anything, off the walls.

“Jeremiah,” I said as I walked up to him. “I don’t get it, what’s wrong?!”

“They’re trying to stop me.”

“They have to stop you.”

“Swallowing things makes me feel better!!”

“Wait, so we’re trying to stop the only thing that makes you feel better?”

“Yes!!”

“Wow, that’s totally messed up. No wonder you hate this hospital. Here we are, forcing to stop you from doing the only thing that helps you feel better.”

“Yes! Yes!”

“This is an awful problem to have. We can’t let you swallow things. But we’re getting in the way of something that makes you feel better. No wonder you hate it here.”

He calmed down a little bit. And then a little bit more.

The next day, we had a full conversation over a meal – no sharp objects at the dining table. As my understanding of him grew, he began to feel more understood and learned to relax more. The conversation shifted from what he was doing to how he was feeling. And those feelings now had words instead of self-harming actions. Sure, he’d had six different diagnoses from ICD-10 (the list of international classification of diseases), but the one diagnosis that mattered to him was that I was trying to understand him. That trust made all the difference on the path towards getting well.

Sometimes the acknowledgment of what’s happening, right in the moment, is the most surprising thing you can say. It can really get people out of their own heads in a way that you can’t when you’re just trying to stop them from doing something crazy. People often do outrageous things because they don’t feel understood. When they feel understood, they stop. When you try to understand something that seems nonsensical to you, you create a shared connection that is desperately needed. You mentalize – to imagine their mind, however imperfectly, and then check if you got it right – and they feel like you put in the effort to understand them.

Not long ago, I was at Zazzy’s, a pizza joint in the West Village, with two of my coworkers. We had just sat down to enjoy our slices when a man walked in and shouted at the top of his voice:

“I NEED 20 DOLLARS! I’M SUICIDAL! SOMEONE GIVE ME SOME MONEY NOW!”

No one responded. Some pretended to ignore him. The guy behind the counter looked really uncomfortable. You could hear chairs shift. Shift. Shuffle. Squeak.

“I AM NOT KIDDING!” the man shouted out again.

In fairness, I don’t believe anyone thought the unhoused gentleman, whose body odor I could smell from where I was sitting, was kidding. It wasn’t funny, that was for sure. Nothing about it was funny. He had a wild look in his eyes.

“SOMEBODY HELP ME! IS ANYONE GOING TO HELP ME?”

He didn’t know it at the time, but he was asking a really good question. The answer should be, “Yes!” But so often, it’s not. Everyone wants to be a superhero, but mostly to petite victims or cute animals. It’s harder to be a superhero when the victim is a stranger who smells and could possibly harm you – or when you don’t think you have time in your day to stop for a second to help another human in need.

I stood up and turned to him. “Hey, come with me. I got you. Let’s talk outside?” I wanted him to pose less of a threat to the customers and possibly to himself. And I wanted him to be curious about what would happen outside, and to know that I was genuinely trying to understand his situation.

My two colleagues watched through the glass as the following scene unfolded: The stranger and I resumed talking outside. Watching from inside the restaurant, you could see the man transition from what was rage, fear, or hurt into what looked like a whole different guy. His body visibly relaxed. If you were looking closely, you’d see that I handed him a $20 bill. He walked away from the restaurant, silently, with a spring in his step.

“That was incredible!” my colleague Lewis said after I returned to the table. “What happened?” Lewis and I had been working together for about six months, but he’d never seen me interact with someone in crisis.

This man, whom I’ll call Dennis, recognized me, and I recognized him. He even remembered my name. I made sure not to lead him, so I asked simply what he wanted to do. To my surprise, he said he wanted to start clozapine again. I agreed. It had really helped him once already. So that’s where he went. I offered to give him a ride, but he said he knew the way to get there, and just like that, he was gone. He didn’t even ask for the $20 I handed him.

Most of us spend our lives trying to pretend we know what’s going on. This is a mistake—not the pretend part, but the idea that the goal in life is to know . That sense of obligation is a heavy burden, which is why we don’t get up to confront the stranger in the pizza shop. We are all exhausted Atlases, shrugging. We feel we don’t have the answers to his problem. But the truth is, no one has the answers. We’re all winging it—and in our most important moments, no less.

Our society has made a deal with the devil: abandon or avoid people like Dennis who are suffering from mental health issues, so as not to have to think about it ourselves. We have created a cult of abdication of duty. The helpers are not to be helped. Helping really sick people places a huge burden on psychiatrists, and we don’t want to assist them with this burden. In this way, we are able to decry extreme conditions like suicidality and stand against death and sorrow, without the risk of contaminating ourselves by standing too close to it.

I deal with the patients who fit on the most extreme end of suicidality. These aren’t all of my clients, but they’re most of them. One of my patients is a young woman, Gwen, whose OCD was so severe that she got denied help by nearly 100 other doctors (severe OCD cases have a much higher rate of suicide than any other diagnosed mental illness, including depression). Gwen has been a regular patient of mine for the last five years. In her case, what helped was transcranial magnetic stimulation. And it was a very specific protocol, which I developed. It’s a modification of an approach pioneered at Stanford. Essentially, we flip the right switch in the brain to turn OCD off. We give more brain stimulation more frequently than is traditionally done, and this is life-saving.

In addition to this protocol, we use mentalizing. It’s more powerful, when it comes to reducing suicide attempts, than SSRIs are for depression. It’s more powerful than lithium for bipolar disorder. It’s more powerful than stimulants for ADHD. It’s more powerful than ECT (electroconvulsive therapy) for treatment-resistant depression. It’s an extremely potent intervention. It’s been replicated in randomized controlled trials and has been proven repeatedly to be as potent as I say it is. When you use mentalizing as a therapy in a therapeutic context, it’s pretty good stuff. Not only does it actually help patients, but it also helps psychiatrists. It helped me keep my mind together when I was faced with Gwen one time, wanting to jump off a bridge —a story I’ll share later in this book. It told me to admit, ironically, that I didn’t have any answers. Which, at the end of the day, provided the answer that my patient needed. That I was there, uncertain, taking a mentalizing stance, but not taking any abrupt action.

This is not to say that I think you, the reader, belong on this same end of the spectrum as Gwen or Jeremiah. Far from it, I hope you are not suffering the way my patients suffer, but I have a guess that if you are reading this, you or someone you care about is dealing with some mental health struggles, and they are no less important to you or your loved one.

That’s why I don’t shy away from telling stories about these extreme cases because I think they have something to teach all of us. By understanding these extreme scenarios, you’ll come to understand that a) you’re not alone and b) your situation may improve. It’ll also open up your compassion and empathy meters, which, as we’ll see later in this book, is one of the essential ingredients towards mentalizing.

From Chapter One: The Mentalizing Revolution

Here’s a quick and easy way to understand mentalizing. Fill an empty crayon box with birthday candles, and then ask a 3-year-old what’s inside. The child will assume crayons until you show them the candles. If you then ask the child what their doll thought was in the box, they’ll say the doll thought it was candles all along. That’s because for the 3-year-old, the perception of reality is no different from the actual reality of what everyone is thinking. Because the child knows there are candles, the doll must have that same knowledge.

But give them another year and their understanding of the mind evolves. A four- or five-year-old will to tell you their doll thought there were crayons inside the box, too. They recognize that one person may see and experience things very differently than another person.

This study is called the false belief test, and it’s the first step towards understanding the process of mentalizing. To sum it up, mentalizing is the capacity to reflect on one’s own thoughts and feelings and those of others to predict and understand behavior. Mentalizing is a form of imaginative mental activity, namely perceiving and interpreting human behavior in terms of intentional mental states (needs, desires, feelings, beliefs, goals).

Trust, however, is the foundation for mentalizing, the currency that actually closes the deal. Money is great, but if you don't trust you'll get it, it doesn't conclude transactions, emotional or business. If you don’t trust information, it’s not safe learn it. It doesn’t make sense to learn new things from goddamn liars. We have a brain system for that. Our first pass in our brain is a giant filter of all of our experiences that asks the question: Should I trust the source of this information?

If the answer is no, we have a series of strategies in our cognitive operating system to avoid, dodge, forget, fight back against, and generally make sure none of that information changes the way we act in the future. This is colloquially termed “bullshit detection.” Teenagers refer to this sort of information as "boring” in conflating different ideas. When you ask teens what they learned at school, and you get the answer "I don't know, whatever” – you are bearing witness to a remarkable system that allows humans to sift through unbelievable amounts of information and only remember the most relevant.

In the chapters of this book, I’ll give you the recipe for the secret sauce that builds trust. If I do a really good job, you’ll learn how to build trust with yourself and others. The surprising part about this recipe? It doesn’t involve knowing anything specific. You need no information other than this: not knowing the answer leads to all the actual answers.

You don’t stop at not knowing. But you always start there. Here are some quick examples to drive the point home:

Would you like someone to calm down?

Won’t work: “You need to calm down!”

Might work: “It seems like you’re really upset?”

Trying to convince someone of your conspiracy theory?

Won’t work: 9/11 was an inside job!

Might work: “On a scale of the number 9 through the number 11, can you give me a numerical rating for how confident you are that 9/11 was not an inside job?”

“Um. 10.9”

“Oh, that’s interesting. What made it 10.9 and not 11?

“Well, there’s this one little doubt I have…”

These techniques–mentalizing by both not assuming I know, and being curious about what the other person thinks– work in all kinds of situations, not just the clinic in which I work as a psychiatrist. If this sounds dangerous to you, like it could be misused, you are absolutely right. People misuse mentalizing all the time. People build trust in order to steal or kill. Or you can make your mom feel bad if she didn’t let you play more video games. Or you can guilt your children into going to bed.

Artificial intelligence designers (and now we) fret endlessly about building ethical AI, and well they should. But for humans, unethical human intelligence is wildly common, because of course it is. The special fail-safe built into mentalizing is that when we are actually connected to someone else—not just faking caring but really do — it’s almost impossible to hurt somebody else. If you’re faking caring, you can do something awful to someone. But actual mentalizing is a shared mental state. These mirror neurons—actually circuits, in my understanding—are firing in both people. You understand each other. And in a state of knowledge, it’s just not one of the options to punch someone else in the face.

The metaphor of H2O, which has three phases (solid ice, liquid water, and gaseous steam) helps anchor our understanding of mentalizing as the process by which we navigate hot and cold emotions. Mentalizing is a lot like swimming: you can only do it in liquid water in the right emotional temperature range. It's not something you can do when it's too hot or too cold.

The graphics of ice, water, and steam will follow us throughout the book. We use them to visually annotate conversations to indicate “mentalizing” or “not mentalizing” emotional states in the conversations we are using to illustrate the phenomenon.

When non-mentalizing occurs, people get stuck on either themselves or somebody else. It’s the ability to move back and forth between these polarities smoothly, like liquid water, that is a feature of good mentalizing.

People love talking about themselves. But when they get too fixated on their own perspectives, it's really not very interesting to other people. On the other hand, when you're having a conversation with somebody, if they're able to change perspective, it's a really good sign that they are mentalizing. Using our mentalizing skills, we are able to notice if someone is stuck on their perspective. Once we can tell that they are stuck, we can use some other mentalizing skills to get them unstuck. People can get stuck either on our perspective or someone else’s, thinking about either past the point of usefulness. From Chapter Six: Resolve Conflicts Like a Pro: The Mentalizing Secret

Validation is how we let another person know that their perspective is understandable. Feeling understood reliably calms down the situation. In this section, we discuss how to make someone feel like they’re understood. This is a technique to use when people’s feelings are too hot.

Validation cools down the emotional temperature. So, it doesn’t make sense to use it when people are too cold and disconnected. But when they’re too hot, this is solid gold.

For example, let’s say someone you care about is hurt.

Won’t work: “You’re really hurt right now.”

Might work: “If I were you, I’d be really hurting right now. What is it like for you?”

Or, let’s say you really pissed someone off.

Won’t work: “You hate me.”

Might work: “It seems like you really hate me?”

The important caveat that goes with validation is that you’re not done after just one iteration. You want to keep relentlessly validating until someone has cooled down enough to be curious again: Goldif*cks temperature, just right.

It’s a little bit like drilling for oil. You can’t drill 5 feet down, get out no oil, throw up your hands and yell “Oh, I guess there’s not any oil!! Our geology team needs to be fired!”

You need to go deep like an oil well.

While not everyone is as tough to understand as Darth Vader, this chapter will take you through the many ways to use validation, so that you can use it effectively with anyone from your most curious friend to the Lord of The Sith.

Explicit Confusion and Clarification

Explicit Confusion is saying “I don’t know what to say” when you don’t know what to say. Instead of just being confused, readers will learn to express their confusion. Clarification is checking to make sure you’re getting it.

This can take the form of something as simple as saying the same thing they just said back with a little bit of a question mark at the end. One of the things I’ve taken to doing is actually saying the words “... question mark?” out loud so that people don’t misunderstand me.

For instance, let’s say someone had a bad day at work.

Person X: It was the worst job I’ve ever had.

Dr. O: It was the worst job you’ve ever had, question mark?

Person X: Did you just say question mark?

Distract and Return and Rewind

Strategies to distract someone when nothing else is working.

This section describes two approaches that work best together. The first is more of a permission slip than a technique. All of us have been in conversations where we feel like we’re getting nowhere. I’ve probably been in more of these than most people.

One of the important things to understand about mentalizing is that it’s not always adaptive for people to mentalize. Times when mentalizing is inappropriate include: when facing a tiger. Never spend time wondering if the tiger is hungry. Run. The risk of being eaten is never worth it. The one time that you’re right, the tiger’s mental state is outweighed by all the other times in which you are eaten. Always run. This happens in everyday situations like attempted muggings. The only point is to get out alive. When you’re in danger or facing things about which you can’t productively be curious, don’t mentalize. Everyone’s tiger is different, but nobody should get eaten alive. When other people’s feelings are out of control, sometimes you’re not going to get anywhere by talking to them.

I was walking with my children the other day to school. They really wanted to listen to a Bluetooth speaker, and they also both wanted to hold the Bluetooth speaker. Trent is six. Quinn is also six. They are twins. They have not yet learned the “it’s not worth fighting about” life lesson. I tried being a good parent:

“As long as the two of you are fighting over who holds the speaker, I’m not gonna play any music through it.”

Trent responded robustly, “But I want to hold it, and Quinn can hold it tomorrow.”

Quinn has a really whiny voice sometimes when she wants to get something. “But I want to hold it. I want to hold it now. I want to listen to Roar by Katy Perry.” she whined, annoyingly.

As an aside, I understand that you’re judging me for my daughter’s musical choices. And you’d be right to do so. This is a terrible song. For adults. But it’s catchy. It’s got a certain sing-song quality that made it popular, and for six year olds, this is just candy for your brain. My son, for reference, is much more of a fan of REM, including songs like Orange Crush, and They Might Be Giants tunes like Dr. Worm. I consider myself a successful parent when my children wake me up at 6 AM by blasting They Might be Giants or REM. I’ll even take Roar by Katy Perry.

Now, back to the main part of this chapter's discussion, but you’ll notice that right there, I just distracted you, as readers, pretty successfully. You probably forgot all about the fact that this is a chapter about distraction, and I did that by distracting you, by giving you something else to think about. Kids are cute. They’re reliably distracting.

At the time of these events with my children, I wasn’t being successful and distracting them, no amount of music was playing, and they were just hammering each other back and forth. Trent: “You’re just making it worse.” Quinn: “But I want it.” Repeat.

So I had a choice to make. I could either keep up my good-parent bonafides, and have my children scream at each other the whole way to school, and then have the school think I’m a terrible parent who brings crying children in the door. Or, I could bail on the whole situation in a way that wouldn’t totally undercut my authority as a parent.

So my way of distracting and returning later was to choose to play some music that had nothing to do with any of the things they wanted, but nonetheless was music they could listen to instead of fighting.

Lo and behold, it was time for Miles Davis, Kind of Blue and the song So What to the rescue!

And it worked. They calmed down. We walked to school. We would revisit the issue of who got to hold the Bluetooth speaker on Monday like we do every morning.

After I use the technique of Distraction successfully, I add another it another just to make sure they arrive at class calm. The next technique I use is called The Rewind. The Rewind is a really reliable technique. When you’re good at recognizing mentalizing, it’s an amazing skill. When you’re new to mentalizing, it’s still a pretty good skill. The idea is that if a person has gotten either too hot or too cold in the process of the conversation, thus far, you try to bring them back, verbally, to the last time they were known to be mentalizing whatever story they are telling you.

So, in the above example with my kiddos, I might say:

“Hey Trent, hold on. Before we left the house you told me the speaker was charged? How do you know? Can you describe how you checked?”

Trent: “It was 98 percent charged. I always check the percentage charge. That is how I know. Your Tesla has 93%. It’s important to remember that.”

Dr O: But what happened next…?

Mentalizing skills pop back online when minds are directed to think about when they were mentalizing in the past. The opposite is also true. Things that are unmentalizable—trauma or abuse, for example—are not the subject of much therapeutic work with this specific approach when I’m doing therapy. If you were to rewind the patient back to a time of abuse or trauma, you’d essentially be rewinding into a fire pit. That is why other techniques are needed to work on trauma (like EMDR, MdMA therapy and the like). I don’t recommend trying to have these loaded conversations about intense topics like trauma for the general public. When you Rewind, Rewind to when people were curious! It will save you from some very difficult conversations that go nowhere.

What we talk about matters. Don’t bother with things about which you or someone else can’t be curious, if you have the choice. And if we are trying to restore mentalizing skills, either a time in the past when you were curious and mentalizing, or the present moment, as it’s occurring, are the safest time frames for good results.

Calling Out the Elephant in the Room

This consists of saying the shared unspoken truth that, once mentioned, unlocks a more honest conversation.

My children are rather clever. We recently had lunch with them and were discussing Christmas presents. My wife responded to one request for my daughter as such:

“Well, why don’t you tell us so we can tell Santa, what you want for Christmas. Some presents are from Mom and Dad, and some are from Grandma and Grandpa, but we let Santa know about some of the things you want, and so Santa might bring some of the gifts separately. We usually open those special gifts from Santa on Christmas morning.

Trent, my son, pipes up. “It’s not possible for Santa to deliver all of those gifts in one night. Santa isn’t real. Those gifts are from you.”

Carlene turns to me with a look of amusement, and then turns back to the children: “How long have you known?”

Quinn, our daughter, pipes up: “About two years. It didn’t really make sense, all the flying back and forth. But some other kids at school were talking about it also.”

Dr O: “So we’ve been pretending Santa exists for two years and you knew the whole time?”

We spend a lot of time having conversations with people where we both are pretending that the elephant in the room that’s right there isn’t right there.

My own children slow-rolled me for two years. They kept collecting presents and kept smiling pleasantly, but they knew there was no Santa, but neither of us admitted the truth we knew to each other. We could’ve spent thousands more dollars more on presents from Santa and had a lot more fake conversation if my children had not picked up that Calling Out the Elephant in the Room would focus me on the presents they really wanted. As in this conversation, you can often get more of what you really want if you move past the unspoken bullsh*t and get to the real issues.

From Appendix: Games People Play

At the end of the book, I will share a series of simple games that you can use at home and with your family to restore mentalizing without having to explain the concept itself.

All of the following games and activities share one really simple, conceptual piece. The thing that ties them together is that you’re comparing what you imagine is contained within someone else’s mind with what’s actually in there. And then, vice versa.

In each and every one of these games, your family is getting practice breaking away from the traditional model of comparing experiences. Some questions are designed to fill space, not actually facilitate understanding. They’re about avoidance of awkwardness, not about helping anyone feel any more understood. These are questions like:

• That breakfast was dope, right?

• How was it for you babe?

• This has been the best Thanksgiving ever, don’t you think?

• Aren’t you glad you used Dial?

Other questions reveal a genuine interest in the other person’s experience, and display your attempts at empathizing or understanding: These are questions like:

• I imagine you liked the oatmeal the best, am I getting that right?

• I think in the second house we saw, you really probably love the high ceilings, and I’m guessing that makes it a major selling point, and you probably think that the arcade room was really up my alley… How did I do?

• That sex was amazing and connected. For me, anyway, what was going on for you?

See the difference? Each time, instead of just asking someone what they think, you’re taking a guess. This guess, you then ask them to correct, if and when you get it wrong. Getting it wrong is the point. You’ve gotta get it wrong sometimes, so you can learn to error-correct. Cult-leaders and demagogues leave this step out. They say things like:

• It seems like nobody understood you.

• And that’s why you’re afraid of failure.

• There are times when you don’t know the answer.

• Those times are terrifying.

• Luckily, our Vice-Presidential Success Program has The Answers you’re looking for.

Curiosity is a super skill. You can build some trust by guessing, but accepting that you can’t know for sure is the basis of all other super-skills. The difference between mind-reading and mentalizing is that mentalizing assumes that the process is error-prone–that’s the point. When you get it wrong and let someone correct you, they feel heard, because they were. There’s proof that you heard them, because you acted non-defensively.

Don’t lie to your kids. They’ll learn to see through it. Being a family demagogue only lasts for so long.

Essentially, what you want to do is account for what takes up all the space in their brain. And then they draw a brain for you, with sections they imagine represent the contents of your mind. And then you compare. Hilarity (and understanding) ensues.

Game 2: Emotional Remote Control

This is a game. For this, we’re going to use just a couple (a.k.a. two people). In the game, you’re each asked to draw a remote control, and then another one.

The first remote control has all the controls you wish you had over your partner. The second one is what you imagine they will draw for you. Then you compare. Hilarity (and understanding) ensues.

My wife, Dr. Carlene MacMillian, and I played this game the night before we got engaged. Here’s how it went:

Dr. O: So, I’m guessing you’ve got a knob that lets you turn down my volume so I shut up when I’m going on and on?

Dr. C: Nope, I just ignore you. That one’s not on there.

Dr. O: Can I see?

Dr. C: Here it is.

Dr. O: Why is there just one button?

Dr. C: Because it’s the only control I need over you.

Dr. O: What does that one button do?

Dr. C: The button is called “Like Cats.”

Dr. O: We’re not getting a cat.

Dr. C: That’s why it’s the one button.

It’s nine years later. We’re still married, and we still don’t have a cat.

Game 3: Theme Songs

This game, which might as well be sponsored by Spotify or another streaming music service, is one where we get to exercise our mentalizing skills by choosing theme songs for the other people in our families. This works just as well with friends, coworkers, or other groups of people with close relationships. Superheroes have theme songs when they enter, and by treating the rest of your family members as superheroes, who each get their own theme song, you get to play a fun game that inspires mentalizing. It also creates funny mistakes by juxtaposing what you imagined versus what people actually selected.

We will use the members of my family to illustrate the exercise, so we don’t get lost:

Carlene = Mom

Owen = Dad

Quinn = Daughter

Trent = Son

The structure of the game is as follows: Each family member gets to choose a theme song for the other family members, and then they get to guess at the theme song selected for them. We use a pairs approach, like this:

Carlene selects:

• A theme song for Trent.

• The theme song she imagines Trent will select for her.

Trent selects:

• A theme song for Carlene.

• The theme song he imagines Carlene will select for him.

Quinn Selects:

• A theme song for Owen.

• The theme song she imagines Owen will select for her.

Owen selects:

• A theme Song for Quinn.

• The theme song he imagines Quinn will select for him.

•

We take turns playing the list of theme songs, and everyone gets to guess which theme song is for and who selected it. Then, we get to compare what the theme song was and why it was picked with what the person imagined.

Game 4: Thinking Together Versus Thinking Apart

Have you ever been to a meeting where people asked rhetorical questions? But of course you have. And often, those rhetorical questions take the format of very long-drawn-out presentations, where you find yourself thinking, furiously, “Are they ever going to get to the point?”

Oftentimes, someone who’s trained in the mentalizing skills we’re talking about will recognize a hallmark of pointlessly long meeting presentations: they are usually given by people who aren’t mentalizing. They’re not curious about the listeners. They’re presenting to manage their own anxiety. They’re worried someone might think they’re not doing a good job, or something like that. Those meetings are pretty terrible.

When humans on teams meet, particularly in person, it’s usually to solve a problem. It’s usually to solve a problem that they couldn’t solve on their own. We have a format for teams that use mentalizing, and that format is called thinking together. It’s called that in order to highlight the opposite, which we call thinking apart.

Game 5: Phone a Friend

In this section, we will describe how you can use the activity of calling someone else if you are stuck on an issue with someone in order to get back into a mentalizing zone. (Astute readers will notice that Phone a Friend was previewed in Chapter 3.)

This technique allows the person you’re having trouble with to essentially “listen in” as you share the dilemma with another person. This makes your thinking transparent. This is especially useful when dealing with people who don’t want to engage with you, but whom you have to help get “unstuck” anyway.
