Sitting there, in a box, haunting me. It's a large box. You're probably thinking of a cardboard box, aren’t you? Admit it. You were thinking of a cardboard box. I could've disambiguated that for you, but it would've ruined the disclosure that this was a box made of wood. It was the size of a small car.

At this point, you're probably grumbling internally, “That's a crate.” You would be right. But I wanted you to be disappointed, and a little frustrated, as I was annoyed and frustrated and disappointed and other negatively valenced emotions too.

It was boxed in, but it was a crate, I'll grant you that. This crate had been boxing me in, even when it wasn't physically in my home; it was just in my mind.

I know you're asking yourself another question, because I've been a writer for a while, and it behooves me to know what people are thinking. You're asking the same thing Brad Pitt was thinking in the movie Seven—what's in the box?

Much like Brad Pitt in the movie Seven, even when you find out, it's not going to be satisfying; it'll only be more disappointment. This is in keeping with my plan for your experiences; I want to make them as frustrating as I felt, and, frankly , feel to this day.

As a brief and probably frustrating aside, my favorite short story of all time is also written to be very frustrating by its Author, David Foster Wallace. It's called The Depressed Person, and I can't recommend it more highly. If you went on a date with me, anytime between my late 20s and mid-30s, this is a story that would've been read to you at some point in our relationship. If you thought the short story was hilarious, it was a signal to me that we were gonna get along. And if you didn't get it, I was pretty sure we were done for; it was just a matter of time.

The box lurks on the right-hand side of the photo, mocking us both. © Owen Muir, 2025.

This story was published originally in Harper's, in the year of our Lord 1998, and is available in its entirety on the Internet . You’ll notice only the top eighth of the page is the actual story, and, at the bottom, 7/8 of the page, approximately, only approximately, this is done with my eyeballs, not with any precise measurement, is a footnote.

The Depressed Person by David Foster Wallace. © Owen Muir

There, I screenshotted a picture of the page for you, so you would know what to expect emotionally. However, one of the things I learned in the process of writing this newsletter is that although footnotes could save me from being too discursive in my prose, they couldn't save me from the hassle of trying to figure out how to copy and paste anything I had written into something that could be turned into a book later. It also did away with the utility of having footnotes that were entirely useful, referencing published literature, that I could then use to remind myself when I wanted to know what study I had referenced in one of the over 1000 articles I had written. Also, I'm not that funny, and I'm not David Foster Wallace, and despite being able to write a very long run-on sentence, the footnotes didn't make my writing better, so I stopped. I'm not David Foster Wallace, and my footnotes reference the overwhelmingly more totalitarian articles that I used to craft my more science-based articles.

Today's article is about a box, and you wanna know what's in it, and I'm telling you about short stories by David Foster Wallace, footnotes, and my failure at footnotes.

One of the brilliant things about the story that David Foster Wallace wrote in Harper's is that he uses the footnotes to replicate the experience of the main character—frustrated—and of interacting with the main character—similarly, frustrating—with an identical tool. The inescapable footnotes that encroach upon the body text give you a sense of what it's like to deal with somebody who is inescapable. You don't hate the footnotes, you might even enjoy them, however, even at the conclusion of a particularly satisfying footnote, you might find yourself lost, and searching for the place in the text that led your eyes to glance south, and see the slightly smaller font of the footnote, and now we have to remember where you were in the story.

The character was a very frustrating person; it's called the depressed person, but it may as well have been called the frustrating person, but that would've given away the whole story.

The character doesn't get to the point, or perhaps gets to the point over and over again. This is an individual in psychotherapy, suffering in a way that doesn't relent, and wants to know if they're annoying, and the honest answer is they are. They are clearly, obviously, and endlessly irritating to everyone in their life.

But what David Foster Wallace, the author, does is make you feel frustrated, so you feel a combination of resentment and empathy for the person, which you are then imagining is what everyone in their life thought about them (e.g., the main character).

I don't know if you're gonna empathize with me about my frustration related to the box—you probably want me to get on with it, and tell you what's in the box, and then the story will make more sense to you. And I'm not making any promises that the story is gonna make any more sense to you when you know it's in the box, but I'm gonna tell you what's in it. You know what, maybe I'll show you?

You're looking at a dream deferred. It's still a dream deferred—in that what you're looking at in that picture was taken just a couple of days ago, and no further progress on the assembly or reconstitution or commissioning— I think that italicized word is the actual word, used to describe putting a console into service.

What follows is a photo of the Rupert Neve Designs 5088 recording console, when all of its modules are installed:

It’s not exactly the console I have—as you can see, there are do-dads on the faders that allow a version of “flying faders” automation, albeit under the brand name “SwiftMix,” which I did not opt for on my console. This is a professional console, which, for yours truly, a man who has no more aspirations of a professional life as a full-time audio engineer, is vestigial, aspirational, even retrograde, perhaps?

I used to work as an audio engineer, many moons ago, when I was young, broke, and more physically attractive to an entirely different cohort of human women than the Carlene MacMillan types I now aspire to inspire desire in. I have a dad bod and no job in audio. I make podcasts ! I make songs! I make music and sounds as part of my work. I am, alas, someone who bought this console years back, when I thought it would live along with me at 111 Powers Street, in Brooklyn, NY, and then was delayed in it’s installation by a series of adverse events, including a pandemic, an exit of a business, and a lawsuit —yet to be fully adjudicated, despite the manifest facts and meritorious legal arguments to be brought, with aplomb, to the doorstep of Plainfitts and Counterclaimants 1 . But I digress.

I ordered the mixer when I had a place to put it. Before it could arrive, I had to move, and there was no room. I had to build, and exit from, yet another business in order to move to the burbs and thus, finally, find a place for my beloved console for the mixing of music. It lived in the interim in Vermont, in a warehouse.

When “fully loaded,” it will be over 700lbs, and despite the “short load” planned for my mixer, wherein my other audio equipment can continue to play nicely with the console in question, bringing you the best-sounding podcasts of all time and songs as well. It’s also a thinking space for me—I learned how to calm myself in the environs of a recording studio. I am bereft without one. Frustrated. Left wanting. Wonton. Hungry for the calm that is the quiet and pristine environment of a recording studio. I tried to get a smaller console—I promise. I was thwarted.

The power supply on the Looptrotter died. I was defrauded by Polish national Michael Nocny ; in theory, another power supply or repair was offered, but never provided. I ran out of emotional energy to deal with the mess. Shame on you, Looptrotter , it’s all I can say. I have the text messages and emails to prove it. I don’t have the energy.

If anyone wants to buy a 16-channel looptrotter off of me and wants to deal with the company directly, call me.

I then obtained a console smaller still—only 12 channels, not 16! It, too, had unsolvable technical problems. I even considered buying, on the second-hand market, one of the consoles on which I learned to engineer at Sony Music Studios, Circa 2002, but decided against it, as it likely needed more maintenance than I was up for. This RND 5088 is a compromise, you see, purchased second-handish—paid, in large part, with gear traded in to pay for it.

The inferior 12-channel console.

I waited. I was patient. I was unsettled. I haven’t felt set up and plugged in for years. I still don’t. I’m waiting, impatiently.

I love the feeling in my brain of deciding a tiny detail of a sound, then changing that detail, and having it make everything better. I recorded a full-length album on an even older console designed by Sir Rupert Neve—a man knighted for his efforts in console design—as the very last album recorded at Tiny Telephone Studios in the Mission District of San Francisco. It’s there, I learned, with the help of analog tape and John Vanderslice, to drop my perfectionism in its entirety and live in the moment. One take, first take, only take. I was in heaven. You can hear “The City of Woe”—the resulting album- here .

So I wanted to bottle that calm. I wanted to sit behind a console and think. But they don’t just get plopped down easily. It’s 700lbs! It requires endless cables, a room to be worth the effort, and patch bays. I bit off more than I could chew at the time, and I had to wait.

I have hated every moment of that waiting. I wanted it to exist and to be able to make calming music in my own home, as I did in that magical series of Oasis Studios in my youth. Before I go deaf, and before no one else could care too. I wish to twiddle knobs. I want to seize the moment, which is also a fader, and turn the volume down just the right amount.

These are foolish things to pursue for a man like me. I feel like an absurdist Ahab. And yet, here we are. I’m going to build a studio around it, and I’m likely to be unsettled still. I know it won’t solve any real problem. I’ll still be the same person who wanted something so impractical in the first place. I’ll still strive for the impossible.

I will still be who I am. But, someday soon, with a few dB lower noise floor.

“If you aren’t sure as to what decision to make, always err on the side of the patient”.

—Jeffrey Curtis, MD

I don't love medical-assisted aid-in-dying for one specific reason. Wherever MAiD has been implemented, it’s been used to end the lives of the most vulnerable people, a predictable fallout from the utter failure of psychiatry to do its job.

The state in which I used to live, and in which I still practice medicine, is New York State. It just passed a medically-assisted aid-in-dying law.

Ironically, in cases of the terminally ill from non-psychiatric illness, I’m overwhelmingly in favor of patient autonomy and the physician-patient relationship leading the way. I shudder to think of other people trapped in lives of endless horror and suffering.

In the state of New York, the candidates for NY-MAiD include the following:

17. “TERMINAL ILLNESS OR CONDITION” MEANS AN INCURABLE AND IRREVERS-
IBLE ILLNESS OR CONDITION THAT HAS BEEN MEDICALLY CONFIRMED AND WILL,
WITHIN REASONABLE MEDICAL JUDGMENT, PRODUCE DEATH WITHIN SIX MONTHS.

This leaves the door wide open to chronically suicidal individuals, for whom psychiatric medications haven't helped. Many of those individuals will have personality disorders, for which suicidality is a common symptom. I think we're making a mistake by not excluding primary psychiatric disorders as a rationale for death by a doctor.

I get that this isn’t the intent of that law. This is always intended to be used only in extreme cases; more people are terminally ill, and then it expands to others whose suffering is tremendous, but not terminal in the traditional sense of the term. That's what happens in the real world. Let’s look at what’s happening elsewhere in the world, to peer into the future:

The NYS law is more restrictive (now) than Canada’s criteria, passed in 2016:

The Act permits medical aid in dying for patients who meet all of the following criteria, i.e the patient:

(1) is an insured person within the meaning of the Health Insurance Act;

(2) is of full age and capable of giving consent to care;

(3) is at the end of life;

(4) suffers from an incurable serious illness;

(5) suffers from an advanced state of irreversible decline in capability; and

(6) suffers from constant and unbearable physical or psychological pain which cannot be relieved in a manner the person deems tolerable.

However, I suspect there will be a problem, and I'm going to use some data to back it up. It always starts carefully, restricted, and framed as rare . Then, it ramps up. Let’s look at the numbers from the Netherlands:

As people got used to the new law, the number of Dutch people being euthanised began to rise sharply, from under 2,000 in 2007 to almost 6,600 in 2017 . (Around the same number are estimated to have had their euthanasia request turned down as not conforming with the legal requirements.) Also in 2017, some 1,900 Dutch people killed themselves, while the number of people who died under palliative sedation – in theory, succumbing to their illness while cocooned from physical discomfort, but in practice often dying of dehydration while unconscious – hit an astonishing 32,000 . Altogether, well over a quarter of all deaths in 2017 in the Netherlands were induced.

In Canada, in 2024, it is the 5th leading cause of death:

Medically-assisted dying – also known as voluntary euthanasia – accounted for 4.7% of deaths in Canada in 2023, new government data shows.

The country’s fifth annual report since euthanasia was legalised in 2016 showed around 15,300 people underwent assisted dying last year after being successful in their applications.

It’s disproportionately white people choosing MAiD, at least in Canada:

Around 96% of recipients identified as white people, who account for about 70% of Canada's population. It is unclear what caused this disparity.

And more data that suggest it's not being used in an equitable or medical-condition-driven fashion, and more by cultural variation, comes from the vast differences in use of MAID across different regions of Canada:

Assisted dying continued to have the highest usage rate in Quebec, which accounted for nearly 37% of all euthanasia deaths, despite the province holding just 22% of Canada's population.

The enlightened Dutch have had MAiD longer than most, since 2012.

We have one cohort study, which looked at the outcomes of individuals requesting medical assistance in dying based on psychiatric suffering , only among the young (those under 24).

The study included 397 processed applications submitted by 353 individuals (73.4% female; mean [SD] age, 20.84 [1.90] years).

The time frame was from:

Between 2012 and the first half of 2021, the number of MAID-PS applications by young patients increased from 10 to 39.

It's not a tidal wave. What were the outcomes?

The most likely outcome was application retracted by the patient (188 [47.3%]) followed by application rejected (178 [44.8%]).

The overwhelming majority of these individuals did not end up receiving aid in dying, by a physician, giving them drugs with which to kill themselves.

Let’s dig into their results, from this primary psychiatric sample, in more detail:

For 12 applications (3.0%), patients died by MAID. 1. It Needs to Work.

You will need data that demonstrates two things:

• Your product is better than existing alternatives ( > 1 standard deviation at least)

• Your product is so much better, it’s worth more, and it should demonstrably save money.

• The data you present needs to have independent validation.

• The people to whom you are selling your product have heard all the other pitches. They are skeptics.

The greatest trick the devil ever played? Something that had good branding, I assume. Today, I take another pass at one of my favorite topics—the ubiquity of oral medicines in the treatment of adolescent depression, and their startling lack of efficacy . At the same time, we have effective treatments that work better, safer, and faster.

It is a pill bottle.

To bring readers up to speed, only Prozac (fluoxetine) has evidence (among pills) for antidepressant efficacy in youth.

“ At this point, we’d like to read you the list of antidepressants that did not pass muster in youth depression, but it’s too long. Suffice it to say that it includes the tricyclics and nearly all modern antidepressants – including the newest ones, levomilnacipran, vilazodone, and vortioxetine – just about all except trazodone and bupropion. Those are still uncharted territory. So, outside of fluoxetine, why do we use antidepressants in children?”

KELLIE NEWSOME, On The Carlat Psychiatry Podcast.

And yet, in 2022, the most recent year in which we have US data, we collectively suborned the prescription of 9.44M non-fluoxetine oral antidepressants to youth 12-17. 1

To be very clear, those medicines only added risk, not benefit.

It is back-of-the-envelope math that excludes all the kids 0-11 who were also prescribed those meds. We thus have somewhere over 10,000,000 prescriptions written, with an FDA black box warning for increasing suicidal ideation.

Well, I’d argue we need to do four things for kids and families, now:

• Child Psychiatrists and Pediatricians should become OBSESSED with deprescribing those drugs. It is a reasonable strategy for both safety and risk management. Any family with a bad outcome— there are 700,000 suicide attempts among adolescents in 2024 —can and will start suing and winning. The last prescriber in holding the hot-potato, and who who didn’t document a plan for deprescribing? I wouldn’t want to be an underwriter. If you want more info on deprescribing of antidepressants, check out Outro Health.

• Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation has an FDA label for adolescent depression, and national coverage from UnitedHealthgroup, Cigna, Medical, and more for heaven’s sake. Other plans will follow. It’s safe and effective and should be a first line treatment along with general psychiatric management in suicidal youth.

• Psychotherapy without antidepressants (plus TMS when needed) should be the standard in suicidal youth. That means physicians either LEARNING therapy themselves or working with therapists in teams, or both. I even wrote a therapy manual on the topic myself, to help— get “ Adolescent Suicide and Self-Injury: Mentalizing Theory and Treatment ” here (amazon affiliate link).

• Stop kidding ourselves, and start working—hard, and now—on more effective options such as TMS for adolescents. We have myriad treatments in the pipeline for adults, lets ask regulators to offer label extensions if study sponsors do pediatric trials, or something of similar value. We need to incentive novel therapeutic and payment models for kids.
