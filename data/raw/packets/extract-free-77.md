I’m done with treatment-resistant depression. It’s got other names, now, too, and I’m here to pick bones today:

Treatment-resistant depression .

Hard-to-treat depression.

Difficult-to-treat depression.

Various words in front of the word depression to indicate how hard depression is to treat. I don't love that we're in this mess in the first place.

A little bit of history: sadness is a transient mood state, and it's normal, and a healthy response to difficult circumstances. Sadness is not generally accompanied by a series of other symptoms, and nor does it last for days to weeks to months to years.

In antiquity, we had the term melancholia to describe this human problem. There are people who would be down, taken by dark moods, pensive, even paralyzed. This was blamed on Black Bile, one of the four humors, being out of balance.

Despondent, fearful sorrow, partial insanity, all of these were different names used to differentiate from more severe perversions of the mind like dementia praecox—that's modern day schizophrenia. People with melancholia were unwell, through about the 1700s , but even before our modern DSM and TikTok influencers, we had to debates about what words meant as they related to psychiatric disorders:

At the time of Hippocrates melancholia was regarded mainly in its affective component. Since that time, and until the eighteenth century, authors and opinions have been divided, with both aspects (affective disorders and abnormal beliefs), being valued. Finally, in the eighteenth to nineteenth centuries, with Pinel at its peak, melancholia becomes exclusively a synonym of abnormal beliefs. At the turn of the nineteenth to the twentieth century, the affective component returns as the main aspect characterizing melancholia. 1

There is a tension between the beliefs that were hard to accept and the affect of the people holding those beliefs. There has always been a tension when it comes to severe depression around what the core of the illness is .

Emil Kraepelin was a physician and the key taxonomist for modern psychiatry’s view of the world. In the mid-19 century:

French alienists proposed a 'double' or 'circular' illness consisting of alternating depressed and manic episodes and at the beginning of the twentieth century, Emil Kraepelin introduced the term 'manic-depressive insanity.' Kraepelin's broad clinical experience resulted in compelling descriptions of the symptoms of mood disorders that have arguably never been surpassed. 2

Kraepelin argued that melancholia might be more than one thing, and it depended on what it was paired with over a longer time course. For some individuals, the recurrent depression was in a dance with mania, or had a cyclicity that resembled that of those who are more clearly in the thrall of what we would come to call bipolar disorder. It's possible that some depression was different than other depression.

We didn't get the first edition of the diagnostic and statistical manual of mental disorders, or the DSM , until 1952. This initial text was descriptive, and instead of blaming depression on Black Bile, it landed on “reaction”—depression might be caused by a reaction to psychosocial stressors, biology, or some combination thereof.

Kraepelin’s obsession with taxonomy—dividing up one thing from another as a way to understand things more clearly as separate from one another or fundamentally the same—became the basis of our modern system of diagnosis.

We had a long list of psychoneurotic disorders in DSM-I, that were clearly different from those caused by medical problems, like stroke or infection:

There's depressive reaction right there! There was a lot of ink spent on personality disturbances, and even a whole separate section for transient situational personality disorders:

The subtext of all of this personality content’s includsion in DSM, of course, is that some people have a personality that predisposes them to problems, and sometimes that personality comes into contact with an environment that makes things worse, and other people have an underlying biological problem that is the whole enchilada. Depression was understood as both of personality and reaction to problems , and, separately, a psychotic disorder “without clearly defined, physical, cause or structural changes in the brain.” There was more than one depression!

The text, even today, is a lot more relatable than one might imagine. When describing psychoneurotic disorders, the authors of DSM-I introduce them thusly:

The chief characteristic of these disorders is “anxiety” which may be directly felt and expressed or which may be unconsciously and automatically controlled by the utilization of various psychological defense mechanisms (depression, conversion, displacement, etc.). In contrast to those with psychoses, patients with psychoneurotic disorders do not exhibit gross distortion or falsification of external reality (delusions, hallucinations, illusions) and they do not present gross disorganization of the personality. Longitudinal (lifelong) studies of individuals with such disorders usually present evidence of periodic or constant maladjustment of varying degree from early life. Special stress may bring about acute symptomatic expression of such disorders.

“Anxiety” in psychoneurotic disorders is a danger signal felt and perceived by the conscious portion of the personality.

But the authors are not describing the different problem of manic-depressive psychosis above. They're describing something different . There are different depressions . There have always been different depressions. Sometimes it's a reaction to what happened to you—the author of DSMs Instructed the readers to spend time on how the patient responds to the trauma that causes the depression, not on the fact of the trauma caused it. DSM-II refined these concepts, leaning a little bit harder into neurosis, but didn't fundamentally change the narrative structure of the book.

By the time we got to DSM-III, however, in 1980, there was a major pivot. We were no longer describing various neurosis, we had operationalized a series of criteria for major depressive disorder, which subsumed most of the other various ways to conceptualize depression, other than manic depressive disorder, which remained separate.

I'm not trying to minimize dysthymia , as a conceptual entity, or cyclothymia , or any of the other minor diagnoses in the DSM that we just don't use, in day-to-day practice. We don't use them for really important reasons—insurance won't cover it. There's no drug for it. There's nothing we can do. When there's nothing we can do, we don't use the category.

But if we rewind to Kraepelin, there was nothing he could do about anything. All he could do was taxonomize. He could be compassionate, he could spend time with people suffering, and he could try to parse what was happening.

And then we got insulin shock, electro-convulsive therapy, modern medications. Psychiatry became about what we could do, we focused on disorders for which we had treatments. Our potency was what mattered. If you had depression? We had antidepressants. If you had psychosis? We had antipsychotics. We were very excited to be able to do something.

Unfortunately, as outlined beautifully by Thomas Main in 1957’s essay, The Ailment, when we feel we can't do something useful, things can go south:

…those [patients] who recover only slowly or incompletely are less satisfying. Only the most mature of therapists are able to encounter frustration of their hopes without some ambivalence towards the patient, and in patients who do not get better, or who even get worse in spite of long devoted care, major strain may arise.

The patient’s attendants are then pleased neither with him nor themselves and the quality of their concern for him alters accordingly, with consequences that can be severe both for patients and attendants.

We know that doctors and nurses undertake the work of alleviating suffering because of deep personal reasons, and that the practice of medicine, like every human activity, has abiding, unconscious determinants. We also know that if human needs are not satisfied, they tend to become more passionate, to be reinforced by aggression and then to deteriorate in maturity, with sadism invading the situation, together with its concomitants of anxiety, guilt, depression and compulsive reparative wishes, until ultimate despair can ensue.

That ambivalence towards the patient is the essence of “treatment-resistant depression.” Instead of spending time on taxonomic questions—what kind of illness is the patient suffering from, and what's the best way to treat it?—we became obsessed with taking a diagnostic entity that is poorly differentiated and trying to understand it based on response to treatment. We could've asked the question “is this depression at all?” or “what kind of depression are we dealing with?” Instead, we focus on non-response to a class of medications that aren't particularly good.

When I say not particularly good, I don't mean to be insulting. The difference between placebo and active agent is not large. That has a lot to do with the underlying disorder—depression response to placebo, especially early in the course, robustly. Treatment resistant depression responds less robustly to placebo, and the more failed trials, the less placebo response we see. This understanding is useful for a clinical trial design, but it's less useful for the patient.

Instead of admitting to ourselves that we got it wrong, and that may be the person has some other problem than is addressable with SSRI, we label their depression, and therefore the patient, resistant .

We parlayed our reactive depression conceptualizations into something stubborn, that we need to bludgeon, that we find unsatisfying, difficult, and requiring more and more sadistic interventions and urges to get the person well, come hell or high water.

If you think I'm exaggerating, I would encourage you to review my prior articles on the metabolic consequences of antidepressant medicines and antipsychotic medication augmentation’s burdens. This is both in terms of obesity and in terms of movement disorders like tardive dyskinesia. These drugs can suck for patients. What happened to psychoneurotic disorder? Don't we have personality to contend with?

It turns out it's easier to do studies on treatment resistant depression, if you've got a treatment that actually works. Placebo response is major issue in clinical trials —the less placebo response in a condition, the smaller the study can be for a treatment that actually works. It's just easier to show a difference, which is the point of a clinical trial.

There's an incentive to run trials in treatment-resistant depression for study sponsors. The FDA decided “TRD” was a failure of two prior medication trials. If you do a study in the population of people who have not gotten any benefit from two prior medication trials, your study is more likely to be successful with less money out of your pocket. Thus, TRD becomes all the rage.

However, the same relentless logic means it becomes less economically viable to study people with depression or other psychiatric conditions for a monotherapy indication, in patients who have never been treated before. It's cheaper to run easy studies. So we only study new treatments— that may be better than existing treatments— in people who failed existing treatments. And then, the FDA approves it in that population, and now insurance won't cover it for anybody else, because it wasn't studied.

I have previously referred to this as the first-line treatment trap.

Treatment-resistant depression (TRD), and it's modern cousins, difficult-to-treat depression (DTD), and hard-to-treat depression (HTD)?

Oh, no! More defining of terms:

• TRD: Failure to respond adequately to ≥2 antidepressants of adequate dose and duration in the current depressive episode is a widely used threshold in pivotal trials (example: esketamine ).

• DTD: “depression that continues to cause significant burden despite usual treatment efforts,” with no uniform definition, which explicitly broadens the ability to market whatever it is past a regulatory threshold of greater than two antidepressant trials.

• HTD: similarly undefined from a regulatory standpoint, and is either synonymous with TRD or DTD depending on who's using it.

These are all hard-to-justify terminology. If we spent all of our time trying to use multi-word hyphenated phrases to get at our concepts-of-a-point, we're gonna be tongue tied.

Depression is often hard to treat, period. Some people have depression that responds well to whatever we choose first. Other people need something different than we chose first. Many people have contributors to their depression that are multifactorial, and even if they have the most purely biological depression you can imagine, it's still gonna create psychological and social chaos in their life if left unchecked.

However, for the next patient, you see, or for yourself?

Depression is ancient. It’s fearsome at times. Also, we can treat it safely and effectively. We have treatments that have been evaluated in more “refractory-to-SSRIs” depression, and it worked.

Some groups have tried to use a TRD treatment— TMS — in first episode depression, and shockingly , found out it still worked:

Methods: A prospective, double-blind, parallel-group trial was conducted involving 42 participants (21 with first-episode depressive disorder and 21 with recurrent depressive disorder)

Results: Both patient groups exhibited significant reductions in suicidal ideation following the composite TMS intervention. Secondary outcomes showed significant improvements in sleep quality, overall depressive symptoms, anhedonia, and cognitive function. Notably, a significant association was found between improvements in sleep quality and depressive symptoms in the first-episode group, suggesting differential underlying mechanisms compared to recurrent depression 3 .

This was notably a study of only 10 TMS treatments over two weeks , so it is low-dose compared to what we use currently, which is 36 stimulations as the standard course approved by the FDA.

Multi-hyphen depression (TRD, DTD, HTD) maybe a phenomenon of having chosen the wrong treatments as our first line. It's also true that there might be different depressions. This idea has been pervasive throughout the entire history of thinking about depression back to Hippocrates. We should, perhaps, spend time understanding what kind of differences might underline the suffering of the humans in front of us. Perhaps they are even in our own mirror. Also, we should remain skeptical that the “best guess treatment” is absolutely the best treatment for the patient. Health professionals should leave room for hope that something we hadn't considered might offer relief. This remains true when nothing we have done thus far has been helpful.

That having been said, the tension from The Ailment remains: sometimes doing too much can be harmful. Bearing witness, alone, is still medical care—and not without its own risks.

Next, is a post written as a ten year anniversary post on a blog that started well before substack, buy a person journaling about her experience with ECT for depression

A lot has happened in the ten years for the author:

Ten years and a day ago I was discharged from 3 months of inpatient ECT treatment.

At that time, I was moderately hopeful. I didn’t know that the outpatient treatment plan was going to fall apart almost immediately.

I didn’t know that I would become Very Sick again – and pretty soon after I went home.

I was resigned to likely having some good periods, followed by some more ECT (though likely not inpatient for 3 months!) – we anticipated a lifetime of this. But the durability of the ECT I had as well as any future ECT was contingent on good psychiatric treatment. Which I did not have access to. And so I got sicker and sicker.

As she noted in 2008, some months after her initial course of ECT:

…In hindsight, I’m not sure that I was fully well for more than a couple of months after ECT and it’s possible that the concurrent trauma that went alongside the whole experience cancelled it out in some ways. If anyone could have created wellness through sheer force of will, it would have been me. I wanted, needed, desperately needed to be well, or mostly well, or well enough, and I was, for the most part, well enough to overlook the deep damage sustained…but it was untenable and not lastingly consistent….

…My family was worried to the point where they flew in to keep an eye on me. I cried all the time. I thought about taking my beloved baby cat back to the ASPCA so that when I killed myself he would be cared for. My therapist was scared. Everyone was scared. My psychiatrist and therapist would eventually insist on a cold turkey termination of the MAOI that didn’t work anyway, which resulted in a debilitating migraine that lasted for more than two weeks and didn’t respond to regular rescue meds, IV meds, a nerve block, and eventually required an out of production med that a very enterprising pharmacist managed to round up from assorted locations around the country because they were so terrified because I was so hopeless and sad that I was really going to kill myself after 13 years of not and that a cold turkey withdrawal was the lesser of the evils.

However, her story didn’t end badly, as it might have…she kept trying new options as presented, and they helped, if in part thanks to an updated psychiatrist and and updated understanding and thus diagnosis:

He was the first to diagnose me with OCD – a disease infinitely more dangerous and deadly than depression, and he did so almost immediately – despite literal decades of multiple professionals diagnosing and treating me for depression.

Even The Frontier Psychiatrists’ editorially beloved modality—TMS—wasn’t durable enough:

As anyone who’s been on every medication in the world like I have knows, psychiatric medication really isn’t sufficiently effective 9 times out of 10. 99 out of 100. Even the research says this! But the same meds still get prescribed and adjuncted over and over and over as standard of care. It’s never ending.

“Brain medicine” is what it sounds like. Directly treating the brain. Neuromodulation. ECT is also brain medicine….but it requires anesthesia and a hospital, and is practically impractical for ongoing treatment. You have to wait until you are really unwell to access it, and then you have to have the luxury to only work 1-2 days a week throughout the duration of treatment.

TMS also directly treats the brain. For a long time, several years, accelerated TMS was a great option for me. I could knock out a day or two or five of TMS treatments and be fully well, entirely asymptomatic, at the end. My psychiatrists were incredible about making TMS available to me whenever. Evenings. Weekends. Etc.

So, she decided to take an ever more unusal step:

So last year, at the suggestion of my psychiatrists, I went through an extensive process to determine if I was a candidate for DBS, deep brain stimulation. I met with multiple specialists. Neuropsychiatrist. Neuropsychologists. A full neuropsych assessment. Neurosurgeon and his team.

And when it turned out that I apparently was a good candidate for DBS, I did a lot of thinking, and then we scheduled dates for the three surgeries that make up DBS.

It is sort of insane to say that I had neurosurgery because of convenience, but that’s pretty much what happened.

DBS, deep brain stimulation, is kind of like a pacemaker, but for your brain. It is directly stimulating my brain at all times. Just about the most brain medicine-y intervention that there ever could be.

You can read her blog for the rest, but I promise, it’s worth finishing.

Dan is the Chief Medical Officer at Definium Therapeutics . This used to be MindMed before the rebrand. He will be joining us at our 6th RAMHT event at the APA this year!

Definium is working on MM-120 as their lead candidate drug—but you probably know it better as LSD. Dan is a friend, but also a bit of “the reason I do much of the work I do personally.”

I own a few hundred shares of Definium, as a disclosure.

Mechanisms in Psychiatry

DK: We fall in love with mechanisms in psychiatry. We move from a monoamine hypothesis to a glutamate hypothesis and dopamine hypothesis, and currently circuitry is all the rage. But to think that the brain works on the level of centimeter-size circuits is probably an oversimplification. So, like every other model of how the brain works!

OM: Welcome to the Frontier Psychiatrists!

DK: I’m Dan Karlin, I’m the Chief Medical Officer of Definium, and I am a psychiatrist by training.

OM: Dan Karlin isn’t just any psychiatrist. He’s got some additional training.

DK: I’m also boarded in Addictions and Clinical Informatics and have been at Definium now for almost exactly five years, which is pretty wild. The time has flown by at an alarming rate. I didn’t come to it through any sort of a priori special interest or expertise in any drug class in particular — certainly not in psychedelics. I’d been watching from the sidelines, having come out of psychiatric drug development at Pfizer, where we were working on very different classes of medicines. I left Pfizer shortly after they left the entirety of brain health — they left neurology and psychiatry. We were able to do some really interesting digital measurement work in other therapeutic areas, but my passion to be there was dependent on working on diseases in psychiatry. I had been in digital health for a little while, and then, through a story that you know, Owen, better than anybody, came to the attention of what was then called MindMed through those digital health efforts.

OM: Dan’s being a little coy here. I may have introduced Dan to the founder of MindMed, and Dan’s experience in informatics and psychiatry led him to have a skillset that was a perfect fit. Ultimately, the goal of these drug development efforts is to demonstrate to the satisfaction of regulators, treaters like you and me, payers, and ultimately patients that we have a drug that has something meaningful to offer them. It needs to ultimately be proved both safe and effective.

OM: Both Dr. Karlin and I have found ourselves in roles as physicians which include interacting with regulators at the FDA to get new treatments over the line. For Dan, he feels that his training is crucial to how he approaches drug development.

The Psychology of Pharmacology

DK: The training that we get in psychiatry includes the same medical school everybody goes to — there’s a mandated degree of true medical training. Then we get quite a bit of training specifically on the medical aspects of psychiatry. We learn medical mimics of psychiatric illness, drugs, drug interactions, drug metabolism. All of that is incredibly useful in drug development and in thinking about how to demonstrate that a drug is safe and efficacious. Most of us would agree that the mind and the brain are entirely contained within at least the body.

We don’t think that there’s some part of the mind that exists outside of the body. Yet when we think about our practice, before psychedelics, we would often talk about psychological interventions and psychopharmacological interventions as though they felt quite separate — though I would argue there’s always a psychological intervention happening. There is not always a psychopharmacological intervention happening! But every psychopharmacological intervention includes a psychological intervention and, to my mind, includes a psychodynamic intervention. We’re always interacting with each other’s conscious brains and unconscious brains. We get to make a choice whether we acknowledge that, but whether we want to or not, we are certainly doing it. Any separation we chose to draw is a matter of convenience.

OM: We moved onto the topic of obtaining informed consent for an experience you can’t imagine. I asked Dan how he thinks about the role of medical ethics in the process of informed consent for something as potentially life-changing as lysergide.

DK: Our western orientation to medical ethics privileges autonomy over other ethical principles like doing good, not doing bad, or justice. Because we privilege autonomy so much, we do informed consent as a standard of practice. This is not without a trade-off.

There are strong arguments for different degrees and ways of doing informed consent, but in part, if you give someone a pill and tell them you’re very confident it’ll make them better, we know that it’s more likely to make them better. And if you give someone a pill and tell them all the things that it might do that harm them, those things are more likely to happen too.

OM: There’s an argument that the extent to which we inform people of risks actually makes our medicine less effective in the real world. We are trading off the odds of the medicine helping more than it hurts by telling people how it might hurt — which isn’t an argument to not do it, but it is something we should be aware of. And this speaks to that overall principle: no pill is given for any purpose that doesn’t also come with some degree of psychological interaction.

DK: The interesting thing — and the biggest change brought about by the recognition that there are these artificially divided two sides, the neuroscientific and the psychological — is that when we deal with psychedelics, we are dealing with drugs that have an acute psychological change associated with them. There are these very distinctive, transient changes in sensorium and perception and affect and cognition that come along with the drug. And while there are some who argue that those are unnecessary side effects that should be designed out, I’ve taken a different stance, in consideration of the evidence that’s available, which to me suggests that those changes — and maybe not all of the changes, maybe just the cognitive or just the affective or just certain aspects of the perceptual — are in fact a necessary part of the mechanism by which these drugs exert change. And so now we’re talking about a collapsing of the distinction between the psychological and the psychopharmacologic, and saying they might not be disentangleable — they might be completely interdependent in the case of these drugs. Which allows us to talk about these things in a way that is more informed by actual research evidence and experiments that show efficacy, but also doesn’t let us retreat into that convenient but erroneous distinction between the two.

OM: What is a psychedelic?

DK: The answer to that question has changed and evolved with societal use of the word. When we talk about what have now been termed classic psychedelics — but were once just psychedelics — those are probably best defined, and the definition becomes a little circular when you’re developing LSD, as medicines that have 5-HT2A agonism: a specific subtype of the serotonin receptor in the brain and elsewhere, that have a quote “LSD-like effect.” But what that ultimately means is that through their action on that receptor system, there are perceptual changes. These drugs have also been called hallucinogens, but they really aren’t hallucinogenic.

An interesting and somewhat unique aspect of them — and I don’t think we even really see this in organic illusions — what differentiates them from organic hallucination is that the quality of the experience is more one of disorganization of the system. The thing that seems to decrease in activity when these drugs are given in doses adequate to induce their characteristic effects is what’s called, on fMRI, the default mode network — really the sort of background, always-on activity of the brain. And what we’ve known for some time is that in the background, when you’re not actively trying to do something with your brain, what it’s doing is mostly filtering the world into some sort of sense of sensorium — which has visual components, tactile components, all the sensory stuff coming up from your body, cognitive components, emotional components — and what your brain is mostly doing is trying to turn that into some sort of order.

OM: I’ll give you a metaphor — you tell me if I’m getting it right. When you’re making a film, you have a director of photography who controls what’s captured, and a director who tells people what to do. Then you have an editor at the end of the process who cuts up what the photography guy captured, cuts up what the audio foley guy captured — they’re putting things together and saying, “Cut this whole scene, it’s not going to make any sense.” So the default mode network’s job, question mark, is editing our experience such that we can make sense of it. And it has to happen so fast there’s no latency in our experience, despite the fact that there definitely is latency. This makes evolutionary sense — in order to survive, we need to be able to process visual, auditory, and tactile stimuli fast enough to react in time to, say, catch a ball thrown toward us.

DK: And this is exactly the sort of thing we have to contend with when trying to create immersive environments — they have to not fall out of sync with the processing happening in the background.

OM: I want to hone in on that. When you say disorganization — in the organic sense, an illusion is a distortion of what we’re seeing. There’s something in the room; it’s a TV, but instead of being rectangular it’s oblong. A hallucination would be seeing something that isn’t there at all — a guy standing there who isn’t really there.

DK: Think about what the filter is doing — and this is what makes drawing hard for a lot of people. The TV is only visually rectangular from one really specific angle, directly in front of it, and even then if you were to draw what you’re actually seeing, it won’t come out looking quite right. So even if you’re looking at the TV from a different angle, your filtered experience of it is “rectangle,” because it likely is rectangular. The hallucination example is interesting because there’s a little distinction here, and this is another thing that makes psychedelics not hallucinogenic in the traditional meaning. Hallucinations are believed to be real by the person experiencing them — they can’t say, “That’s not something actually in the room.” Pseudo-hallucinations are susceptible to reality testing. I see someone in the corner; I know there’s not someone in the corner, but I still see them — versus: there is someone in the corner.

In an auditory hallucination in schizophrenia, I hear the CIA talking to me. In an auditory experience from a psychedelic compound, it feels like I’m hearing voices, but you retain the ability to know that’s not what’s really happening. You say, “Wow, that was weird, man,” as opposed to, “That’s just what’s happening.”

The most interesting one — which LSD can prominently produce — is synesthesia, where I hear the color blue. I know blue is not an auditory thing, but I tell you I’m hearing it. I know this is an unusual drug-induced experience.

OM: I often talk about the synesthesia of distress that is OCD — things that wouldn’t normally be distressing, like objects being asymmetrical, can be experienced as just as painful as burning your hand. So this is a routine experience in humans: one thing substitutes for another. Similarly, things can sound blue in the right context or set of circumstances.

DK: OCD represents a different kind of failure — an inability to tune out the question, “Is my front door locked?” Everybody has that thought sometimes, but for someone without OCD it’s not distressing, not all-consuming. A failure to filter appropriately, I think, falls into a lot of distressing experiences that ultimately end up as components of various psychiatric disorders.

OM: So what I’m hearing is: in classical psychedelics like LSD, we’re getting a hint at some mechanism of something, because what they do to the sensory experience during acute administration is produce disintegrated sensory change. They’re taking the default mode network — which is responsible for cropping, filtering, and editing in very rapid sequence — and taking it in some way offline, such that our sensory experience is different by virtue of a lack of editing. And we’re overwhelmingly aware, correct me if I’m wrong, that that weirdness of disintegrated sensory experience is not real.

DK: Yes, absolutely. That is the characteristic of it — the world experienced without, as we’ve been talking about, the perceptual filters. But I think there’s a more important mechanism of filtering that we learn through development. And this actually coincides really nicely with development from intrauterine life — because sound processing and things probably start there — all the way through to sometime in adulthood. Arguably development in some form goes on all the way till death. But these key parts that lead us to be who we are, happening through adolescence and early on, include this sort of visual, auditory, tactile filtering: figuring out where your body ends and the world begins, figuring out how to organize the world into meaningful visual units. We talk about the infant recognizing the primary caregiver’s face first, the sound of the primary caregiver’s voice — that’s an organization of those perceptual systems.

In part II we discussed how Classical psychedelics like LSD may work by suppressing the default mode network — the background system responsible for filtering and organizing sensory information into coherent experience. They produce "pseudo-hallucinations" and perceptual distortions that the user knows aren't real, including phenomena like synesthesia. This disorganization of perception offers a window into how the brain normally edits raw sensory input into a structured, meaningful experience of the world.

DK: around age one, when kids first say their first words — there’s language. Not only do we have to turn the perceptual world into order, we as people, possibly uniquely, then turn the world into language. We experience the world through language, we experience ourselves through language, we experience others through language. Arguably something like the self or the ego is just a story we tell ourselves about ourselves. And once we have words — our human token — we can do much more complex and perhaps computationally efficient editing. We can pop out a word in the middle of a conversation. Words are a great way to edit our experience.

OM: You’ll notice this whenever you talk to somebody who doesn’t speak the same language. German has words we don’t have — there’s no English word for Schadenfreude , taking pleasure in the misfortune of others, so we borrow it because it’s a useful concept. In Hebrew they’ve got words and sounds we don’t have in English, and chutzpah among my Israeli colleagues and chutzpah among my New York friends is a different chutzpah . These are concepts that frame our understanding. Am I getting it right that language, as I’m presenting it, is in keeping with what you’re talking about?

DK: The neat thing is that while individual languages do have unique characteristics, and are deeply informed by and in turn inform the cultures in which they’re used, the quality of language-having seems universal. By virtue of having language — which far predates any of the languages we have today — having language at all is necessary to have almost everything we have today as people. Language-having is what makes us storytelling creatures. The amazing quality of language is that it both allows us to put things into one another’s heads and is how we contain things in our own heads. The thing that contains neuroses is the language structures. And by virtue of having those, we can have specific maladaptive narratives about our lives — narratives about our past that were useful then but aren’t useful now, stories that hold us together now but ultimately aren’t conducive to moving forward in life.

What we so often encounter in the psychotherapeutic process is: yes, this is distressing what you describe, and we can talk about what this distress does that hurts you — but in many cases, this distress is protecting you somehow. The question is often more importantly phrased: what does it do for you to have this? Because if we can figure out what it does for you, it’s easier to work through.

COVID, the Bronx, and Bearing Witness

OM: And so you’re a measurement guy. You clearly have a real background in psychotherapy, psychodynamics, the traditions that make psychiatry a unique subspecialty in medicine. And your friend calls you up in the middle of COVID — and you’d spent some time using your skills as a physician. You can tell me if this is okay to say, but you went to the ICU and did some work as a regular old doctor, keeping people alive in New York City, which at the time was ground zero for the pandemic.

DK: God, I don’t remember exactly what dates, but it must have been April. It was in the Bronx. I had been associated with the Montefiore system for a little teaching — I was credentialed, I covered a service every once in a while or an inpatient service if someone called out last minute, because I lived in the neighborhood and was running the digital health company. So if they needed me, I was happy to duck in and do what I could to help. Plus, I love the work, and the thing that working in industry takes away is the opportunity to see patients as much. So I really appreciated that department and the people who worked in it.

When things were clearly taking a turn — there was a day that New York sort of went from playfully arm-bumping instead of handshaking to a degree of panic — a general call was put out, including in the Department of Psychiatry: did anyone want to be helpful in the event they were needed? My background: I grew up on ambulances from when I was sixteen. When I was at Columbia, I ran their ambulance service on the Upper West Side of Manhattan, where I’m originally from. When 9/11 happened, because of my role there, even though I wasn’t in the city at the time — I was in Western Massachusetts — I was able to get to the city pretty quickly, and spent three days at the World Trade Center scene helping out.

With this pretty clearly impending disaster, particularly up in the Bronx, it was obvious that participating to the extent I was able would be helpful. My EMS training and longstanding interest in disaster medicine and overwhelmed systems made me think I could be useful. So I got a call one Saturday that a team was running out of docs, and I went into a hospital up there and found a terribly overwhelmed system. The team I joined was taking care of twelve patients, four of whom were ventilated, several more on aggressive respiratory interventions but not yet intubated. There was one pulse ox for the four ventilated patients. If you wanted a blood gas — which, for folks not in medicine, is how you monitor how well you’re doing breathing for someone — you could draw one and walk it down to the lab yourself. That was the level of being overwhelmed.

On the second day there, we ended up doing fourteen resuscitations over twelve hours — unexpected deaths. People who were sort of expected to die were intubated because that decision had been made, but these were people dying unexpectedly. It was as badly overwhelmed as I’ve seen any system.

OM: Did any of them survive those resuscitations?

DK: Not to leave the hospital. You get a pulse back and keep moving. But it’s not like on TV. CPR doesn’t always work — in fact, most of the time it doesn’t. Especially when you’re dealing with an overwhelmed system. And so early in the experience of the pandemic, in many cases we weren’t even sure why we were seeing what we were seeing. There was the respiratory pathology, obviously, but there was also a lot of clotting going on that we just didn’t know was a feature of serious COVID — people getting pulmonary embolisms even after they seemed in the clear. And again, it was early. Everyone was doing their absolute best.

I spent two days there and left glad that I was able to help, but completely overwhelmed on behalf of the residents and staff physicians who had to keep going back. I got to leave because someone else got better enough to come in. I was glad I could do what little I could and, in many cases, just witness the heroic work that the folks there were doing.

One thing I could do — and I felt fortunate to be able to do it — was make calls to families whose loved ones were in these units and dying, and try to talk through that with them in a way that an incredibly busy internal medicine resident neither had the time nor the training to do. Trying to bring, I think like so much of what we do, some relief to a situation that is intrinsically difficult if not impossible. And again, this comes right back to where we were: trying to help people form a story around their experience that will allow them to move forward — with as much self-realization, self-actualization, sense of efficacy, sense of purpose as possible. So much of even that situation, once you’re done dealing with the acute physiological needs of a person, is about trying to help them tell themselves a story about the situation they’re in.
