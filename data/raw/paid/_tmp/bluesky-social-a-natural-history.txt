I’m going to explain AT protocol as a lay person. It’s the federated technology behind bluesky social, and explain why moderation is different over there. This is profoundly different for this platform compared to centralized social media—with both risk and benefits. For non-technologists, a data repository is like a Blockchain. A ledger, like a notebook, tracks every transaction—if it's a cryptocurrency.

This will be easier to explain by starting with cryptocurrency. With Bitcoin, for example, every Bitcoin transaction that ever happened in the entire history of Bitcoin is observable to everybody who would like to look. A bitcoin can travel from one Bitcoin wallet to another Bitcoin wallet, and that transaction becomes a permanent part of the ledger, which is the expanding data set of Bitcoin transactions that make up the blockchain.

Practically, although the wallets don't have your name on it, it is possible to work out who has what Wallet​ —at least in theory. This decentralization makes it impossible to control Bitcoin, or nearly impossible, but it also makes it impossible for things to be private.

That same architecture tracks things other than transactions with Bitcoin. Ethereum, the next cryptocurrency to gain popularity, had quite a bit more functionality than Bitcoin—more data, computer programs, and more could live on that blockchain. “Smart contracts” allowed for more complex logic around what takes place on the Etherium.

But if you don't care about a blockchain supporting a debatably scammy currency and you had other uses in mind? For example, if you wanted to track everything that happened over time on a social media site? And then all of that data could be available for subsequent analysis, and similar to how anybody can write an application that runs on Ethereum Blockchain, using a contract, developers to build tools social media built an open source protocol that works quite a bit like a blockchain?

This is what AT protocol does:

Identity

Users are identified by domain names on the AT Protocol. These domains map to cryptographic URLs which secure the user's account and its data.

Data repositories

User data is exchanged in signed data repositories. These repositories are collections of records which include posts, comments, likes, follows, media blobs…

Bluesky Social isn't one social media application, although it started as one. It allows anyone to build their own native version of BlueSky and interact with other open-source protocols. Facebook built Threads on a similar, but not identical, system. Mastodon is also built on a different federated architecture.

Just like cryptocurrency can have an unexpected and problematic phenomenon—there's no one to call to reinstate your wallet if you lose your key! Other problems/features include the following: No centralized authority can stop the flow of cryptocurrency, just as they can stop the flow of US dollars. Protocolized social media has similar features.

For those who are interested in the topics of defamation or cyberstalking, this kind of protocol comes with fascinating risks and benefits. For example, on Bluesky, if you block somebody, that's an action that's logged. Every action is logged, public, and available to developers. Every post can live forever—you could delete it, but somebody already saved it to some giant server before you could do that. Technologically enabled stalkers already see that you block them and create a new account to harass you. Powerful technology! The flip side is that their harassment is similarly permanent. So let's say you were interested in harassing somebody and maybe even posting a thing that was false, defamatory, and indisputably and enforceably so. Well, that lives forever, also. Unlike Facebook or X, there isn't a centralized authority you have to subpoena to get those records. Everything everybody does is publicly available. Forever.

If you post something on Bluesky, and someone might want to use it against you in the future, they can. There is a relentless level of accountability built into this design and a non-trivial amount of vulnerability as well.

One doesn't need to work hard to imagine the world in which she might post something that you think better of, and delete, and you're done. Nobody saw it. On Bluesky, opposite. Again, all of this is public.

There are some other features of the design of the platform that mitigate against harassment. On Twitter of old, you can do the quote-post to dunk on someone.

On Bluesky, you can detach your post from their quote post. To the very curious, the record of both of these actions will be indelibly recorded forever. Still, the person who just tried to belittle you by making fun of your post a quote will get the rug pulled out from under them when it’s just their snarky comment and “this has been deleted.”

Twitter used to have a thriving medical community trading papers, education, and memes back and forth. This has been reborn on Bluesky as #medsky, #academicsky, and who knows what else. These can all live on private servers or hybrid servers in the future.

Bluesky disambiguates speech—which should be very free—and reach—who is forced to put up with what you have to say. Quote:

Speech, reach, and moderation

Atproto's model is that speech and reach should be two separate layers, built to work with each other. The “speech” layer should remain permissive, distributing authority and designed to ensure everyone has a voice. The “reach” layer lives on top, built for flexibility and designed to scale.

The base layer of atproto (personal data repositories and federated networking) creates a common space for speech where everyone is free to participate, analogous to the Web where anyone can put up a website. The indexing services then enable reach by aggregating content from the network, analogous to a search engine.

Bluesky lets speech be endlessly free—and it also empowers people to not put up with nitwits and trolls who aren’t there to contribute to your conversation as you moderate it. On Twitter, it's a big deal to the platform somebody, but on Bluesky, everyone is empowered to platform or deplatform everybody else from their life.

It's not centralized authority making a decision; it's individual people making the decision for themselves and maybe sharing those recommendations with their community so that people of mind can keep their version of the trolls out.

It's a remarkably different model compared to the centralized systems controlled by the likes of Zuckerberg and Musk. It's both different in terms of its experience—at least thus far, for me, it hasn't been polluted by bots, scammers, endless porn, or Andrew Tate.

It also has profound implications for what happens when individuals decide to use that platform to commit defamation. It's the most “built for legal discovery” social media platform that's ever existed, and your author might be the first person to demonstrate that in court. But judging by how humanity likes to act, I will not be the last.
